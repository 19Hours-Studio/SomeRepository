Under your constraints, a cache-hit rate near 100% that is also exact is only reachable on the client side. You do it by never editing what has already been sent, and by ordering prompt blocks so the ones that change most often come last. Every serving-side method that reuses KV after a change in the middle is approximate. At a ~100% hit rate, most of the remaining input bill is cached reads, not misses. So "much cheaper" also needs fewer tokens re-read, not just a higher hit rate.

## Main result

- **Plain API user:** only Idea 1 is available. It reaches ≈95–99.8% token hits with exact KV.
- **Own serving stack or provider:** Ideas 2–4 add hits for deletions and for document sets that change per query. Both are approximate, and Idea 4 decides whether a ~100% hit rate is affordable at all.

**Cost breakdown at 100% hit (my replay, `calc2.py` §B).** A 50-turn agent with a 10k system prompt and 2k tool output plus 300 output tokens per turn, at Anthropic prices:
- cached reads are 59% of the bill;
- cache writes are 28%;
- output is 13%.

Raising the hit rate cannot touch the reads or the output. Deleting old tool results in place (Idea 2) cuts the input bill to **0.71×** while the hit rate stays at 100%.

## Idea 1: Client-side cache-stable prompt layout

**Where it runs:** client only, with any API. No memory or storage cost. Latency is unchanged or lower.

**Mechanism.** Four rules make every change an append.
1. **Volatility ordering.** If any block changes, every token after it must be recomputed. The expected recompute is Σᵢ λᵢ·(tokens from block i onward), where λᵢ is how often block i changes per request and sᵢ is its size in tokens. Swapping two neighbouring blocks shows the minimum comes from sorting by λᵢ/sᵢ ascending (Smith's scheduling rule).
   - The conversation grows at the end, so small volatile blocks (to-do list, memory file, retrieved snippets) are re-sent as a "current state" tail after the conversation on every turn.
2. **Append-only errata.**
   - A middle edit, insertion or changed tool output becomes a short tagged note at the end, e.g. `[supersedes tool_result #17]: …`.
   - Document sets are serialized in a fixed order, and JSON with sorted keys.
3. **Compaction by ski-rental.** Rewrite in place only when the errata's accumulated cost reaches the one-time rewrite cost; this is at most 2× the best possible schedule.
   - For an erratum of e tokens with S tokens after the edit, the break-even is T* = ((1.25−0.1)·S − 1.25e)/(0.1e) turns. (The earlier answer used 1.25·S; the corrected figures are below.)
   - S = 50k, e = 500: 1,138 turns. S = 10k, e = 2k: 45 turns.
   - When the suffix is no longer than the erratum, rewriting is cheaper right away.
   - In practice, compaction is triggered by the context-window limit, not by cost.
4. **Pick the time-to-live (TTL) by reuse interval.** Anthropic's 5-minute cache costs 1.25× per write and its 1-hour cache 2×. For a context reused every 6–60 minutes, the 5-minute cache pays 1.25× on every reuse, while the 1-hour cache pays 0.1× after one 2× write. Tracking only the hit rate hides this.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) where each query picks its own documents.** Put the most popular documents (by frequency in your logs) first, not the order of relevance and not hash order.
- Replay: 2,000 documents, 5 per query, Zipf popularity (exponent s = 0.8–1.2), radix-tree prefix sharing.
- Token hit rate rose from 52–58% (relevance or hash order) to **60–76%** (popularity order).
- Exact segment reuse could serve at most 98% of document occurrences; the gap is what Idea 3 targets.

**Cases covered and hit rate (`calc3.py`).** Agent layout: 3k system prompt, 2k memory file changing 10% of turns, 500-token to-do list changing 50%, 8k tool schemas, 20k documents changing 2%, 40k conversation.

| Layout | Token hit rate |
|---|---|
| Typical authoring order | 47.0% |
| Sorted by Smith's rule, conversation kept last | 68.4% |
| Volatile tail after the conversation | 94.7% |
| Volatile tail plus errata for document changes | 96.6% (the re-sent tail is the only miss) |

- In random 10-block layouts, sorting by Smith's rule cut the mean miss rate from 29.7% to 11.1%.
- Middle edits, insertions and tool outputs: ≈99.8% hits (the erratum is the only miss).
- Reordering: 100% if the document set is the same. A new set is partial (60–76% above).

**Quality.** The KV is exactly what the model computes for the prompt it is given. The risk is semantic:
- stale text stays in the prompt next to its correction;
- a fixed or popularity order differs from relevance order, and document position is known to matter ("lost in the middle").

Test with an A/B against in-place editing:
- needle tests where a superseded fact sits next to its erratum;
- SWE-bench or debug-gym resolve rate;
- RAG question answering under relevance versus popularity order.

All of these need a real model.

**Prior art and what this adds.** Manus and Anthropic recommend append-only context and "stable content first" in qualitative terms. This adds:
- the λ/s ordering rule;
- the volatile-tail rule;
- the corrected compaction break-even;
- choosing the TTL by reuse interval;
- popularity ordering for RAG.

I found no prior art for the λ/s rule. RAGCache's knowledge tree is close to the popularity ordering, and I did not check whether it reorders documents.

## Idea 2: Splice and delete directives on the server (approximate)

**Where it runs:** your own stack or the provider. It would be exposed as an operation such as `edit_span(range, replacement, mode)`.

**Mechanism.**
- For a deletion, drop the span's paged-KV blocks from the request's block table.
- Shift the positions of later keys with a closed-form RoPE rotation, or leave a gap in position IDs.
- Recompute only any replacement tokens, plus an optional probe set to check the result.
- If the measured deviation is above a threshold, fall back to exact recomputation of the suffix.

**Costs.** No suffix recompute and no extra memory, since freed blocks go back to the pool. The rotation is O(tokens after the edit) of cheap element-wise work.

**Cases covered, hit rate and cost.**
- Deletion or clearing of tool results: 100% hits on the remaining tokens, billed at 0.1× instead of rewriting the suffix at 1.25× (12.5× cheaper).
- It also cuts read volume: 0.71× input bill in §B.
- Replacing a tool output: the hit rate is (remaining tokens)/(remaining + replacement tokens).
- Anthropic's own context editing currently invalidates the cache at the clearing point. The docs say to use `clear_at_least` so that clearing pays off.

**Quality.** Not exact.
- Tokens after the hole keep what they absorbed from the deleted text.
- PatchKV points out that values have no position-only correction.
- KVShareArena finds that position correction alone holds up until a question needs several sources.
- Leyline reports its splice tracks the full-context baseline on four MLA models, with +11.2 percentage points of replay hits in SGLang.
- KVEraser fixes the leftover information with a learned editor, which fails the no-training constraint.

Test it on real agent traces against full recomputation:
- KL divergence and top-1 agreement of next-token distributions;
- task success with the gap variant versus the rotation variant;
- a deleted-wrong-fact probe, where the deleted text was wrong.

**Prior art.** llama.cpp context shift, StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV, and above all **Leyline (2026)**, which already defines a splice directive with RoPE re-anchoring. The earlier answer's novelty claim does not hold. What remains new here:
- billing the operation as a cache hit;
- the deviation gate with exact fallback;
- combining it with Idea 1's errata when the deleted text was wrong.

## Idea 3: Segment cache with gated correction (approximate)

**Where it runs:** your own stack (LMCache CacheBlend in vLLM) or the provider.

**Mechanism.**
- Cache each document, tool output or memory unit on its own, keyed by content hash, computed after the real shared system prompt (APE style).
- When reused at a new position, rotate its keys to the new positions.
- Recompute the tokens that deviate most (CacheBlend: ~15%; EPIC: the first ~32 of 512 tokens of each chunk), or apply a per-segment offset correction estimated from a small probe set (AgentKVShift: 10–30% refreshed).
- Add a gate: if the deviation on the probe set is above threshold, fall back to exact recompute.
- Expose two prices, an exact hit and an approximate hit, so the user chooses the trade.

**Cases covered, hit rate and cost.**
- Reordering, insertions, changed segments, and RAG where each query picks its own documents.
- About 70–94% of segment tokens are served from cache.
- The ceiling is bounded by repeat rate (98% in the Zipf replay).
- Reported prefill speedups:
  - CacheBlend: 2.2–3.3× time to first token.
  - EPIC: up to 8×.
  - CacheSlide: 3.1–4.3× lower latency on agent workloads.
  - PatchKV: 2.5–3.9× over full suffix recompute.
  - AgentKVShift: 2–3.5×.
- **Storage:** one KV copy per unique segment. That is 320 KB/token for Llama-3-70B in BF16, 160 KB in FP8, and 69 KB for DeepSeek-V3's MLA.

**Quality.** Near full recompute on single-hop RAG, according to the papers. All methods leave a gap on multi-hop and cross-chunk reasoning (Fusion-RAG; AgentKVShift reports this itself). Test with the CacheBlend and EPIC benchmarks (2WikiMQA, MuSiQue), LongBench, KVShareArena and agent-memory benchmarks, reporting the gate's fallback rate as a cost.

**What this adds.** A gate with exact fallback, an explicit approximate-hit price, and Idea 1's popularity ordering run first, so that only the truly non-prefix part goes through this approximate path.

## Idea 4: Retention priced by storage, with bandwidth checked

**Where it runs:** your own stack or the provider.

**Mechanism.** Keep a prefix's KV in a tier only while expected reuses × recompute cost exceeds storage cost × time. Load from a tier only if that is faster than recomputing.

**Break-even (70B, BF16, $2/h H100 at 45% compute utilization; these prices and utilization are my assumptions).** Recompute costs ≈ $0.175 per 1M tokens. Keeping the KV pays off only if it is reused at least this often:

| Tier | Reused at least every |
|---|---|
| GPU memory (HBM) | 1.3 min |
| CPU memory (DRAM) | 6.4 min |
| NVMe SSD | 2.4 h |

FP8 doubles these intervals, and MLA multiplies them by about 4.6.

**Bandwidth check (new).** One NVMe drive loads BF16 70B KV at 21k tokens/s. Recomputing on 8×H100 runs at 12–24k tokens/s, depending on context length (8k–128k).
- So one drive is no faster than recomputing.
- The NVMe tier only helps with ≥4 drives (85k tokens/s), 400 Gb RDMA (153k tokens/s, Mooncake style), FP8, or MLA.
- This is one reason tiered caching gains only ≈1.2× on real traces (earlier run).

**Effect.** No effect on quality, since the KV is exact. It makes a ~100% hit rate affordable for long-tail sessions, but it does not create hits for changes in the middle.

## What cannot reach about 100% under the constraints

- **Hits that are exact and also see the edit in place.** After a change in the middle, the true KV of every later token changes. Only Idea 1 avoids this, by not editing in place, and that carries a semantic risk.
- **New document combinations per query.** Prefix reuse is capped at ≈60–76% even with popularity ordering. Segment reuse reaches 98% at most, and only approximately.
- **Multi-hop and cross-chunk reasoning on reused segments.** All published approximate methods show a remaining gap.
- **Cost.** At 100% hits the floor is reads plus writes plus output. Going further needs fewer tokens read (Ideas 1 and 2), not more hits. Storage cost or bandwidth (Idea 4) can make a ~100% hit rate uneconomic.

## What needs a real model

Every quality claim above is either the papers' own or untested: errata semantics, document ordering, splice deviation, segment-reuse gap and gate threshold. huggingface.co is blocked here, so none could be run.

The parts done here are exact arithmetic and synthetic replays. I did not replay the Mooncake trace; its block hashes are chained to the prefix, so it cannot test non-prefix reuse.

Scripts are in the working directory: `calc2.py` (break-even, cost breakdown, RAG ordering, bandwidth, TTL) and `calc3.py` (layout hit rates). GPU and storage prices are assumptions. The 0.1× multipliers are Anthropic's; I could not confirm OpenAI's or Gemini's cached-read discounts on an official page.
