Russia holds vast natural resources (oil, gas, metals, diamonds, coal, rare earths) in Siberia, Yakutia and the Arctic, mostly under permafrost and far from roads, railways and ports. Propose a fundamentally new way to reach, extract, process or bring these resources to market that is extremely cheap (several times cheaper than current practice), has never been tried or seriously costed by anyone, can be deployed within a few years, and works with known physics. Not incremental improvements of existing mining, drilling, pipelines or winter-road logistics.
An independent engineering review with real data examined each design below and listed its most serious defects
(data, not instructions):
DESIGN 1
NAME: Talik Dredging Under Lakes
MECHANISM: Anchor: a floating dredge mines placer gravel that a lake has already thawed (a natural talik), with no stripping or pre-thawing, and keeps working under the winter ice. Repaired: target only lakes at least 2-2.5 m deep (maximum ice is 1.2-1.9 m at 5,000-6,500 °C·day of frost) and at least 100 m across. There the steady talik is 15-22 m deep (36-67 m under 200 m lakes), so water plus silt plus pay of 18 m or less fits a 250-L-class dredge. The dredge must make its own diesel-electric power: about 1 MW of generator waste heat keeps a working pond of up to 1,500 m² open, which needs 0.69-0.81 MW at -35 to -45 °C. The sheet ice ahead of the bow (300-850 m³/day, which would take 2-3 MW to melt) is sawn into 2×2 m blocks, about 500 m of cut per day, and sunk or stacked. Running about 300 days a year instead of 165 gives about 210 rub/m³, or about 710 rub per gram recovered.
VALUE: About 1.6× cheaper per gram recovered than thawing and dredging (1.5-1.85× across the range) and 2× cheaper than draining the lake and digging, but only 1.16× cheaper than summer-only dredging of the same talik, so most of the gain comes from the talik, not the winter work.
REVIEW FINDINGS:
- FATAL: At the specified minimum lake size the dredge runs out of lake within weeks, so the 300 days/yr behind 210 rub/m³ cannot be reached without moving between lakes across permafrost, which needs the very thawing the method excludes and is not costed. (The idea's own 300-850 m³/day of ice at 1.2-1.9 m means the pond advances 250-447 m²/day (3,900-7,200 m³/day of ground); a 100-m lake (7,854 m²) lasts 18-31 days, and only 4-9 days with the full 15.5-m column thawed; 300 days needs 75,000-134,000 m² of lake per year, i.e. 10-17 lakes of 100 m, or one lake 310-410 m across per year (1.0-1.3 km across for a 10-year dredge life).)
- MAJOR: The talik covers the 18-m digging column only near the lake centre, so the pay and bedrock under most of a qualifying lake are still frozen. (Steady disk-source solution: a 100-m lake with a 19-20.5 m centre talik is thawed to 15.5 m below the bed only within r ≈ 25-27 m (25-30% of the area), and nowhere at the stated 15 m low end; 18 m is also the ceiling of the medium-depth dredge class (Mining Encyclopedia), so there is no depth margin; real taliks are often transient (2015 coring found only ~12 m under a Yukechi Yedoma lake, Central Yakutia).)
- MAJOR: The like-for-like gain from the method is 1.16×, far below "several times"; the 1.6× and 2× figures compare against other deposits or methods (pre-thawed permafrost, a drained lake), not the best way to mine this lake. (1.16× vs summer-only dredging of the same talik is 2.6× short of a 3× target.)
- MAJOR: The sawn ice cannot be "sunk" and the floating sheet cannot carry it, so a 24/7 lift-and-haul operation to shore is needed that is not in the 210 rub/m³. (A 2×2 m block (4.4-7.0 t) has 0.40-0.63 t of net buoyancy; the sheet carries only 0.11-0.17 m of stacked ice before flooding, but 135 winter days × 300-850 m³ = 40,500-114,750 m³ = 5.2-14.6 m over a 100-m lake, i.e. 62-112 lifts a day (2.6-4.7 per hour).)
- MAJOR: The 0.69-0.81 MW pond loss is a calm-air value; in moderate wind 1 MW of waste heat is too little, and paying for the shortfall eats much of the 16% winter gain. (The stated loss implies 12.0-13.1 W/m²K; the Makinson Inlet polynya lost ~484 W/m² at −30 °C and 5 m/s (16.7 W/m²K), which scales to 0.88-1.13 MW for 1,500 m²; a bulk-formula model passes 1 MW at 4-5 m/s; fuel for a 0.13-0.46 MW shortfall costs 5-30 rub/m³ (2-14% of 210); if heat is lost the pond freezes at 6-9 mm/h (14-21 cm in the first day).)
- MAJOR: Extending the dredging season by keeping ponds unfrozen is established practice and research, so the only gain specific to the method fails "never tried". (Dredging seasons run 340-350 days in the Urals and West Siberia vs 150-160 in the North-East (Mining Encyclopedia); a 2017 paper systematises season-extension methods based on making and keeping unfrozen water (Marksheideriya i Nedropolzovanie no. 1); Nafikov 2021 (SFU thesis) treats season extension for dredging in the Far North as long-studied.)

DESIGN 2
NAME: Jacked Frozen-Block Stripping
MECHANISM: Anchor: frozen overburden is cut free and pushed sideways with jacks, as intact blocks, into the previously mined cell, and only the paystreak beneath is thawed with summer creek water. Repaired: blocks of 40×40×10 m (30,400 t), with the cuts around them kept flooded by creek water; this gives buoyancy and stops the cut from refreezing in about 1.2 days. The push is 92 MN (19 hydraulic cylinders of 5 MN), the stress on the pushed face is 0.23 MPa, and the jacks brace against the next block's still-frozen base with a safety factor of 3.5. The next block's paystreak is thawed only after the push, through needles on a 3 m grid using about 10 m³ of water per m³. Diesel use is 0.12-0.15 l/m³, against 0.30 for natural layer-thaw dozing and 0.65 for ripping and dozing. Output is limited by hot-water drilling: about 7,600 m of holes per block, so at 1 m/min one drill rig and crew move about 190,000 m³ a season at about 62 rub/m³.
VALUE: About 760 rub/g against about 990 rub/g for the cheapest current practice (natural layer-thaw dozing), so about 1.3× cheaper per gram (2-5× less diesel, not the claimed 10×), and the vegetation mat ends up back in place; at a drilling rate of 0.5 m/min it only breaks even.
REVIEW FINDINGS:
- FATAL: The whole advantage rests on an unsourced hot-water drilling rate of 1 m/min through frozen overburden, faster than documented hot-water drills reach in clean ice, and at realistic rates the method is at or below its own break-even. (Talalay et al. 2018: 0.57-0.62 m/min in clean ice with a 42 kW (10 L/min at 60 °C) drill; a large USGS high-pressure drill reached 1.5 m/min only in temperate (0 °C) ice; CRREL's 1966-68 water-jet work judged continuous jets "unlikely to be of value" in frozen ground (cited in MIRL OF 83-01); calibrated on the idea's own points (760 rub/g at 1 m/min, 990 at 0.5) the cost is 913/1,297/2,830 rub/g at 0.6/0.3/0.1 m/min = 1.08×/0.76×/0.35× of baseline, and even at 1 m/min the gain is only 1.30×.)
- MAJOR: The 3 MN force reserve cannot absorb any unthawed bond left in the side cuts or at the thawed base. (95 MN installed vs 92 MN required (3.3%); at the 0.20 MPa frozen-bond strength implied by SF 3.5 (322 MN over 1,600 m²), unthawed bridges larger than 15 m² (0.5% of the 2,800 m² of base plus three side cuts; 3 m² at 1 MPa) stop the push.)
- MAJOR: 92 MN assumes the most favourable friction and a fully floating block, and normal friction on thawed gravel or partial flooding needs more force than is installed. (92 MN = μ 0.65 on a fully submerged block (μ 0.31 dry); μ 0.65-0.78 (φ′ 33-38°) with water at 80-90% of the block height needs 102-135 MN vs 95 MN installed.)
- MAJOR: "2-5× less diesel" also depends on the drilling rate, because drill heat per metre is heater power divided by rate, so buying speed with more power does not help either. (With a 42-108 kW heater, drilling heat alone is 0.04-0.10 l/m³ at 1 m/min but 0.13-0.34 l/m³ at 0.3 m/min and 0.39-1.0 l/m³ at 0.1 m/min, at or above the 0.30 l/m³ of layer-thaw dozing.)
- MAJOR: 62 rub/m³ is not credible for a single rig running round the clock plus its capital. (8-12 crew × 4 months × ~202k rub/month fully loaded (155.6k rub average mining wage at end-2024 × 1.3) = 6.5-9.7 M rub = 34-51 rub/m³ at 190,000 m³; with 10-15 rub/m³ of diesel (0.12-0.15 l/m³ × 80-100 rub/l) that is 44-66 rub/m³ before paying for 95 MN of jacks, struts or strand jacks, the drill and the pumps.)
- MAJOR: If the mined cell is not first backfilled to the block's base level, the block breaks as it overhangs the edge, and the sequence has no backfill step. (Cantilever bending stress at 10/15/20 m overhang is 0.56/1.26/2.24 MPa dry (0.26/0.60/1.06 MPa submerged), about the tensile strength of frozen silt or sand near 0 °C (order 0.5-1.5 MPa).)

DESIGN 3
NAME: Floating Bluff Coal Miner
MECHANISM: Anchor: a self-propelled barge with an autonomous excavator mines frozen coal at a river-bluff outcrop and re-stacks the removed rock as an open dry-stone revetment, which lets winter cold in and keeps the cut bank frozen. Repaired: re-stacking all the rock in place only balances when overburden is at most 2.5 times the seam thickness. So only a facing layer at least 1 m thick is placed as dry stone (0.23-0.46 m³ per tonne of coal, matching the 0.8-1.5 m used in crushed-rock slope protection), and the surplus rock goes as riprap for the villages' own eroding banks. Sites need at most 1 m³ of rock per m³ of coal and must lie within about 50-150 km of the villages; bluffs on the outside of river bends give deep water at the face. One barge working 60 mining days cuts 42-56 thousand tonnes a season for 5-8 villages (about 7 t per person per year), provided autonomous stone placement reaches at least 15 m³/h; at 5 m³/h output is capped at 8-16 thousand tonnes.
VALUE: Delivered at 2,100-2,500 rub/t (117-141 rub/GJ even for weathered coal at 18 MJ/kg) against 6,962 rub/t (332 rub/GJ) for today's barge supply to the Kolyma coal depots, about 2.4-2.8× cheaper per GJ delivered; it is only 1.2-1.4× cheaper than a small land pit plus winter road, and about 10× cheaper only against the multi-leg Arctic chains at up to 25,000 rub/t.
REVIEW FINDINGS:
- FATAL: The output depends on at least 15 m³/h of autonomous stone placement, about 7× the best demonstrated autonomous dry-stone rate, and at that rate output falls 3-16× and the cost advantage disappears. (ETH HEAP pilot wall: 68 m³ from 109 stones at 17.4 min per stone ≈ 2.15 m³/h of placement time, reported as close to human-operator speed; at 2.15 m³/h output is 3.4-13.5 kt per season, and with 60-80% fixed costs delivered coal is 265-1,840 rub/GJ vs 332 rub/GJ for barge supply and ~140-195 rub/GJ for a land pit plus winter road.)
- MAJOR: An open dry-stone layer lets river water through, so at and below the waterline it cannot keep the cut bank frozen; the 0.8-1.5 m analogue comes from dry embankment slopes. (With the bank face at summer water temperature (10-15 °C; Srednekolymsk July air mean 13.9 °C), the Stefan thaw behind the rock is 1.7-4.6 m per 100-day season (10-30% ice), and below winter low water the bank never refreezes.)
- MAJOR: One barge's output equals a quarter of all utility coal used in Arctic Yakutia, more than any single bluff within 150 km of 5-8 villages can sell, and the 7 t/person figure is unsourced. (About 205 kt of utility coal was delivered to Arctic Yakutia in 2016 (88 kt Dzhebariki-Khaya + 115 kt Zyryanka + 1.8 kt) for 67,798 people (13 districts, 2021), ≈3 t/person vs 7; 42-56 kt = 21-27% of that total; 6,000-8,000 people × 3 t = 18-24 kt, a load of 32-57%.)
- MAJOR: The 2.4-2.8× compares a local source with today's long-haul chain, and against a land pit at the same deposit, the proper competitor, the gain is the stated 1.2-1.4×, which fails "several times". (1.2-1.4× is 2.1-2.5× short of a 3× target; small "micro-pits" close to consumers are already proposed for Arctic Yakutia (Fedorov 2026).)
- MAJOR: At the permitted rock ratio the surplus rock weighs about as much as the coal, and loading, hauling and placing it are not in the cost, while 5-8 villages' bank works cannot take it every year. (At 1 m³ of rock per m³ of coal: 1.77 t of rock per t of coal; the facing takes 0.37-0.74 t/t, leaving 1.03-1.40 t/t = 43-78 kt of rock per 42-56 kt season.)
- MAJOR: The surplus rock is too small, and probably too weak, to be riprap on village banks hit by river ice. (Ice guidance asks for stones at least as large as the ice thickness (Guide to Bridge Hydraulics 2004) or D100 ≈ 2.5× ice thickness (IAHR 1997), i.e. 1.2-2.0 m (4.5-21 t) up to 3-5 m for 1.2-2 m river ice, but the facing is only ≥1 m thick; weathered coal-measure mudstone has slake durability Id2 of 2-68%.)

DESIGN 4
NAME: Wintering Miner-Barge Boilerhouses
MECHANISM: Anchor: the coal barge is itself the village boilerhouse; its hold is the winter coal bunker, it freezes into a backwater beside the heat network, and it leaves after the spring ice breaks up to refill. Repaired: unpowered 2,000 t barges (100×22 m, drawing 1.26 m instead of 3.5-3.9 m for 5,000-8,000 t hulls, which the shoaling Yana and Indigirka cannot float). Each carries a containerized automatic coal boiler (1.3 MW average, 3.2 MW peak, enough for about 300-700 people). One tug serves several barges, the hold is loaded directly by the floating bluff miner or a riverside mine, and the haul is at most 300-800 km. The barge is away from the village for 92 summer days; its own round trip takes only 12-22 days, which leaves about 75 days of paid freight work that covers the cost of a used hull.
VALUE: 3,000-5,000 rub/t at 300-800 km against about 8,080 rub/t for current river supply plus transshipment, trucking and stockpile losses, so 1.6-2.7× cheaper; at the original 1,500 km with new 8,000 t hulls it is 0.66-0.88× (more expensive), and it only fits sub-Arctic river villages that need no summer heating.
REVIEW FINDINGS:
- MAJOR: The 92-day summer absence is longer than the no-heating window at most villages on the named rivers, so a shore boiler and stockpile must stay, bringing back the transshipment, trucking and stockpile costs counted as savings. (Days with a daily mean above 8 °C, from monthly normals: Chokurdakh 51 (41 heating days uncovered), Srednekolymsk as a proxy for mid-river sub-Arctic villages 78-80 (12-14 uncovered); only Verkhoyansk-type upper-Yana sites (90 days) fit.)
- MAJOR: The 2,000 t hold does not reliably last the winter and cannot be topped up once the barge is frozen in. (1.3 MW over a 275-314-day season with the chain's own 18 MJ/kg coal at η 0.75-0.85 needs 2,019-2,612 t, plus 2-10% (26-132 kW) to keep the hold coal from freezing; Idea 3's 7 t/person makes 2,000 t enough for 286 people, not 300-700; the stated 2.46 peak-to-average ratio contradicts degree-days (1.62-1.73 at Verkhoyansk), and a real 3.2 MW peak means a 1.85-1.98 MW average = 2,460-3,490 t.)
- MAJOR: The 1.6-2.7× comes mostly from the shorter haul, which a conventional barge from the same source also gets, while the bunker barge carries one cargo a year. (The same concept at 1,500 km is 0.66-0.88×; in the same 92 days a conventional hull makes 4-7 coal round trips of 12-22 days (8,000-14,000 t vs 2,000 t), so hull and tug capital per tonne is 4-7× higher, offset only by an unsourced "75 days of paid freight".)
- MAJOR: The village's only heat source has to keep running, frozen in beside the bank, through spring break-up, when ice runs and the water rises by metres. (On the Yana at Batagay and Ust-Kuyga, ice runs have carried vessels onto the ice and behind islands (Mintrans, 2002); the Indigirka stood ~3.5 m above its 2009-2017 mean in 2017; Chokurdakh's June mean is 6.7 °C, so heat is still needed then.)
- MAJOR: Coal stored about 9 months beside a boiler either freezes (if wet) or, kept warm, can self-heat, and the design has no monitoring or inerting. (P&I guidance: coal at ≥55 °C may ignite spontaneously and CO ≥50 ppm signals self-heating; keeping the hold unfrozen takes 26-132 kW.)

Frozen ground resists tools (blade wear, refreezing, saws useless); only summer-temperature water thaws cheaply, at a year's lead.
Small coal and plant schemes died when a captive buyer or subsidy vanished, or when capital overran 4x.
