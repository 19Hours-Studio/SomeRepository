=== REVIEW ===
IDEA 1: Talik Dredging Under Lakes
DEFECT | FATAL | At the specified minimum lake size the dredge runs out of lake within weeks, so the 300 days/yr behind 210 rub/m³ cannot be reached without moving between lakes across permafrost, which needs the very thawing the method excludes and is not costed. | The idea's own 300-850 m³/day of ice at 1.2-1.9 m means the pond advances 250-447 m²/day (3,900-7,200 m³/day of ground); a 100-m lake (7,854 m²) lasts 18-31 days, and only 4-9 days with the full 15.5-m column thawed; 300 days needs 75,000-134,000 m² of lake per year, i.e. 10-17 lakes of 100 m, or one lake 310-410 m across per year (1.0-1.3 km across for a 10-year dredge life).
DEFECT | MAJOR | The talik covers the 18-m digging column only near the lake centre, so the pay and bedrock under most of a qualifying lake are still frozen. | Steady disk-source solution: a 100-m lake with a 19-20.5 m centre talik is thawed to 15.5 m below the bed only within r ≈ 25-27 m (25-30% of the area), and nowhere at the stated 15 m low end; 18 m is also the ceiling of the medium-depth dredge class (Mining Encyclopedia), so there is no depth margin; real taliks are often transient (2015 coring found only ~12 m under a Yukechi Yedoma lake, Central Yakutia).
DEFECT | MAJOR | The like-for-like gain from the method is 1.16×, far below "several times"; the 1.6× and 2× figures compare against other deposits or methods (pre-thawed permafrost, a drained lake), not the best way to mine this lake. | 1.16× vs summer-only dredging of the same talik is 2.6× short of a 3× target.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The sawn ice cannot be "sunk" and the floating sheet cannot carry it, so a 24/7 lift-and-haul operation to shore is needed that is not in the 210 rub/m³. | A 2×2 m block (4.4-7.0 t) has 0.40-0.63 t of net buoyancy; the sheet carries only 0.11-0.17 m of stacked ice before flooding, but 135 winter days × 300-850 m³ = 40,500-114,750 m³ = 5.2-14.6 m over a 100-m lake, i.e. 62-112 lifts a day (2.6-4.7 per hour).
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 0.69-0.81 MW pond loss is a calm-air value; in moderate wind 1 MW of waste heat is too little, and paying for the shortfall eats much of the 16% winter gain. | The stated loss implies 12.0-13.1 W/m²K; the Makinson Inlet polynya lost ~484 W/m² at −30 °C and 5 m/s (16.7 W/m²K), which scales to 0.88-1.13 MW for 1,500 m²; a bulk-formula model passes 1 MW at 4-5 m/s; fuel for a 0.13-0.46 MW shortfall costs 5-30 rub/m³ (2-14% of 210); if heat is lost the pond freezes at 6-9 mm/h (14-21 cm in the first day).
DEFECT | MAJOR | Extending the dredging season by keeping ponds unfrozen is established practice and research, so the only gain specific to the method fails "never tried". | Dredging seasons run 340-350 days in the Urals and West Siberia vs 150-160 in the North-East (Mining Encyclopedia); a 2017 paper systematises season-extension methods based on making and keeping unfrozen water (Marksheideriya i Nedropolzovanie no. 1); Nafikov 2021 (SFU thesis) treats season extension for dredging in the Far North as long-studied.
DEFECT | MINOR | The melt-power figure does not reproduce at the low end. | 300-850 m³/day of ice = 1.06-3.01 MW of latent heat (1.20-3.39 MW including warming from −20 °C), not 2-3 MW.
DEFECT | MINOR | The ice window is optimistic for the coldest placer districts and for windswept lakes. | Verkhoyansk monthly normals give ~6,690 °C·day (above the 6,500 bound); α = 0.027 (windy lake, little snow) gives 1.91-2.18 m, leaving 0-0.6 m of water under 2-2.5 m lakes; pure Stefan with no snow gives 2.49-2.84 m.
DEFECT | MINOR | No test is defined, and a direct winter-vs-summer comparison per gram cannot resolve 16% within a pilot. | With grade CV 0.3-0.5 between monthly blocks, n = 64-178 block-months per arm at 95%/80%, about 14-40 winter seasons.
SURVIVES | Ice of 1.2-1.9 m reproduces (α 0.017-0.024 at 5,000-6,500 °C·day); 0.69-0.81 MW reproduces for calm air (bulk model 0.57-0.71 MW at 1-2 m/s); talik depths of 15-22 m and 36-67 m are reachable with steady-state parameters (the same sets give 32-81 m under 200-m lakes); ~500 m of saw cut a day matches 250-447 m²/day; 710/210 implies a plausible 0.30 g/m³ recovered.

IDEA 2: Jacked Frozen-Block Stripping
DEFECT | FATAL | The whole advantage rests on an unsourced hot-water drilling rate of 1 m/min through frozen overburden, faster than documented hot-water drills reach in clean ice, and at realistic rates the method is at or below its own break-even. | Talalay et al. 2018: 0.57-0.62 m/min in clean ice with a 42 kW (10 L/min at 60 °C) drill; a large USGS high-pressure drill reached 1.5 m/min only in temperate (0 °C) ice; CRREL's 1966-68 water-jet work judged continuous jets "unlikely to be of value" in frozen ground (cited in MIRL OF 83-01); calibrated on the idea's own points (760 rub/g at 1 m/min, 990 at 0.5) the cost is 913/1,297/2,830 rub/g at 0.6/0.3/0.1 m/min = 1.08×/0.76×/0.35× of baseline, and even at 1 m/min the gain is only 1.30×.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 3 MN force reserve cannot absorb any unthawed bond left in the side cuts or at the thawed base. | 95 MN installed vs 92 MN required (3.3%); at the 0.20 MPa frozen-bond strength implied by SF 3.5 (322 MN over 1,600 m²), unthawed bridges larger than 15 m² (0.5% of the 2,800 m² of base plus three side cuts; 3 m² at 1 MPa) stop the push.
DEFECT | MAJOR | 92 MN assumes the most favourable friction and a fully floating block, and normal friction on thawed gravel or partial flooding needs more force than is installed. | 92 MN = μ 0.65 on a fully submerged block (μ 0.31 dry); μ 0.65-0.78 (φ′ 33-38°) with water at 80-90% of the block height needs 102-135 MN vs 95 MN installed.
DEFECT | MAJOR | "2-5× less diesel" also depends on the drilling rate, because drill heat per metre is heater power divided by rate, so buying speed with more power does not help either. | With a 42-108 kW heater, drilling heat alone is 0.04-0.10 l/m³ at 1 m/min but 0.13-0.34 l/m³ at 0.3 m/min and 0.39-1.0 l/m³ at 0.1 m/min, at or above the 0.30 l/m³ of layer-thaw dozing.
DEFECT | MAJOR | 62 rub/m³ is not credible for a single rig running round the clock plus its capital. | 8-12 crew × 4 months × ~202k rub/month fully loaded (155.6k rub average mining wage at end-2024 × 1.3) = 6.5-9.7 M rub = 34-51 rub/m³ at 190,000 m³; with 10-15 rub/m³ of diesel (0.12-0.15 l/m³ × 80-100 rub/l) that is 44-66 rub/m³ before paying for 95 MN of jacks, struts or strand jacks, the drill and the pumps.
DEFECT | MAJOR | If the mined cell is not first backfilled to the block's base level, the block breaks as it overhangs the edge, and the sequence has no backfill step. | Cantilever bending stress at 10/15/20 m overhang is 0.56/1.26/2.24 MPa dry (0.26/0.60/1.06 MPa submerged), about the tensile strength of frozen silt or sand near 0 °C (order 0.5-1.5 MPa).
DEFECT | MINOR | The 40 m of push travel and the 10-m-deep flooded jack trench are neither designed nor costed. | 5 MN over 40 m at SF 3 needs I ≥ 0.0116 m⁴ (a 1.0 m × 30 mm tube, ~30 t each, 562 t for 19 struts), or 20-40 re-bracings per block; the trench is not among the 7,600 m of holes.
DEFECT | MINOR | Even at 1 m/min the drilling barely fits the creek-water season. | 11.9 blocks × 127 h = 1,504 h of bit-on-bottom time = 12.5-15 h/day over 100-120 days before moving between holes; 21-25 h/day at 0.6 m/min.
SURVIVES | 40×40×10 m = 16,000 m³ at 1.90 t/m³; 0.23 MPa face stress; 19×5 MN = 95 MN; 990/760 = 1.30× and diesel ratios of 2.0-5.4× reproduce; 92 MN reproduces at μ 0.65 with full flotation; 10 m³ of water per m³ thaws pay with 15-25% ice if creek water is at least 3-8 °C at 30-50% heat-use efficiency; the vegetation mat moves intact.

IDEA 3: Floating Bluff Coal Miner
DEFECT | FATAL | The output depends on at least 15 m³/h of autonomous stone placement, about 7× the best demonstrated autonomous dry-stone rate, and at that rate output falls 3-16× and the cost advantage disappears. | ETH HEAP pilot wall: 68 m³ from 109 stones at 17.4 min per stone ≈ 2.15 m³/h of placement time, reported as close to human-operator speed; at 2.15 m³/h output is 3.4-13.5 kt per season, and with 60-80% fixed costs delivered coal is 265-1,840 rub/GJ vs 332 rub/GJ for barge supply and ~140-195 rub/GJ for a land pit plus winter road.
DEFECT | MAJOR | An open dry-stone layer lets river water through, so at and below the waterline it cannot keep the cut bank frozen; the 0.8-1.5 m analogue comes from dry embankment slopes. | With the bank face at summer water temperature (10-15 °C; Srednekolymsk July air mean 13.9 °C), the Stefan thaw behind the rock is 1.7-4.6 m per 100-day season (10-30% ice), and below winter low water the bank never refreezes.
DEFECT | MAJOR | One barge's output equals a quarter of all utility coal used in Arctic Yakutia, more than any single bluff within 150 km of 5-8 villages can sell, and the 7 t/person figure is unsourced. | About 205 kt of utility coal was delivered to Arctic Yakutia in 2016 (88 kt Dzhebariki-Khaya + 115 kt Zyryanka + 1.8 kt) for 67,798 people (13 districts, 2021), ≈3 t/person vs 7; 42-56 kt = 21-27% of that total; 6,000-8,000 people × 3 t = 18-24 kt, a load of 32-57%.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 2.4-2.8× compares a local source with today's long-haul chain, and against a land pit at the same deposit, the proper competitor, the gain is the stated 1.2-1.4×, which fails "several times". | 1.2-1.4× is 2.1-2.5× short of a 3× target; small "micro-pits" close to consumers are already proposed for Arctic Yakutia (Fedorov 2026).
DEFECT | MAJOR | At the permitted rock ratio the surplus rock weighs about as much as the coal, and loading, hauling and placing it are not in the cost, while 5-8 villages' bank works cannot take it every year. | At 1 m³ of rock per m³ of coal: 1.77 t of rock per t of coal; the facing takes 0.37-0.74 t/t, leaving 1.03-1.40 t/t = 43-78 kt of rock per 42-56 kt season.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The surplus rock is too small, and probably too weak, to be riprap on village banks hit by river ice. | Ice guidance asks for stones at least as large as the ice thickness (Guide to Bridge Hydraulics 2004) or D100 ≈ 2.5× ice thickness (IAHR 1997), i.e. 1.2-2.0 m (4.5-21 t) up to 3-5 m for 1.2-2 m river ice, but the facing is only ≥1 m thick; weathered coal-measure mudstone has slake durability Id2 of 2-68%.
DEFECT | MINOR | The rounding and the output scaling are inconsistent. | 2,500/18 = 138.9 rub/GJ, not 141; placement capacity is linear (15 m³/h × 24 h × 60 d = 21,600 m³ → 47-94 kt, and 5 m³/h → 15.7-31.3 kt), yet the text drops from 42-56 kt to 8-16 kt (2.6-7.0×) for a 3× change in rate.
SURVIVES | 2,100/18 = 116.7 rub/GJ; 332/141 to 332/117 = 2.35-2.84×; 6,962/332 implies a credible 21 MJ/kg baseline coal; 42-56 kt at 7 t/person = 6,000-8,000 people; ~10× against the 25,000 rub/t chains (8.6-10.2× per GJ); 60 mining days fit the open-water season.

IDEA 4: Wintering Miner-Barge Boilerhouses
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 92-day summer absence is longer than the no-heating window at most villages on the named rivers, so a shore boiler and stockpile must stay, bringing back the transshipment, trucking and stockpile costs counted as savings. | Days with a daily mean above 8 °C, from monthly normals: Chokurdakh 51 (41 heating days uncovered), Srednekolymsk as a proxy for mid-river sub-Arctic villages 78-80 (12-14 uncovered); only Verkhoyansk-type upper-Yana sites (90 days) fit.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 2,000 t hold does not reliably last the winter and cannot be topped up once the barge is frozen in. | 1.3 MW over a 275-314-day season with the chain's own 18 MJ/kg coal at η 0.75-0.85 needs 2,019-2,612 t, plus 2-10% (26-132 kW) to keep the hold coal from freezing; Idea 3's 7 t/person makes 2,000 t enough for 286 people, not 300-700; the stated 2.46 peak-to-average ratio contradicts degree-days (1.62-1.73 at Verkhoyansk), and a real 3.2 MW peak means a 1.85-1.98 MW average = 2,460-3,490 t.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The 1.6-2.7× comes mostly from the shorter haul, which a conventional barge from the same source also gets, while the bunker barge carries one cargo a year. | The same concept at 1,500 km is 0.66-0.88×; in the same 92 days a conventional hull makes 4-7 coal round trips of 12-22 days (8,000-14,000 t vs 2,000 t), so hull and tug capital per tonne is 4-7× higher, offset only by an unsourced "75 days of paid freight".
DEFECT | MAJOR | The village's only heat source has to keep running, frozen in beside the bank, through spring break-up, when ice runs and the water rises by metres. | On the Yana at Batagay and Ust-Kuyga, ice runs have carried vessels onto the ice and behind islands (Mintrans, 2002); the Indigirka stood ~3.5 m above its 2009-2017 mean in 2017; Chokurdakh's June mean is 6.7 °C, so heat is still needed then.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Coal stored about 9 months beside a boiler either freezes (if wet) or, kept warm, can self-heat, and the design has no monitoring or inerting. | P&I guidance: coal at ≥55 °C may ignite spontaneously and CO ≥50 ppm signals self-heating; keeping the hold unfrozen takes 26-132 kW.
DEFECT | MINOR | The 1.26 m draft needs an unrealistically light hull, and a "used" 100×22 m hull is unlikely to exist. | 1.26 m needs lightship plus boiler ≤495 t (Cb 0.9) or ≤772 t (Cb 1.0); scaling the V1500 river barge (75×17.2 m, 1,500 t at 1.8 m) gives 736-1,277 t and a 1.50-1.52 m draft; standard 1,500-1,800 t hulls are 75×16-17 m.
DEFECT | MINOR | No test can soon show the reliability a sole heat source needs. | Showing ≤5% failure per winter at 95% confidence needs about 60 failure-free barge-winters (rule of three).
SURVIVES | 92 − (12 to 22) = 70-80 days ("about 75"); 8,080/5,000 to 8,080/3,000 = 1.62-2.69×; 2,000 t matches 1.3 MW only with 21 MJ/kg coal over Verkhoyansk's 275-day season (1,730-1,960 t); Verkhoyansk's 90-day warm window fits the 92-day absence to within ~2 days.
