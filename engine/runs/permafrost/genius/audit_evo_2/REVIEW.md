=== REVIEW ===
IDEA 1: Pond-Heated Jet Undercut Caving
DEFECT | MAJOR | The roof is not shown to cave "within hours to days": bending stress falls as 1/cover, and across the idea's own range of long-term strength the time to failure spans five orders of magnitude, with no caving at all for thicker cover. | sigma=gamma*L^2/(2h) with gamma=19 kN/m3 and L=25 m gives 0.59 MPa at 10 m of cover, 0.40 at 15 m, 0.30 at 20 m and 0.20 at 30 m. A Vyalov law sigma=beta/ln(t/B), fitted to 1-2 MPa short-term and 0.2-0.5 MPa 50-yr strength, gives a time to failure at 0.58 MPa of 0.015-789 days. At 20 m or more of cover the roof never fails if the long-term strength is 0.35 MPa or above. Rochev (Gornyi Zh. 2016) cites the "stable roof" of frozen pay as the reason borehole hydraulic mining works.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Output depends on a face thaw rate that is assumed, not sourced: two monitors at 1,543 m3/d each on a 25 m face means the face retreats 21-62 m/d (pay 3-1 m), which needs a face-average heat flux of 22-66 kW/m2. | That flux needs h=11-33 kW/m2K at the ~2 K mean dT allowed by water going in at 4 C and leaving at 0.9 C. A sweeping jet would have to hold that over the whole face, all the time. It is the top of the range for a rough fast film (8-22 kW/m2K at 1-3 m/s; Gnielinski x3 roughness per Douglas et al. 2023) and equals jet stagnation values (12-15 kW/m2K for a 50 mm jet at 10-15 m/s). Each monitor needs hA of at least 781 kW/K; at h=3-6 kW/m2K that means 130-260 m2 of wetted frozen surface against a 50 m2 face, so the heat goes into roof, ribs and bedrock rather than pay.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The cost ratio does not reproduce, and the idea's own sensitivity range wipes most of it out. | 650/620 implies 1.05 g/m3 recovered. 1,100/620 = 1.77x and 2,650/620 = 4.27x (not 3.4x). At the 965 rub/m3 end, 920 rub/g gives only 1.20x against 1,100 rub/g, which is not "several times".
DEFECT | MAJOR | It is not new, and it ignores the direct competitor: thawing frozen placer pay with water jets and lifting it as slurry has been field-tested, and borehole hydraulic mining is the baseline for deep thin frozen placers. | The USBM/NIOSH prototype borehole miner (Tech News 460, Tenderfoot Creek, AK; 0.3 m pay under 13 m cover) jet-thawed the pay and pumped it up. Arens et al. (Gornyi Zh. 2019) used warm-water jetting with an airlift at the Ryveem placer in Chukotka and designed for up to 1 t Au/yr.
DEFECT | MAJOR | By its own Shields result (0.026-0.043 < 0.047), gravel will not flow along a 0.5-1% floor, so the hydro-elevator sump must stay within one jet working length of the face and be moved every few hours under unsupported roof. That contradicts "nobody underground". | A monitor's working length is 350-450 nozzle diameters, about 18-34 m for 50-75 mm nozzles, while the face retreats 21-62 m/d.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Gold in the bedrock is lost: 4-7 C jets cannot dig frozen fractured bedrock, which is normally mined as part of the pay, yet recovery is costed as if equal to open-cut. | Taking the usual 0.2-0.5 m of bedrock (typical practice, not sourced here), that is 7-50% of a 1-3 m pay layer. Each 10% of gold left behind raises the 620 rub/g by about 11%.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Water inrush and thermokarst are not mentioned: caving 6-18 m of cover in the valley floor brings cracks and subsidence right under the creek and beside the 3 ha pond that feed the jets. | Subsidence of 0.7-0.9 times the extracted height (assumed caving factor) is 0.7-2.7 m over 25 m panels.
DEFECT | MINOR | The 7 C heat ratio is wrong, and the 4 C margin is thin. | The need implied by 1.27 at 4 C is 92 MJ/m3 (30%-porosity ice, which is consistent). The same need gives 2.22 at 7 C, not 2.5. The 1.27 requires 79% of the water's heat above 0 C to go into pay. An energy balance for a 7 C pond (Susuman 14.9/10.9 C, Kt 0.40-0.45) gives only 3.1-3.6 MW in August, against the 6.4 MW needed to heat 0.25 m3/s from 0.9 to 7 C. The 70-day season is also longer than July-August (62 d).
SURVIVES | The Shields figures reproduce at constant discharge (tau* ~ S^0.7, ratio 1.62 against 1.65 stated). 5.4 MW is a plausible July-August average for the pond (4.7-6.5 MW from the energy balance). The water flow (0.25 m3/s) and thaw demand (3.3 MW) are consistent, and 0.58 MPa holds at 10 m of cover.

IDEA 2: Frozen-In Barge Auger Drift Mining
DEFECT | FATAL | The design's own stability condition, backfill freezing in each hole "within weeks", cannot be met by conduction. | An enthalpy model at 0.35 water fraction gives these freeze times for an isolated hole of 2.2 m diameter: 304 d at -3 C, 164 d at -5 C, and 93 d even at -8 C. A 1.6 m hole takes 87-161 d. A whole panel, where the pillars hold only 3-7% of the cold the backfill's latent heat needs, takes about 1.3-15 yr at -5 to -2 C. Measured ground temperatures in the Upper Kolyma are -0.1 to -3.8 C (Kriosfera Zemli 2024), and floodplains are only seasonally frozen (ICOP 2024, Verkhoyansk).
DEFECT | FATAL | It fails the brief and has no niche: by its own numbers it costs more than drift mining for exactly the deep placers it targets. | $36/g against $29/g for drift mining (1.24x dearer) and $23/g for open pit (1.57x dearer). The brief asks for several times cheaper.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The pillar check uses 10-20 m of cover, but the target is 30-40 m, which doubles to quadruples the stress on pillars about 0.6 m wide at the waist. | The implied factor is 4.5 (0.9 MPa / 0.2 MPa), so 30-40 m of cover gives 2.7-3.6 MPa. That is 34-72% of the 5-8 MPa short-term strength and above an assumed long-term strength of about a third of short-term (1.7-2.7 MPa). The geometry that reproduces 0.61 extraction is r=1.05-1.1 m, s=2.6-2.8 m, a 0.5-0.6 m waist and w/h about 0.27.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Boring horizontally from a barge needs bedrock at river level under 30-40 m of cover. That means driving 2.2 m holes, in effect an artificial thermo-erosional niche, into the toe of a 32-42 m frozen bluff with the barge parked in a notch below it, and it is all flooded at breakup. | High water on the upper and middle Lena rises 9-15 m (Britannica), more than 10 m above low water at Yakutsk, with ice jams. The open holes and unfrozen backfill sit below that level.
DEFECT | MAJOR | There is no winter process water on rivers that freeze to the bed, and the 280-day heated operation does not cost a closed heated water circuit. | The Yana at Verkhoyansk is frozen to the bottom 70-110 days a year, yet 215k m3 must be washed and returned as slurry backfill.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The stated geometry needs an auger at or beyond the largest ever built, working in abrasive frozen gravel with 0.4 m clasts. | 0.61 extraction with the centre 0.5 m above bedrock and a 0.55-0.6 m bedrock cut needs D of about 2.1-2.2 m. The largest coal auger was 84 in (2.13 m), and the modern CAS AM1500 is 1.83 m; at D=1.83 m extraction is only 0.38-0.58.
DEFECT | MINOR | The 65-74% bedrock sweep assumes flat bedrock along a straight bore of 60-200 m. | With r=1.1 m and the centre 0.5 m up, bedrock 0.3 m lower cuts the contact chord from 1.96 m to 1.51 m (sweep 0.70 to 0.54), and bedrock 0.6 m lower gives no contact at all.
SURVIVES | $36/g reproduces: 3 g/m3 x 1.8/2.3 dilution x 0.70 sweep x 0.85 plant = 1.40 g/m3, so $45-55/m3 gives $32-39/g. The 0.9-1.8 MPa at 10-20 m reproduces (factor 4.5), and the open-water and winter rates are consistent (540-770 vs 768 m3/d).

IDEA 3: Sealed Derelict-Hull Coal Bunker
DEFECT | FATAL | The value claim contradicts the idea's own numbers: the bunker costs more than the handling it removes, so in general it raises cost rather than giving "parity to 1.8x". | Removable handling is 12-29% of 8,080 = 970-2,343 rub/t, against a 2,800 rub/t bunker. That is +457 to +1,830 rub/t, or +6-23% on delivered cost, with the bunker costing 1.2-2.9x what it replaces. Breaking even in failure-prone villages needs a failed-delivery premium of at least 1,500-9,200 rub/t at p=0.2-0.3.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The baseline ignores what a competent operator would do: keep a second-winter buffer on the existing land yard, or top up by winter road (as with fuel to Olenyok district in 2026). Against that, the bunker only saves the buffer's open-storage losses. | 2,800/8,080 means the bunker breaks even only if open-yard storage destroys at least 35% of the coal's value per year.
DEFECT | MAJOR | It is not new: old hulls used as permanent coal stores were standard port practice for about a century. | Larkins coal hulk at Albany, WA (1853); Himalaya as hulk C60 (1894-95); HMAS Hankow, Sydney (1913); the Russian "blokshiv".
DEFECT | MAJOR | The afloat option needs about 3.8 m of water at winter low level, which the backwaters of shallow-river villages (where barges fail) rarely have. The grounded-pad option floats off in every spring flood. | Waterplane 2,878 m2 gives a draught of 0.52 m + coal(kt)/2.878, i.e. 1.6-2.6 m with 3-6 kt aboard, against spring rises of several metres (9-15 m on the Lena) with ice drift. The dike needed to prevent this is not costed.
DEFECT | MAJOR | Winter reclaim is unsolved and breaks the seal: a river hull has no self-unloader, so frozen coal must be dug daily from a 4-5 m hold by grab or loader in a space that is N2-inerted and producing CO. | 2-3.5 kt over about 240 days is 8-15 t/day. Opening the hatches lets in far more air than the 80 kg O2/day (267 m3 air/day) budget, and entry into enclosed holds is a known cause of fatalities on bulk ships.
DEFECT | MINOR | Self-heating is judged by the bulk-average temperature rise, but ignition is local, where air gets in, and storage spans 1-3 summers. | 80 kg O2/day x 12.3 MJ/kg = 11.4 kW, or 197 GJ per 200-day winter: enough to heat 200 t of coal by the hatches by about 900 K if not dissipated. Low-rank coal on open yards starts self-heating after about 2 weeks (TPU 2015).
DEFECT | MINOR | The 1.3 MPa ice-crack stress does not reproduce. | A beam on an elastic foundation under an edge load gives 1.6-3.6 MPa for 1.5-2 m ice at the 3 kt uplift; 1.3 MPa matches about 1 m of ice or 1 kt. The conclusion holds: the ice cracks after 0.6-1 kt has been burned.
SURVIVES | These all reproduce: 2.1-4 winters of storage; the 1.05 m rise (3 kt over 2,878 m2); the 32 kPa pad pressure; 76 kW = 3.3% of 2.3 MW; 1.4 t O2 giving 1.96 K; 80 kg O2/day giving 11.4 kW. The 76 kW moat works only if it is no wider than 0.2-0.4 m at -40 C, where open water loses 650-1,400 W/m2.

IDEA 4: Under-Ice Brine-Thawed Tin Dredging
DEFECT | FATAL | It is not cheaper than best current practice: the "3-5x" is measured against a method that already failed, and it mixes grades. | At equal grade the gain over the seasonal mode is 3.4x (78.8 vs 23 $k/t at 0.8 kg/m3). PT Timah's cash cost was $17.9k/t in FY24 (about $20k in FY25), so $23-37k/t is 1.15-2.1x dearer. Break-even grade is 0.84 kg/m3 to match $22k and 1.32 kg/m3 to match $14k, and no measured grade for the Vankina/Chokurdakh placer is given.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The borehole the idea cites does not support its "0.3 km offshore" limit: at that borehole's own linear degradation rate, ice-bonded permafrost 300 m offshore lies above the 17 m dig floor. Even a sqrt(t) law leaves only about 2 m of margin, and the borehole is in a different bay with a different coastal-retreat history. | Buor-Khaya (Overduin et al. 2015): 4 m water, 2 m sea ice, about 750-800 m offshore, ice-bonded permafrost at 24.75 m below seabed (28 m below sea level), coast retreating 1-2 m/yr and the frozen layer deepening 3-4 cm/yr. At 300 m offshore that gives 150-300 yr x 3-4 cm = 4.5-12 m below seabed, i.e. 8.5-16 m below sea level, shallower than 17 m. A sqrt(t) law gives 15.2 m below seabed, about 19 m below sea level.
DEFECT | MAJOR | The Dec-June window is far longer than the only working analogue allows, and landfast ice is not reliably stationary. | Under-ice dredging at Nome is allowed only from mid-January to mid-April (about 90 days); the 15 April cutoff is set for deteriorating ice and breakup risk. Landfast ice on the Siberian shelf has midseason breakouts driven by cyclones (EGUsphere-2026-826). The 214 days needs 74% availability under ice (156 of 212 days) with no operating record.
DEFECT | MAJOR | It is not untried: winter dredging through landfast sea ice has been routine at Nome since the 2000s. | KNOM reported 11-12 crews in 2014, running sled-mounted suction dredges over holes cut in the sea ice.
DEFECT | MINOR | At the 4 m depth limit only about 2 m of water remains under the ice, so bulked tailings from a 13 m cut pile up into the ice behind the dredge. In practice the lower depth limit is nearer 6 m, where ice per m3 of ground rises. | A swell of 1.1-1.3 on a 13 m cut raises the bed 1.3-3.9 m, against a 1.8-2.2 m gap (4 m minus 0.9 x 2.0-2.5 m of ice). Ice per m3 of ground is 0.17 only at 4 m of water; at 8 m it is 0.24.
DEFECT | MINOR | The melt power does not reproduce at the stated dredge rate. | 600 m3/h x 0.17 = 102 m3/h of ice; x 917 kg/m3 x 334 kJ/kg = 8.7 MW, not 4.3 MW (4.3 MW matches about 300 m3/h). Sawing instead means lifting about 94 t/h of ice continuously.
DEFECT | MINOR | The turbidity plume under the ice is not addressed: it would sit in a thin layer of water with no wind mixing. | A 600 m3/h dredge handles about 1,000 t/h of solids; losing an assumed 5% as fines puts 50 t/h into a layer about 2 m thick.
SURVIVES | The day count holds (58 + 156 = 214, with Dec-June = 212 d), as do the 0.84 kg/m3 break-even and 37 x 0.5/0.8 = 23. Slot heating holds: 0.65 MW for about 3,000 m2 of covered slot needs U of 6 W/m2K or less, and 2.25 MW of waste heat is available. The Buor-Khaya figure (28 m below sea level) and the history check out (Sevmorolovo stopped over waves and its gravity plant; BIMA was profitable in only 2 of 5 seasons).
