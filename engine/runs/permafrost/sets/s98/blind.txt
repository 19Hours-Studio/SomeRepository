F01
MECHANISM: In winter, slightly buoyant torpedo-shaped pods of concentrate are pushed through a slot cut in the ice of a big Siberian river's main channel. They ride the current along the smooth underside of the ice, out of reach of wind and waves, to a downstream recovery station with a boom and slot at a port. Weekly current profiles taken through the ice show where the flow stays fast. Where it slows, or where slush ice builds a dam below the ice, a relay slot is cut, the pods are lifted past the blockage and released again, and the spacing between releases is adjusted to the measured speed.
VALUE: Ore reaches Arctic ports during the frozen months and is already on the quay, instead of competing for the short summer window on the river.

F02
MECHANISM: An insulated, lined salt-gradient pond is floored with crushed chalcocite-rich copper ore. The dark ore absorbs sunlight like a solar pond's black bottom and sits in the 60–80 °C chloride bottom brine, which is also the leach liquor. In winter the hot brine drives an organic Rankine cycle that dumps its heat into −40 °C air at about twice the efficiency of temperate solar ponds. That power runs a divided cell: cuprous copper in brine drawn from under the ore plates out as powder, and the anode makes cupric chloride that goes back to attack the ore. The chalcocite simply splits into copper and sulfur, with no acid, oxygen or other reagent used up. Fresh ore goes in down a submerged chute along one bank, and a slow submerged scraper, run on the pond's own power, drags the bed across the floor to a sump where leached residue is lifted out. The pond therefore leaches a continuous stream of ore instead of a single batch.
VALUE: A remote chalcocite deposit in the Transbaikal permafrost can produce copper metal year-round at mine scale from local salt and summer sun, with no fuel, acid, oxygen plant or power line.

F03
MECHANISM: Each spring, black UV-stable geotextile is laid over the deposit to absorb round-the-clock summer sun. Each autumn, rows of cheap snow fences are set up to trap a thick snow blanket that blocks most winter refreezing. Buried temperature strings track the thaw front, and film coverage and fence spacing are re-tuned every season to maximise net heat gain, so the unfrozen zone deepens year after year. Light rippers and loaders can then dig the thawed gravel without blasting or heavy frozen-ground machinery.
VALUE: Replaces diesel-intensive excavation of frozen ground with free solar heat for placers and shallow overburden.

F04
MECHANISM: Reusable sealed pods, shaped like Nansen's Fram so that ice pressure squeezes them upward instead of crushing them, are filled with coal or concentrate whose value does not suffer from slow delivery. In autumn they are set on newly formed Laptev Sea ice, drift with the ice to the ice edge at Fram Strait, and are picked up there by an ordinary ship. Comparing daily satellite radar maps of the ice gives drift paths traced backward from the Fram Strait exit. Starting sites whose paths turn off into the Beaufort Gyre are dropped, new sites are added where paths converge on the exit, and pod placement is re-optimized each season from beacon logs.
VALUE: Bulk cargo moves from the Laptev coast to the North Atlantic with no engines, no icebreakers and no infrastructure, trading about two years in transit for almost no freight cost.

F05
MECHANISM: Each autumn, coal or concentrate is sealed in heavy plastic bulk bags on the new Laptev Sea ice, and seawater pumped up through the ice is sprayed into the −40 °C air over each load until it is encased in a thick ice block with the hull shape of Nansen's Fram, so that ice pressure squeezes it upward instead of crushing it. Several cubic metres of ice per tonne make the whole block float even with dense concentrate inside, so the hull costs only the pumping and a bag, is never returned, is thick enough to outlast two melt seasons, and carries a cheap satellite beacon frozen in with the load. The Transpolar Drift carries the blocks to the ice edge in Fram Strait in about two years, where an ordinary ship finds them by beacon and lifts out the bags. Launch points are chosen from satellite ice-motion maps traced backward from Fram Strait, keeping only those whose paths reliably lead out rather than into the Beaufort Gyre, and the choice is updated each season from the beacon logs.
VALUE: Bulk coal and concentrate move from the Laptev coast to the North Atlantic at almost no freight cost, with no engines, icebreakers or port and no pod hulls to build or bring back, in exchange for about two years in transit.

F06
MECHANISM: Track panels are laid straight onto snow-compacted frozen tundra, with no permanent embankment, and rivers are crossed on artificially thickened ice. The line runs only during the 5–6 months when the ground is rigid. Each autumn, tamping machines re-level the track to undo the summer heave.
VALUE: Rail-scale bulk capacity reaches remote deposits for a fraction of the cost of an all-season Arctic railway.

F07
MECHANISM: Ice-class LNG carriers fill up by volume long before they reach their weight limit, so both legs from Arctic terminals carry thousands of tonnes of seawater ballast. That is far more free tonnage than any mine's concentrate. Sealed cassettes of crushed but unconcentrated ore take the place of that ballast in the double-bottom spaces and are swapped for seawater at the port where the LNG is unloaded. An ordinary mill there, with grid power and cheap reagents, separates the metal. On the empty return leg the cassettes come back full of diesel, and summer river barges on the Ob and Yenisei shuttle them between the deposits and the terminal, so the mine itself runs only drills, loaders and a crusher.
VALUE: A remote Arctic mine can do without its concentrator, power plant and reagent supply altogether, because the hard step of separating metal moves to a warm, powered port on freight that is already paid for.

F08
MECHANISM: Broken ore is stacked in insulated heaps laced with pipes and saturated with water. Fans then alternate −40°C winter air with warm water heated by waste heat from stranded gas or a generator. This runs many freeze-thaw cycles per winter instead of one per year. Water expands about 9% when it freezes, which forces cracks open along grain boundaries and shatters the rock to fine sizes. Temperature and acoustic sensors in the heap detect when cracking stops, and the cycle timing is adjusted to match.
VALUE: Most of the crushing and grinding energy, the largest energy cost in hard-rock mining, is replaced by free Arctic cold, so remote deposits can be heap-leached or concentrated on site.

F09
MECHANISM: Each winter, crews build barge hulls from pykrete (ice reinforced with sawdust, much stronger and tougher than plain ice) right on a frozen tributary near the deposit. They need only spray nozzles, local sawdust and -40°C air, and they load ore concentrate into the hulls on the spot. A forecast built from snowpack and river-gauge data sets the loading schedule for the spring breakup flood, which briefly makes thousands of otherwise unnavigable creeks deep enough to float the barges. A small pusher tows them to a railhead or Arctic river port, where they are emptied and left to melt. A hull costs little more than water, sawdust and labour, so the cost per tonne is a few weeks of towing rather than a road kept open all year.
VALUE: Turns the spring flood on every remote tributary into near-free, single-use bulk freight capacity with no road, dredging or shipyard.

F10
MECHANISM: Two fixed guns, one at the deposit and one at a railhead or river port more than 100 km away, fire 0.5 t steel casings steeply upward at a modest 0.6–0.8 km/s. Near the top of the arc each casing unfolds spring-loaded wings and glides the rest of the way, steered by satellite-guided fins onto a sand catch bed beside the other gun. Gliding carries a casing several times farther per unit of launch energy than a purely ballistic arc and lands it slowly, so the guns are cheaper and wear less, the casings and wings are reused, and landing errors shrink from tens of metres to a few. Casings fly out full of concentrate and come back full of diesel and reagents, each inbound load carrying more fuel than the outbound shot burns, so the same loop powers the gun, the mill and the camp, with no road, convoy or seasonal return route.
VALUE: A stranded deposit is linked both ways all year, concentrate out and fuel and reagents in, by two fixed installations and no corridor at all, using a fraction of the energy and wear of a purely ballistic link.

F11
MECHANISM: Blasting breaks up the ore body underground, and circulating warm brine thaws only a bounded block of it. A leaching solution is then pumped through the block to dissolve the metal, and wells recover it. Any liquid that leaks toward the edge of the block refreezes on contact with the surrounding frozen ground, so the permafrost acts as a self-healing container. Temperature readings at the boundary show where the seal is. Only the recovered metal, often under 1% of the ore's mass, is ever transported.
VALUE: Extracts metals from remote frozen deposits with no pit, no ore haulage and no artificial liner.

F12
MECHANISM: Water at 0–4 °C is pumped from under lake or river ice and sprayed over coils where propane or CO₂ boils. The water's warmth and the heat it gives off as it freezes boil the fluid, which drives a turbine. The fluid then condenses in finned radiators open to −35 to −50 °C air. About 30 kg/s of water turned to slush gives roughly 1 MW. The slush is piled into ice heaps, and output rises on its own as the air gets colder.
VALUE: Remote deposits get fuel-free power for the seven-month winter, when crushing and haulage peak, and diesel no longer has to be trucked in.

F13
MECHANISM: In winter, crews at the mine's riverbank spray water with about 14% sawdust into hollow barge moulds cut in shore ice, freezing thick hulls around sealed concentrate containers. After the spring ice run, the flood wave floats them free; one light tug pushes a convoy to the river mouth, where cargo moves to sea ships and hulls are broken up to melt. The sawdust's insulating skin slows melting enough for a two-to-four-week trip, and wall thickness is set from forecast water temperature and voyage length.
VALUE: Lets mines on tributaries navigable only at flood peak ship bulk cargo with no steel fleet or return voyages.

F14
MECHANISM: Folded rubber weirs are flown in and inflated in series on a small tributary below a deposit, where they store snowmelt and rain in a chain of ponds. Frost-broken ore is spread in a riffled side lane of the stretch below each weir. Each timed gate release sends a measured flood pulse over the ore that sweeps clay, silt and light rock into the next pond, where they settle as tailings, while gold, cassiterite, diamonds or other heavy minerals stay in the riffles to be scooped out. The same pulses carry shallow-draft barges of this concentrate over each shallow stretch to the navigable river, and after the season the weirs are deflated and flown out, leaving no road, plant or tailings dam.
VALUE: A roadless placer or heavy-mineral deposit gets its washing plant, tailings ponds and haul route from one seasonal staircase of water, with no pumps, fuel or reagents.

F15
MECHANISM: In winter, sprayed water builds an ice wall across a small creek, and a peat cover keeps it from melting. Snowmelt fills the basin behind it, and the water is released through a gate in midsummer as a timed crest. Flat-bottomed barges ride the crest down to a river deep enough for ships. Downstream depth gauges control the gate opening so the crest stays deeper than the barges' draft for the whole distance.
VALUE: Creeks that are too shallow for boats become seasonal freight routes from remote deposits, with no road or dredging.

F16
MECHANISM: Inflatable rubber weirs are flown in folded and set up in series on a small tributary between the deposit and a navigable river; each pond fills with snowmelt and rain. Shallow-draft barges loaded with concentrate wait in the top pond. A hydraulic routing model times the gate releases so that each pulse lifts the barges over the next shallow stretch into the pond below, and the model re-plans from water-level gauges after every pulse. Empty barges are winched back across the flat pools, and the weirs are deflated after the season, leaving no road or rail.
VALUE: Turns an ordinary summer stream into a temporary waterway, so bulk tonnage moves at water-freight cost without building a corridor.

F17
MECHANISM: Dilute brines, heap-leach liquors or mine water are sprayed through snow-making nozzles into -30 to -50°C air over lined pads, building cones of nearly salt-free ice while rejected brine drains to a sump. The sump liquor is re-sprayed in cycles until lithium, potassium or metal content reaches target, and in spring the salty first meltwater is collected separately. Spray rate tracks air temperature so freezing stays complete.
VALUE: Gives Siberia a free equivalent of desert evaporation ponds, so brine minerals and leach metals are concentrated on site before shipping.

F18
MECHANISM: In deep winter, frozen placer gravel is dug and crushed while it stays below −20 °C. At that temperature the ice behaves as a brittle, light mineral. Air tables and dry-washers separate the gold and heavy minerals from gravel and ice by density, with no water at all, and the tailings are stacked frozen.
VALUE: Placer mining can run all year without thawing, water or tailings ponds.

F19
MECHANISM: Crushed dry rock is tumbled against a plate so that different minerals pick up different electric charges. The near-zero moisture of −40 °C air stops that charge from leaking away. The grains fall between two charged plates and are pushed into separate bins according to their charge. Readings from each bin continuously adjust the plate voltage and the dividers between bins.
VALUE: Valuable material is separated from waste on site through the winter with no water, cutting the tonnage to be hauled by 5–20 times.

F20
MECHANISM: The surface of ice-rich yedoma is stripped or darkened along a planned line toward a navigable river, while insulating mats protect the banks as a mask. Each summer, meltwater ponds in the strip, absorbs more sun and deepens the thaw. Mud drains away until thawing stops at ice-poor ground, leaving a channel deep enough for barges. Ground-temperature probes guide each year's re-darkening and widening, like etch-rate control.
VALUE: Solar heat does the digging, so lowland deposits get a waterway for the cost of surface treatment instead of a road at millions of dollars per kilometre.

F21
MECHANISM: Sealed plastic capsules of ore concentrate are lowered through an ice slot near the mine and carried north for months by the current still flowing under frozen Siberian rivers. Each trails a heavy guide chain: if it sinks, more chain rests on the riverbed and it gets lighter, so it holds height between bed and ice unpowered. A net hung from the ice downstream funnels capsules to a cut hole, acoustic tags track each one, and the port stockpiles them for summer shipping.
VALUE: Turns the eight frozen months, when rivers carry nothing, into fuel-free freight far cheaper than winter-road trucking.

F22
MECHANISM: Along a surveyed line from a lowland deposit to a navigable river, the moss is stripped or darkened in a strip with insulating mats along its edges, so the ground beneath thaws first and becomes the weakest path. A sun-warmed thaw lake or a dammed creek at the deposit end is then breached into the strip. Its 10–15 °C water runs down the line, melts the ice-rich yedoma at metres per day by thermo-erosion and carries the collapsing silt to the river. This is the same process that cuts gullies metres deep in days when such lakes drain naturally. Ground-temperature probes show where to darken further before the next lake is released, and once barges can reach the deposit the ore leaves unprocessed for a plant that already has power and reagents.
VALUE: Summer heat stored in lakes digs a barge channel in one or two seasons instead of decades, so a roadless lowland deposit is opened to water freight without building a road.

F23
MECHANISM: Dry crushed ore slides down a chute lined with a chosen material, so each mineral picks up its own sign of charge, and in −40 °C air, which holds almost no water vapour, that charge does not leak away. The grains fall between two plates into two metal-lined bins, each wired to the plate on the far side, so the charge carried into the bins keeps driving the plates up to a spark-gap cap of tens of kilovolts, as in a Kelvin water dropper. One unpowered pass gives only a modest upgrade, but each stage costs only a lining, two plates and a few metres of drop, so a dozen stages are stacked down a hillside or pit wall: each stage's concentrate bin feeds the next stage's chute, and a small conveyor lifts the reject of the lower stages back into the feed. The cascade reaches shipping grade with no power supply, water or reagent for the separation, and its only settings are the splitters, moved according to the grade measured in each bin.
VALUE: Remote deposits make shipping-grade concentrate on site all winter from dry ore, with no water, reagent or power for the separation, cutting the tonnage to be hauled by 5–20 times.

F24
MECHANISM: In winter, shallow moulds on the frozen banks of a river near the mine are flooded with river water mixed with sawdust, and the cold freezes them into thick pykrete hulls. Ore concentrate is sealed inside these hulls in waterproof bags. At spring breakup the hulls float free and ride the flood hundreds of kilometres downstream to an Arctic port or rail point. There the cargo is lifted out and the hull is simply left to melt. A short GPS and ice-thickness log for each hull is used to adjust next year's hull thickness and launch dates to the river's real flood behaviour.
VALUE: Bulk cargo can leave roadless deposits once a year with no steel fleet, no return trips and no road, using a free current and a hull that costs almost nothing.

F25
MECHANISM: Frozen placer gravel is dug in winter and fed, still below −30 °C, through a light impact breaker. The brittle ice cement shatters while pebbles and gold grains stay whole. A screen then removes the pebbles, and a rising air stream blows away the ice chips and silt. The remaining sand passes over a corona-roll electrostatic separator, which throws off conducting gold and black sand while quartz and ice stay pinned to the roll. Mineral-sands plants have to heat their feed to dry it for this step, but −30 °C air holds almost no water, so the frost does that drying for free. A permanent magnet then strips out magnetite and leaves a gold concentrate. Electrostatic force grips fine and flaky gold best, which are exactly the sizes that air and water tables lose.
VALUE: Placer operators in Yakutia, Magadan and Chukotka can mine all winter straight from frozen ground and recover their fine gold, using a small generator and no thawing, water, ponds or reagents.

F26
MECHANISM: Copper-bearing liquor from a summer heap leach is stored in a pond, where it stays liquid at 0–4 °C under the winter ice. Through the winter nearly all of it is frozen in successive passes over coils, and the heat given off as it freezes boils propane, which drives a turbine and condenses in finned radiators open to −35 to −50 °C air, giving roughly 15–30 kJ of electricity per kilogram frozen. Each pass leaves a stronger drained brine, until copper sulfate reaches saturation and crystallises out as blue vitriol while the far more soluble ferric sulfate stays dissolved, so freezing both powers the plant and separates the copper from the iron. The crystals are redissolved in the spent electrolyte of a small, clean electrowinning cell run on the same power, and in spring the ferric, acidic brine and the ice meltwater, with any copper trapped in it, return to the heap as its oxidant and acid.
VALUE: A remote copper heap leach makes its own winter power, separates its copper from iron and wins clean cathode copper with no solvent extraction, no diesel and almost no acid shipped in.

F27
MECHANISM: Before freeze-up, placer sand screened in summer is laid as a long windrow beside a riffle mat on the bed of a river reach that keeps flowing all winter. Through the winter, snow is scraped off a band of ice across the reach above the mat and piled on the ice upstream and downstream of it. Bare ice grows much faster than snow-covered ice, so the band grows a keel that squeezes the whole river's flow beneath it and speeds up the current over the windrow, faster each month as the keel deepens. The faster current strips the windrow grain by grain: light grains leave downstream, gold and heavy minerals drop into the riffles, and in late winter the mat is lifted through a slot and the concentrate is cleaned by hand.
VALUE: A placer mine can wash a full season's sand during winter at several times the natural under-ice rate, with frost building the sluice and the river powering it, so the short summer is spent only digging and no washing fuel, pumps or heated plant are needed.

F28
MECHANISM: At a nickel, cobalt or iron deposit close to a gas trunk line, a little of the line's gas is partly burned to make carbon monoxide. That carbon monoxide is passed over crushed, reduced ore at about 50 °C and a few bar, and the metal leaves as a volatile vapour (one nickel atom bound to four carbon monoxide molecules). The vapour is metered into the pipeline gas at about 0.1 mol%. That keeps it below its condensation pressure at line temperatures, and dosing follows measured flow and temperature. At the receiving plant, before any customer, the gas passes over beds heated to about 200 °C. The vapour splits there into pure metal pellets and carbon monoxide, which is burned or sent back.
VALUE: A line carrying about 38 billion m³ a year could carry roughly 100,000 t of refined nickel a year at almost no extra transport cost, using infrastructure that is already built.

F29
MECHANISM: Oil fields, gas fields and deep mines on the Siberian Platform already pump up strong calcium-chloride brine (often 0.2–0.5 g/L lithium) and must dispose of it. In winter this brine runs through a chain of shallow trays covered with polymer radiative-cooling film under an infrared-clear windshield. The film sheds heat through the 8–13 µm atmospheric window, so the trays sit about 10 K below air that is already at −40 to −50 °C. Each night every tray freezes past the calcium chloride–ice eutectic (about −50 °C). Ice and calcium chloride hexahydrate lock up together as a crust, while lithium chloride, which stays liquid down to about −75 °C, collects in a shrinking leftover liquid that drains by gravity into the next tray. After a few nights and steps, the liquid is tens of times smaller in volume and richer in lithium and bromide, and goes to an existing lithium plant. The spent crust melts in spring and goes back down the field's own disposal wells.
VALUE: Brine that Siberian oil, gas and mine operators now pay to reinject becomes a large-scale lithium concentrate, made with only the winter sky and the wells they already run.

F30
MECHANISM: Sprinkler rigs build a narrow, graded ice trough along a fixed line from a deposit to a railhead or river. They refresh it automatically whenever the temperature drops enough. Steel-runner ore sleds clip onto an endless steel cable loop, like a ski lift, driven by motors at the two ends. Because runners on ice have friction close to rail, a single loop moves a continuous stream of cargo without trucks, drivers or road surface. Sensors on the track trigger re-spraying wherever the ice wears down.
VALUE: Gives near-railway freight costs every winter for the price of a cable, two winches and a water pump.

F31
MECHANISM: Sealed, buoyant, satellite-tagged pods with rounded, sledge-like hulls like Nansen's Fram ride up instead of being crushed when ice squeezes them. They are filled at the deposit itself and launched into a north-flowing river such as the Lena, Yana or Indigirka, whose summer current carries them to the delta with no barge or fuel. A boom at the delta gathers the pods. In early winter they are hauled over the fast ice and set on young ice at its outer edge, where offshore winds push the new ice into the Transpolar Drift, which carries it to Fram Strait in 2–3 years. A pod freed by melt floats on and is frozen in again the next winter. Because no leg of the route costs anything per tonne-kilometre, the pods can carry screened run-of-mine ore instead of concentrate, and ships collect them by tag position and take them to a European plant that has the power and reagents to separate the metal.
VALUE: A roadless deposit on any Arctic-draining river can send its ore to a powered plant with no road, barge, icebreaker, fuel or on-site concentrator.

F32
MECHANISM: In early winter, two parallel strips are cleared of snow along the fast main channel of a frozen river, while the snow between and beside them is left as insulation. Ice grows faster under the cleared strips, forming two downward keels with a smooth groove between them on the underside of the ice. Passive, slightly buoyant concentrate pods put in through a slot ride the groove on the current without drifting into slow water near the banks. Ice-thickness readings taken through the ice guide where the strips are cleared again after each snowfall.
VALUE: It turns any large frozen river into a self-built winter conveyor for ore with no fuel, vessels or moving parts.

F33
MECHANISM: Worn rail panels, which mainline track renewals retire every year at near-scrap cost, are laid by a standard track-laying train straight onto frozen tundra compacted with snow, with no embankment. Rivers are crossed on ice thickened by repeated flooding, tamping machines re-level the line each autumn to undo the summer heave, and trains run only in the 5–6 months when the ground is rigid. Because the line carries rail-scale tonnage at rail cost, it hauls raw ore to a mill on the permanent network where grid power and reagents already exist, and the empty trains come back with fuel, so the deposit needs only a pit and a loading ramp. When the deposits it serves are worked out, the same panels are lifted and relaid to the next group of deposits, so the line's capital cost is mostly labour, is reused, and is small enough for deposits shipping a few hundred thousand tonnes a year.
VALUE: Mid-size remote deposits, not only giant ones, get rail-scale haulage of raw ore to grid-powered mills for a small fraction of the cost of an all-season Arctic railway, with no plant on permafrost.

F34
MECHANISM: This is set up beside a trunk line that ends at a gas processing or LNG plant. A small side stream of the line's own gas is partly burned to carbon monoxide and hydrogen. This gas first reduces roasted nickel ore to metal. Then, cooled to about 50 °C at a few bar, it carries the nickel off as nickel tetracarbonyl vapour, while iron, copper and rock stay behind. The vapour is metered into the line at about 0.1 mol%, which stays below its condensation pressure even in permafrost-chilled sections. A little extra carbon monoxide stops it breaking down in compressor stations, and the line runs under the same rules already used for lines carrying toxic sour gas. At the plant, heated beds in the inlet train, where mercury guard beds already sit, split the vapour into pure nickel pellets and carbon monoxide. The carbonyl never leaves the plant fence and no customer ever receives it.
VALUE: A line already carrying about 38 billion m³ of gas a year could also deliver about 100,000 t of refined nickel a year from a deposit along its route. It needs no new transport route and no reagents brought in, and nothing changes for gas customers.

F35
MECHANISM: Over a stripped, ice-rich placer, a spillway holds a shallow pond of the ground's own meltwater. The pond absorbs the round-the-clock summer sun and thaws the gravel beneath it, wind waves lift silt and sand over the spillway, and their pressure pulses jig gold and heavy sand down through the stones onto the frozen floor. Each autumn the pond is set just shallower than the winter ice will grow, so the ice freezes onto the tops of the stones. In spring the spillway is closed, snowmelt lifts the bottom-fast ice, and the wind pushes the floes and their stones onto a dump shore, the way lake ice builds boulder ramparts. The spillway is lowered each year so the thin heavy layer rides down with the thaw front to bedrock, where only that layer is dug and washed.
VALUE: Even in the cobbly gravels typical of Siberian placers, sun, wind and winter ice remove the silt, sand and stones in place, so only a few percent of the deposit is ever dug and washed, with no pumps, washing-plant fuel or reagents.

F36
MECHANISM: At a crude-oil pump station near a gold or platinum-metal deposit, ore is heap-leached all year with a strong calcium chloride brine that stays liquid far below −30 °C. The chlorine and acid the leach needs are made on site by electrolysing a bleed of the same brine with the station's existing power, so only top-up salt is shipped in. A side stream of the line's own crude, dosed with a gold extractant made only of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen such as dibutyl carbitol, is stirred once through the metal-loaded brine and rejoins the line as a density-tracked slug. As a result the site needs no carbon columns, elution, electrowinning, smelter or gold room. At the coastal terminal the slug is cut into its own tank and washed with hot oxalic acid, which drops the gold out as metal powder, and the crude is exported as ordinary crude.
VALUE: A deposit beside a crude pipeline can leach and recover precious metal right through the Siberian winter with only a heap, a mixer and an electrolysis cell on site and only salt brought in.

F37
MECHANISM: An insulated, lined pond holds a salinity gradient made from local Siberian Platform brines. The gradient traps summer insolation in the dense bottom layer at 60–80 °C, and in winter the ice lid adds insulation. In winter an organic Rankine cycle draws heat from the bottom layer and rejects it to −40 °C air. That temperature difference of about 110 K roughly doubles the efficiency of solar-pond plants in temperate climates. Gradient sensors trigger brine injection whenever the non-convecting layer starts to mix.
VALUE: Supplies winter heat and electricity to remote operations from local salt and sunlight, cutting the fuel that has to come in by winter road.

F38
MECHANISM: The overburden is stripped from an ice-rich placer, and a shallow pond of meltwater is kept on top so it absorbs round-the-clock summer sun. Each summer the top layer thaws, and its excess ground ice turns it into a weak, saturated slurry. Gold and other heavy minerals sink through the slurry and settle on the still-frozen floor. Year by year the advancing thaw front carries this heavy layer down to bedrock. Ground-temperature probes track how deep the front has gone, and the pond depth is adjusted to speed it up or slow it down. Then only the thin enriched bottom layer is dug and washed.
VALUE: Sunlight and gravity do most of the excavation and concentration, so a placer is mined by moving a few percent of the gravel that conventional digging moves.

F39
MECHANISM: Diamond pits on the Siberian Platform, such as Udachny, already pump out very salty brines and inject them back underground. These brines are rich in lithium, bromine, strontium and magnesium. In winter they are sprayed through snowmaking nozzles into −40 °C air. Almost pure water freezes out of the spray, and the salts stay behind in the liquid that has not frozen. That liquid drains from the bottom of the ice pile and is pumped to the next spray stage. A few stages reach concentrations that solar evaporation ponds take months to produce. The concentrate then goes to standard lithium and bromine recovery, and in spring the ice pile melts into nearly fresh water.
VALUE: A waste stream that costs money to dispose of becomes a source of lithium and bromine, with winter cold doing the job of an evaporation pond and energy needed only for pumping.

F40
MECHANISM: At a port on the Laptev or East Siberian Sea, bulk cargo is loaded into rounded steel pods with no engines. Their shape makes ice pressure push them upward instead of crushing them, the hull principle of Nansen's ship Fram. In autumn they are towed into the forming pack ice and freeze in. They then ride the natural Transpolar Drift across the Arctic Ocean and reach Fram Strait in about one to two years, where tugs collect them near Svalbard. Every pod carries a satellite beacon, and a drift model updated with each year's ice-motion data picks the release points and dates that give the fastest, safest crossing.
VALUE: Siberian bulk commodities reach the Atlantic with no fuel, icebreaker escort or Northern Sea Route fees; the main cost is a year or two of capital tied up in the cargo and the cheap hulls.

F41
MECHANISM: In autumn, a dense grid of cheap boreholes is drilled into the ore body and flooded with water. Winter air is then blown or naturally drawn through them, so the water freezes and expands by about 9%. Repeated over several freeze-thaw cycles, this splits the rock along its grain boundaries into fine fragments. Temperature and acoustic sensors in the boreholes record where cracking has slowed, and the next year's water injection is concentrated in those zones. In the thaw season the fines are washed or vacuumed out instead of being blasted and crushed.
VALUE: Most of the cost of blasting, explosives transport and crushing disappears, because the Arctic climate breaks the rock for free.

F42
MECHANISM: Frozen, ice-rich ore or wet concentrate is spread in thin layers on pads exposed to the dry, windy −30 °C air of deep winter. Ice turns straight into vapour without melting, at roughly 1 kg/m² a day, and a harrow turns the layers. Moisture probes decide when each strip is dry enough to be swept up and the next layer laid down.
VALUE: It removes 20–50% of the hauled weight and gives dry material ready for dry separation, with no fuel or thermal dryers.

F43
MECHANISM: A dilute leach solution or brine carrying metals or rare earths is sprayed or spread in thin layers over a sloped field during winter. Freezing pushes the dissolved salts out of the pure ice, and the concentrated liquid drains away into collecting channels. The spraying rate and the number of passes are adjusted from conductivity readings at the outlet until the target concentration is reached. The ice left behind is nearly clean water that is reused in spring.
VALUE: Concentrating metals before shipping, normally an energy-hungry step, becomes nearly free, so only a small volume of high-grade liquor has to leave the site.

F44
MECHANISM: Ice-class LNG carriers leave Arctic terminals with full tanks but under their weight limit, so they also carry thousands of tonnes of seawater ballast. Sealed cassettes of briquetted concentrate, brought by barge from deposits on the Yenisei and Ob, take the place of that ballast in double-bottom spaces and are swapped for seawater at the port where the LNG is unloaded. For each voyage, cargo reports and the ship's draft from AIS tracking give the ratio of paid tonnes to available deadweight. That ratio sets how many cassettes the next loading plan assigns, turning ballast space into cargo space.
VALUE: Arctic concentrate rides ships that already sail to Asian and European markets, at almost no extra cost for fuel or icebreakers.

F45
MECHANISM: A long riffled sluice box is hung beneath a slot cut in the ice of a river that keeps flowing all winter. Frozen placer gravel from a nearby deposit is crushed and dropped into the slot. The river's own 0 °C current, kept liquid by the ice lid, washes the gravel. Gold and heavy minerals settle in the riffles, and fine tailings disperse in the low winter flow. The sluice is lifted weekly through the slot to collect the concentrate, and its angle is set from flow speeds measured through the ice.
VALUE: Placer gold can be washed through the whole winter with no pumps, heated plant or tailings ponds.

F46
MECHANISM: Run-of-mine ore goes into 1 t sacks, each with a thin plastic air bladder, and is frozen into a shell of river water in a simple mould. The trapped air plus the ice make each package slightly lighter than water, so the float itself comes free from the winter and nothing has to be shipped back upstream. The packages sit in a slot pool until they warm to 0 °C and are then pushed under the ice of a large river that flows all winter. In 0 °C water the shell cannot melt, and the current carries the packages 10–40 km a day to a net under a slot at a downstream town with power, where an ordinary mill separates the metal and the shells melt away. Packages that snag are freed at breakup and caught by a boom at the same site, and the only consumable is one liner per tonne.
VALUE: Moving whole ore costs almost nothing per tonne and nothing returns upstream, so even ordinary-grade deposits upriver of a powered town need no plant, power, reagents or return freight of their own.

F47
MECHANISM: At the deposit, an acid solution of gold, platinum-group metals, cobalt or rare earths is stirred with diesel-range oil that contains a metal-grabbing reagent, so the metal moves into the oil. The loaded oil goes into the crude trunk line as separate slugs between crude batches, about 2% of throughput, and density meters track each slug. At the coastal terminal each slug is cut into its own tank and washed with strong acid. The acid takes the metal back for plating out, and the cleaned oil is sold as ordinary fuel, so the carrier never has to come back.
VALUE: Remote high-value metals reach a seaport for almost nothing by riding inside an existing oil pipeline. At 1 g/L that is up to about 2,000 t of metal a year.

F48
MECHANISM: Driverless trains of sleds cross frozen tundra and coastal sea ice pulled by large controllable traction kites, like those used to tow ships and on polar expeditions, scaled to tonnes per sled. A small onboard computer steers the kite, picks a route from wind forecasts and ice thickness maps, and anchors the train to wait out calm or unfavourable wind. Each train stores its route log and the ice conditions it meets, and the shared route map improves every season. It has no fuel, road or driver costs, and slow delivery is acceptable for ore stockpiled over the winter.
VALUE: It replaces diesel winter-road trucking with nearly free wind power over the roadless distances where trucking costs the most.

F49
MECHANISM: In early winter, the pay gravel stockpiled in autumn is laid across a creek in layers and sprayed with water, so the dam itself is frozen ore. A peat cover on its crest and upstream face keeps it frozen while spring snowmelt fills the basin behind it. All summer a fixed timber gate lets a steady flow of sun-warmed basin water run down the dam's bare downstream face. This water melts the ice cement at a rate set by the flow, so gravel drops into the water at roughly the right ratio for washing. The mix runs straight down a riffled chute, where cassiterite, wolframite or gold settle and the light gravel goes to a pit. The stored water and its head do both the feeding and the separation, with no loaders, pumps, fuel or reagents.
VALUE: A roadless placer can run a full washing season unattended from one autumn stockpile and one winter's snow, cutting out the summer fuel, pumps and loaders that make up most of its cost.

F50
MECHANISM: In winter, sealed and buoyant pods built to withstand ice pressure are filled with dense concentrate that does not spoil (nickel matte, rare-earth or tin concentrate) and placed on fast ice off the Lena delta. The Transpolar Drift, the current that carried Nansen's Fram, then moves them across the Arctic Basin to Fram Strait in roughly one to three years. Each pod carries a satellite beacon, and a drift model kept updated with beacon tracks and ice forecasts predicts where pods will exit. One ice-class vessel can then collect each season's arrivals in the East Greenland Current. Because the pods float if their floe breaks up, losses are limited to beacon failures and pods crushed in pressure ridges.
VALUE: Moves concentrate from the Siberian shelf to Atlantic markets with no fuel and no icebreaker escort, trading speed for a slow but predictable supply pipeline.

F51
MECHANISM: Pads, embankments, tailings and foundations are covered with polymer radiative-cooling film. It reflects sunlight and radiates heat through the 8-13 µm atmospheric window, so the surface stays below air temperature even in the 24-hour summer sun. Buried thermistors show where the ground is warming, and more film layers or wider coverage go there.
VALUE: Ice-rich ground stays frozen with no power, piles or thermosyphons, so foundations cost far less and stop failing.

F52
MECHANISM: Each winter, pumps on a floodplain next to the mine's river spray water mixed with sawdust from local sawmills into moulds. It freezes layer by layer into pykrete hulls (ice with about 14% wood fibre, several times stronger than plain ice and much slower to melt), each carrying a few thousand tonnes. Concentrate or coal stockpiled over winter is loaded straight in, and the spring flood lifts the hulls off the floodplain and carries them north at 100–200 km/day, with one small pusher boat steering each convoy. At the delta port the cargo is moved to ships and the hull is left to melt; wall thickness is set each year from the forecast water temperature and the route length.
VALUE: Winter cold and the spring flood become a free, throwaway fleet that moves bulk cargo from roadless interiors to Arctic ports with no steel hulls to build and no return trip.

F53
MECHANISM: At freeze-up a plow makes a single pass down the fast main channel of a big frozen river. It heaps the thin early snow into a low berm along that line and leaves the ice bare on either side. The berm insulates the ice beneath it, so through the early winter the bare sides grow downward much faster. This leaves a smooth upward channel in the ice's underside, and later snow, which falls evenly, does not erase it, so no more clearing is needed. Slightly buoyant pods of crushed high-grade ore are put in through a slot. They rise into the channel and ride the current inside it, so they cannot stray into slow water near the banks or catch on rough ice. A slot at the downstream plant or port lifts them out for concentration where power and reagents already exist.
VALUE: After one plow pass, any large frozen river becomes a self-maintaining, fuel-free winter lane that carries ore from a riverside mine to an existing plant, with no road, vessel or processing plant at the mine.

F54
MECHANISM: Sealed, buoyant ore containers with satellite tags are set into Laptev Sea fast ice near the Lena delta. The Transpolar Drift carries the ice and containers to Fram Strait in 2–3 years without fuel or escort. When the floes melt, the containers float in open water and ships pick them up by their tag positions.
VALUE: Bulk ore can reach European waters with no icebreakers, paying only the cost of time.

F55
MECHANISM: Each winter, barge hulls 2–3 m thick are frozen in place at a mine's landing on a north-flowing river (Lena, Vilyuy, Yenisei, Kolyma). Water mixed with about 14% sawmill pulp is sprayed onto a mould layer by layer, and the −40 °C air does the freezing for free. The result, pykrete, is several times tougher than plain ice and melts slowly because a crust of insulating fibre forms on its surface. Ore concentrate or coal is loaded onto the deck during winter. The spring flood floats the hulls, and a few tugs guide convoys through the cold early meltwater to a transfer point in the river delta on the Northern Sea Route. There the cargo is lifted off and the hulls are left to melt. A hull costs only water, sawdust and pump fuel: no steel fleet sits idle, no barge makes an empty trip back upstream, and shallow tributaries are used only while the flood makes them deep.
VALUE: Bulk cargo from deposits with no roads reaches Arctic shipping at almost no cost for the hulls.

F56
MECHANISM: All winter, frozen placer pay gravel is ripped and spread a few centimetres thick over a broad pad of flat, windy tundra next to the cut. The dry −30 °C wind sublimes the pore ice from the top of the layer, freeing up to about a millimetre a day, and because no liquid water ever forms, the freed silt and light quartz and feldspar sand blow off loose into a snow fence. Gold, cassiterite, wolframite and other heavy grains stay behind; a daily harrow exposes fresh frozen layer, and the residue is swept up and dry-screened to remove the gravel. Because the rate is fixed per square metre, the pad is sized by area (about a square kilometre handles a season's pay gravel), and the silt-free residue, a small fraction of the original volume, is stockpiled so that the short summer wash plant finishes the whole year's ore in a few weeks of clear water, with no mud reaching the river.
VALUE: Placer mines gain a second production season in midwinter and finish all their ore in a few clean-water summer weeks, with no water, reagent or silt discharge and almost no fuel for the winter concentration.

F57
MECHANISM: In winter, crews cast barge hulls on the frozen river surface near the mine. The hulls are made of pykrete: water frozen with 10-15% local larch sawdust or pulp, which is several times tougher than plain ice. They are filled with ore concentrate or coal and capped with a sprayed ice-and-sawdust lid, insulated with a layer of sawdust. When the ice breaks up in spring, the flood floats them north to an estuary. GPS beacons report their drift, and a few tugs gather them for transfer to sea ships. The hulls are never brought back: they melt into the river, so the transport fleet costs almost nothing to build and needs no return trip. The hull wall thickness and the cargo load are set each year from flood forecasts and from strength tests of the frozen hulls.
VALUE: Any deposit near a north-flowing river can ship bulk cargo once a year with no road, railway or permanent fleet.

F58
MECHANISM: Rigid spheres about 1 m across, with closed-cell foam shells, sealed holds and no gas, valves or electronics, are trimmed to about 0.5% positive buoyancy and clipped about every 20 m onto a light floating rope, in strings of a hundred or more. A string rides against the smooth underside of a big Siberian river's winter ice and rolls downstream with the current; because the drag on the whole string pulls each sphere along, none can stall in slack water or behind an ice keel long enough to freeze in, and the flow pushes the string down and around ridges and hanging dams. Since these rivers flow north from the railway, strings pushed through a slot at an upstream railhead carry diesel, brought to neutral buoyancy by dense reagents such as acid or lime in a second hold, down to a riverside mine. There they are emptied, refilled with concentrate, re-trimmed and pushed back under the ice to a boom at a downstream port that catches each string by its lead rope, and the empties go back upriver by barge in summer.
VALUE: Through the seven months when nothing else moves on the river, the current under the ice both supplies a riverside mine with fuel and reagents and carries its concentrate to port, without losing cargo to freeze-in and with no vehicles, fuel or tending.

F59
MECHANISM: Strings of sledges on low-friction plastic runners carry concentrate, coal or equipment across frozen tundra, rivers and coastal sea ice. Cold, dense air at −30 °C and the kites' figure-eight flight produce tens of kilonewtons of pull from the ordinary 6–8 m/s winter winds of Yamal and Taimyr, enough for tens of tonnes per kite. F60
MECHANISM: Pods are made on the spot at a slot cut in the main-channel ice of a big north-flowing river. Crushed high-grade ore and enough dry snow to float it (roughly a cubic metre of snow per tonne of ore) are packed into a mould and sprayed with river water. The −40 °C air freezes this overnight into a sealed ice shell. The pods float up against the smooth underside of the ice and ride the current, out of reach of wind and waves, to a boom and recovery slot at a plant or year-round port downstream. There they are lifted out and cracked open, and the ice is thrown away. The 0 °C river cannot melt the pods, they cost nothing and they never go back upstream, so throughput is limited only by how fast the two slots work. Whole ore can therefore travel, and separation stays at an existing concentrator.

F61

Casings are recovered and sent back on the seasonal supply route.

F63
MECHANISM: Wells are drilled into a frozen sulfide orebody and warm acidic lixiviant is circulated, thawing only a bounded pocket while the surrounding ice-filled permafrost acts as a natural impermeable freeze wall that stops solution escaping. Sulfide oxidation is strongly exothermic, so once started the thawed zone heats itself; temperature and conductivity readings from monitoring wells set injection rates and oxidant dosing to grow the zone along the orebody and halt it at its edge. Metal is recovered on site as cathode or loaded resin, and at closure the zone refreezes, permanently locking residual solution in place.

F65
MECHANISM: A gently sloping pyritic copper-sulfide outcrop is blasted once to rubble in place, tens of metres deep. Each spring its surface is covered with black geotextile that lets air and water through and soaks up the round-the-clock summer sun, and each autumn it is fenced so that drifting snow buries it under an insulating blanket. Once the rubble is warm, bacteria oxidise its own pyrite, which makes sulfuric acid and heat, and the trapped heat thaws the rubble a little deeper every year; the warm column also acts as a chimney, drawing air in at the downslope toe and out through the fabric, so oxygen reaches the whole thawing bowl with no fans. VALUE: A whole shallow sulfide orebody, not just its summer thaw layer, leaches itself in place over the years, using sun, snow and its own pyrite for heat, water, acid and air flow, and ships out only cement copper, with no plant, power line or acid supply.
