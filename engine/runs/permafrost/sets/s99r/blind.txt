R01
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: a pond over the placer lets winter bottom-fast ice freeze onto the stones, and the spring water rise floats the ice off with the stones in it. The in-place sorting part fails by orders of magnitude. Pond waves give a bottom orbital velocity of only 0.03 m/s at 1 m depth, and the pore flow they pump is 6×10⁻⁴ m/s against the 0.02-0.1 m/s needed to fluidise (jig) the gravel. Fines leaving over the spillway would take 2,000-11,000 years, so that part is dropped. The repaired design times the pond depth so the ice (natural 1.6-1.8 m, or 3.1-3.3 m when built up by flooding the ice surface) reaches the boulder tops only in March, while the summer-thawed talik below stays unfrozen. Each m² of floe then lifts 0.08-0.17 m³ of stone a year, and a tractor winch, not the wind, drags the floes to a dump bay. The pond's 1.4-2.2 m of summer thaw prepares the ground.
VALUE: Clearing boulders by ice costs about $1/m³ of boulder against about $6/m³ to drill, blast and doze them (roughly 6x cheaper), which opens bouldery placers that dredges reject; overall washing is only 1.25-1.5x cheaper, so this is a niche.

R02
MECHANISM: A dilute leach solution or brine carrying metals or rare earths is sprayed or spread in thin layers over a sloped field during winter. Freezing pushes the dissolved salts out of the pure ice, and the concentrated liquid drains away into collecting channels. The spraying rate and the number of passes are adjusted from conductivity readings at the outlet until the target concentration is reached. The ice left behind is nearly clean water that is reused in spring.
VALUE: Concentrating metals before shipping, normally an energy-hungry step, becomes nearly free, so only a small volume of high-grade liquor has to leave the site.

R03
MECHANISM: Dilute brines, heap-leach liquors or mine water are sprayed through snow-making nozzles into -30 to -50°C air over lined pads, building cones of nearly salt-free ice while rejected brine drains to a sump. The sump liquor is re-sprayed in cycles until lithium, potassium or metal content reaches target, and in spring the salty first meltwater is collected separately. Spray rate tracks air temperature so freezing stays complete.
VALUE: Gives Siberia a free equivalent of desert evaporation ponds, so brine minerals and leach metals are concentrated on site before shipping.

R04
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: the frozen ore pile feeds itself. Warm water thaws its face at a rate set by flow × water temperature, so the feed needs no dozer. Two changes from the original: the 1-2 mm film spread over a 40-100 m face is replaced by a channel along the toe of the face, and the ore pile no longer holds back water. In the toe channel, 47-100 L/s at a slope of 0.05-0.08 gives 30-57 Pa of shear, which clears clasts finer than 42-78 mm. The water gives up its heat within e-folding lengths of 10-31 m, so feed still equals flow × heat. Water comes by gravity from a lined solar pond, 0.4 m deep and 0.7-1.5 ha, that warms the creek by 4-6 K. Season output is about 123,000 m³ with the pond, against 54,500 m³ from a 4 °C creek. A fixed weir bypass keeps the chute at 4 m³ of water per m³ or more, while the face alone swings from 2.4 to 12.1. A grizzly and a loader (2-3 h a day) remove the oversize.
VALUE: Feed dozing, ripping and pumping drop out of the washing stage at winter-mined placers. Per recovered gram this is only 1.03-1.13x cheaper than today's stockpile thawing and dozer feed.

R05
MECHANISM: Anchor: the frozen ore pile itself holds the water, and stored warm water run over its bare downstream face melts the ice cement at a rate set by the flow, so the dam doses its own gravel into a riffled chute. Calculation confirms the self-dosing: releasing 1 m³ of ice-cemented gravel takes 101 MJ, which is 4.3-7.6 m³ of 8 °C water, inside the normal sluicing range (a working placer uses 2.2 m³ per m³). The face can transfer heat 20-100x faster than needed, so feed really does equal flow times water temperature. The repair splits the structure into two parts. One is a permanent frozen core under 0.8-1.0 m of saturated peat (it thaws only 0.5-0.6 m per summer). The other is a sacrificial ore buttress downstream, 75 m thick and retreating 0.8 m a day. Creek baseflow (about 47 L/s for 60,000 m³ a season) passes through a small forebay instead of a season-long reservoir, which cuts the dam-break peak from 460 to 128 m³/s. It is used where it is strongest: winter (underground) placer mining, where the hoist already builds a frozen stockpile that today has to be thawed with steam or sprinkling, ripped and fed by dozer.
VALUE: For winter-mined placers the summer washing stage drops from about $6.7 to about $1.3 per m³ (5x cheaper), with no feed dozers, pumps or ripping; for summer-mined gravel built up in autumn the gain is only 2.2x.

R06
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: falling grains charge themselves on the chute lining, and the bins that collect them, wired across to the opposite plates, drive the separating field with no power supply, like a Kelvin water dropper. Many stages are stacked down a slope. Grains charged to 5% of the physical maximum (about 10 µC/kg at 300 µm) drift 30-60 cm sideways while falling between 1.5 m plates. A 1 m wide stage self-charges to 45 kV in about 1.4 s, and it does so even with 10^10 Ω of leakage. A passive corona regulator replaces the spark gap. The repair concerns humidity. Grains at -40 °C air temperature sit at about 90% relative humidity, so the original "dry cold air" claim was wrong. The cascade is therefore enclosed and ventilated with outdoor -40 °C air warmed by the grinding mill's heat (about 690 m³ per tonne of ore). At 0 °C that air is at 1.9% relative humidity, the same dryness as preheating the feed to about 85 °C in an ordinary plant. With middlings recycled, 9 stages that each send 85% of the valuable mineral and 45% of the waste forward lift spodumene ore from 1.2% to 6.1% Li2O at 82% recovery. The circulating load is 7.2x the feed and the total height is about 27 m.
VALUE: About $27/t ore including haul of the 16% mass concentrate, against $105/t to haul raw ore 700 km by winter road (3.9x cheaper), but only about 1.2-1.6x cheaper than an Arctic flotation plant plus haul.

R07
MECHANISM: The anchor (night-sky cooling that drives CaCl2 brine below its -50 °C eutectic) cannot be rescued. Even with a heat pump rejecting to -40/-45 °C air, it needs 5.5-15 MWh per t LCE and must freeze 450-910 t of brine per t LCE, and the leftover liquor is still about 30% CaCl2, so DLE is still needed. Above the eutectic, cold concentrates lithium only 1.25x. The surviving niche keeps only "Arctic cold concentrates lithium" and applies it to Ca-free sorbent eluate. Reverse osmosis first takes the eluate to 6 g/L Li (37 bar osmotic pressure). Two stages of spray freezing (snowmaking guns rather than trays) then take it to 20.6 g/L by draining the unfrozen brine and collecting the early melt. That moves 53% of the Li per pass, needs 62 t of ice per t LCE, and gives a product that freezes at -14 °C. Late melt at ≤1.1 g/L Li goes back to the RO feed, so no lithium is lost in the ice, and winter-only freezing needs 0.37 Mm³ of stored RO concentrate, not 2.2-7.3 Mm³.
VALUE: $110-240/t LCE against $470-1,085/t LCE for the MVR evaporator it replaces (2-4x cheaper for that step), but that is only 5-15% off the $3.4-6.2k/t LCE whole route, and the night-sky/eutectic anchor is not used.

R08
MECHANISM: In autumn, a dense grid of cheap boreholes is drilled into the ore body and flooded with water. Winter air is then blown or naturally drawn through them, so the water freezes and expands by about 9%. Repeated over several freeze-thaw cycles, this splits the rock along its grain boundaries into fine fragments. Temperature and acoustic sensors in the boreholes record where cracking has slowed, and the next year's water injection is concentrated in those zones. In the thaw season the fines are washed or vacuumed out instead of being blasted and crushed.
VALUE: Most of the cost of blasting, explosives transport and crushing disappears, because the Arctic climate breaks the rock for free.

R09
MECHANISM: Driverless trains of sleds cross frozen tundra and coastal sea ice pulled by large controllable traction kites, like those used to tow ships and on polar expeditions, scaled to tonnes per sled. A small onboard computer steers the kite, picks a route from wind forecasts and ice thickness maps, and anchors the train to wait out calm or unfavourable wind. Each train stores its route log and the ice conditions it meets, and the shared route map improves every season. It has no fuel, road or driver costs, and slow delivery is acceptable for ore stockpiled over the winter.
VALUE: It replaces diesel winter-road trucking with nearly free wind power over the roadless distances where trucking costs the most.

R10
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: freezing the stored copper leach liquor runs a Rankine engine that rejects heat to Arctic air, and the same freezing crystallises CuSO4·5H2O. Repairs: (1) The direct-contact refrigerant changes from propane to n-butane. Propane boiling at -6 to -8 °C (3.5-3.9 bar) sits inside the propane-hydrate field. (2) Fan power is now counted: 8.7 kJ per kg of ice against 15.2 gross at -40 °C. The fans are replaced by a natural-draft dry tower. (3) Integrated over a real Yakutsk November-March winter, the engine nets 7.6 kJ per kg of ice. That covers 50% of electrowinning power at 2.5 g/L, not 80-104%. (4) Freezing costs $1.5-2.5 per m³ of feed, so dilute liquor loses. The process is moved to 10-15 g/L liquor from leach-solution recycle on siliceous low-Mg oxide ore (Mg ≤ 2 g/L, Fe ≤ 1 g/L, since impurities concentrate 24x). There it needs 77-117 kg of ice per kg Cu, and the engine supplies 8-12% of electrowinning power.
VALUE: $1,222-1,379/t Cu against $1,590/t Cu for diesel-powered solvent extraction plus electrowinning (1.15-1.3x cheaper), but it loses to grid-powered SX-EW ($1,086/t). NICHE.

R11
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: crushed ore plus snow sealed in an ice shell, carried by the current under winter river ice with no vessel and no return trip. The near-neutral +3 to +9 kg trim fails by orders of magnitude. Shell melt alone (the idea's own 6 cm) costs -196 kg of buoyancy. Snow at 250-350 kg/m³ creeps under hydrostatic load and loses 95-5,300 kg of buoyancy per day (Vionnet 2012 viscosity), and the trapped air gives 521 kg per metre of depth (a Cartesian-diver instability). The repair: a 1.6 m diameter rolling "log", snow pre-compacted to 550 kg/m³ (0.74 m³/t for coal, 1.6 for rock ore), sealed at -35 °C so it self-pressurises to +14.9 kPa, with a +750 kg margin. Creep then costs only 0.6-1.4% (about 100 kg) per 45 days. But rolling needs a mean under-ice velocity of 0.20-0.41 m/s, and getting past ice keels of 5-20 cm needs 0.62-0.95 m/s. Measured under-ice velocities are 0.05-0.25 m/s (Lena delta), and tailwater at +0.5 to +2 °C below dams melts the shell in 0.3-1.1 days.
VALUE: If it ran, pods would cost about $6.8/t for coal against about $124/t by winter road and $27-33/t by summer barge plus stockpiling (4-5x cheaper than the barge), but no surveyed reach has the required velocity, so the ratio is moot.

R12
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: in spring, cold dry wind freeze-dries frozen pay gravel by sublimation and blows away the freed fines without any liquid water, leaving the heavies behind as a lag. An hourly energy-balance model for Pevek shows the claimed 0.4-0.7 mm w.e./day holds for only about 2 weeks. A dark surface begins melting around Apr 28 (7-24 mm/day of melt in May). The repair is to steer the albedo: keep the surface dark while it is cold, then leave the raked, lighter lag (albedo 0.3-0.55) once the surface nears 0 °C. This keeps the surface below 0 °C and gives 13 mm w.e. per season (48 days), times 0.6 for the dry-lag penalty (1 mm of dry grains halves the rate). That frees only 58-109 mm of gravel per year, depending on pore ice of 8-15%, so 100,000 m³/yr needs 0.9-1.4 km² of wind-scoured bench or dump surface. The site also needs a blade pass after each snowfall (spring snowfall of about 10 mm w.e. equals the whole budget), 0.6-1.4 m snow fences, and a low saltation-trap lip downwind. The trap is needed because gold flakes start moving at u* 0.23-0.32 m/s, the same as sand, although gold never goes into suspension (Stokes settling 0.6-6.6 m/s).
VALUE: About $3.7-4.5 per m³ processed (about $8-9 per g recovered at 0.6 g/m³) against $6.0/m³ (about $12.5/g) for bulldozer-sluice washing, so 1.3-1.6x cheaper, not 3.6x; on old tailings it is only about 1x.

R13
MECHANISM: Anchor tested as stated: trays under film that radiates through the 8-13 µm atmospheric window are pushed below the CaCl2-ice eutectic (about -50 °C), so CaCl2 hexahydrate and ice lock up together and LiCl stays liquid. At -50 °C that band carries only 30 W/m² (21% of blackbody). With no brine load at all the film settles only 7.8 K below -40 °C air. At -52 °C it removes no net heat unless the air is already -45 °C or colder. At Udachny (January mean -39.4 °C) that gives about 273 usable hours, which freeze about 55 kg of brine per m² each winter. That means 11,600 m² of trays per tonne of lithium carbonate equivalent (LCE) per year, about $55,000/t LCE. Above the eutectic, ambient cold concentrates Li only 1.0-1.55x, so the anchor cannot be rescued at scale. The only workable niche drops the calcium part of the anchor: the same trays freeze sorbent eluate that is free of calcium (LiCl only, eutectic -75 °C) with plain -40 °C air. That concentrates it about 30x using roughly 100 m² per t LCE/yr, about $500/t LCE.
VALUE: As stated the idea is about 10x more expensive than the whole sorption route ($3.4-6.2k/t LCE), so it fails; only the eluate-freezing niche, which does not use the eutectic, is cheaper than the evaporation step it would replace.

R14
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: pond ice freezes onto stones and the spring rise floats the ice off with the stones in it. The design now uses a gap: water depth is set so the ice bottom stays at least 0.3 m above the mined pit floor in 99% of winters. With the snow ploughed off, ice is 1.8 ± 0.08 m and the depth is 2.12 m. The floor never freezes and no gold-bearing matrix is caught. Only oversize (over 1.2 m) left standing on the floor after the gravel is mined is gripped, with P ≈ 1.00. Ploughed spring ice gives 101-126 kg/m² of net lift, so a 1.5 m boulder needs 21-31 m² of floe. Adfreeze grip margins are 1.6-4.9x for 1.5 m boulders but only 0.6-1.8x for 2.5 m boulders, which caps the method at about 2 m. The pond sits below grade in the mined-out pit, so there is no dam, and floes are winched from shore.
VALUE: Clearing oversize by ice costs about $2-2.5/m³ of boulder against $4-8/m³ for secondary blasting, about 2-3x cheaper for the oversize alone. That is only 0.04-2.5% of the cost per recovered gram, so it is a niche.

R15
MECHANISM: At a port on the Laptev or East Siberian Sea, bulk cargo is loaded into rounded steel pods with no engines. Their shape makes ice pressure push them upward instead of crushing them, the hull principle of Nansen's ship Fram. In autumn they are towed into the forming pack ice and freeze in. They then ride the natural Transpolar Drift across the Arctic Ocean and reach Fram Strait in about one to two years, where tugs collect them near Svalbard. Every pod carries a satellite beacon, and a drift model updated with each year's ice-motion data picks the release points and dates that give the fastest, safest crossing.
VALUE: Siberian bulk commodities reach the Atlantic with no fuel, icebreaker escort or Northern Sea Route fees; the main cost is a year or two of capital tied up in the cargo and the cheap hulls.

R16
MECHANISM: Anchor: the cold, dry wind freeze-dries frozen pay gravel by sublimation and blows away the freed silt and light sand without any liquid water, leaving gold, cassiterite and other heavies behind as a lag. In midwinter Yakutia this runs at only 0.014-0.041 mm w.e./day, which frees 0.05-0.15 mm/day of gravel, so the design moves to the windy Arctic tundra in spring (Mar 15 to about May 10), when sun falling on dark, harrowed, still-frozen gravel drives 0.4-0.7 mm w.e./day, freeing 2.5-5 mm/day. Pay gravel is worked in place, either on the stripped cut whose active layer was drained by ditches in autumn (pore ice 0.15 instead of 0.30) or on old tailings dumps, which need no stripping at all. That removes 0.21 m per year, so 100,000 m³ a year needs about 0.5 km². A daily harrow-and-rake pass windrows the lag. Harrowing is done only when the 10 m wind is 7-8 m/s or less: at 7 m/s quartz from 25 to 500 µm leaves and no gold moves. The dry -10 mm residue is about 25% of the volume and is washed clean in a few summer weeks.
VALUE: In place on thin-overburden placers or old tailings, digging, feeding and washing cost about $1.7/m³ against about $6.0/m³ for summer washing today (3.6x cheaper); on a pad for thick virgin pay the gain is only 2.1x.

R17
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: grains charged on the chute lining build the separating field themselves, through bins cross-wired to the opposite plates, with no power supply. The stated 30 kV/m gives only 4-9 cm of drift (not 30-60) and is space-charge-limited to 0.15 t/h per metre, which would need 7 km of plates. Repaired design: - A 0.3 m gap self-excites to 90 kV (300 kV/m, 8% of breakdown at -40 °C). That gives 41 cm of drift over a 1.5 m fall and 1.47 t/h per metre, or about 700 m of plates in total for 1 Mt/yr. - Insulating grains cannot hand over charge the way water drops do in a Kelvin dropper. Instead, discharges inside the charged grain pile pass about 98% of it to the bin. Leakage must stay above 2.2e10 Ω per metre. - Intake air must be heated to 20-40 °C, not 0 °C, to dry ore with 1-2% moisture: 137 MJ/t against 43-54 MJ/t of mill heat.
VALUE: $3,994/t Li2O delivered against $4,588 for dense-media separation plus haul (the best baseline, 1.15x) and $6,292 for Arctic flotation (1.6x). NICHE.

R18
MECHANISM: All winter, water is pumped in thin layers over a log-and-geotextile mat in a backwater beside the mine, freezing in -40°C air into a reinforced ice raft about three metres thick on which the mine stockpiles concentrate or coal. Before spring the raft is blanketed with sawdust, which preserves ice for months; at flood stage, after the ice run, the backwater connects to the river and a small tug steers the raft downstream to a railhead or trunk-river port. The cargo is unloaded and the raft left to melt, so there is no hull, shipyard or return voyage; each season's raft area is sized from measured ice thickness and planned tonnage.
VALUE: Mines on tributaries too shallow for barges can ship over a thousand tonnes per raft for little more than the cost of winter pumping.

R19
MECHANISM: Instead of blasting, drill a grid of holes into the frozen orebody, fill them with water and seal each hole's top with a packer. The surrounding permafrost is below 0 °C, so each water column freezes from the wall inward. This traps the liquid core and pressurises it to tens of MPa, well above rock tensile strength (5–15 MPa): the burst-pipe effect at bench scale. Acoustic sensors log cracking in each hole, and software refills holes that cracked too little and adjusts hole spacing for the next bench.
VALUE: Removes explosives from remote mines, along with the airlift, magazines and permits they need, cutting rock-breaking cost several-fold and allowing vibration-free mining next to settlements and infrastructure.

R20
MECHANISM: Each winter, barge hulls 2–3 m thick are frozen in place at a mine's landing on a north-flowing river (Lena, Vilyuy, Yenisei, Kolyma). Water mixed with about 14% sawmill pulp is sprayed onto a mould layer by layer, and the −40 °C air does the freezing for free. The result, pykrete, is several times tougher than plain ice and melts slowly because a crust of insulating fibre forms on its surface. Ore concentrate or coal is loaded onto the deck during winter. The spring flood floats the hulls, and a few tugs guide convoys through the cold early meltwater to a transfer point in the river delta on the Northern Sea Route. There the cargo is lifted off and the hulls are left to melt. A hull costs only water, sawdust and pump fuel: no steel fleet sits idle, no barge makes an empty trip back upstream, and shallow tributaries are used only while the flood makes them deep.
VALUE: Bulk cargo from deposits with no roads reaches Arctic shipping at almost no cost for the hulls.

R21
MECHANISM: In winter, metal leach liquors or the Siberian Platform's lithium-bearing calcium-chloride brines are pumped into shallow lined ponds. Air at -40 to -55°C freezes pure ice out of them, and skimming it off concentrates the liquid without burning fuel for evaporation. As the pond cools past successive eutectic points, salts such as mirabilite, hydrohalite and magnesium chloride hydrates crystallise one after another and are harvested, leaving a liquor ever richer in the target metal. Temperature and density sensors time each harvest and decide when to move liquor to a colder pond. The skimmed ice is stockpiled as clean process water for summer.
VALUE: Produces a refined, high-value product at a remote site using free cold, so only a small fraction of the original tonnage has to be transported.

R22
MECHANISM: Anchor tested: trays radiating through the 8-13 µm window push Ca-Li brine below the CaCl2-ice eutectic (-49.8 °C) so Ca locks up and LiCl stays liquid. My own band model agrees with the original: - With no brine load the film settles 8.1 K below -40 °C air. - A film at -52 °C cools only when the air is at -45 °C or colder. That is 288 hours a winter, freezing 36 kg per m². - So the trays need 13,000-35,600 m² per t LCE per year (Udachnaya brine averages 147 mg/L Li). R23
MECHANISM: Broken ore is stacked in insulated heaps laced with pipes and saturated with water. Fans then alternate −40°C winter air with warm water heated by waste heat from stranded gas or a generator. This runs many freeze-thaw cycles per winter instead of one per year. Water expands about 9% when it freezes, which forces cracks open along grain boundaries and shatters the rock to fine sizes. Temperature and acoustic sensors in the heap detect when cracking stops, and the cycle timing is adjusted to match.
VALUE: Most of the crushing and grinding energy, the largest energy cost in hard-rock mining, is replaced by free Arctic cold, so remote deposits can be heap-leached or concentrated on site.

R24
MECHANISM: Wells are drilled into a frozen sulfide orebody and warm acidic lixiviant is circulated, thawing only a bounded pocket while the surrounding ice-filled permafrost acts as a natural impermeable freeze wall that stops solution escaping. Sulfide oxidation is strongly exothermic, so once started the thawed zone heats itself; temperature and conductivity readings from monitoring wells set injection rates and oxidant dosing to grow the zone along the orebody and halt it at its edge. Metal is recovered on site as cathode or loaded resin, and at closure the zone refreezes, permanently locking residual solution in place.
VALUE: Low-grade frozen copper, nickel and gold sulfide deposits become mineable without pits, mills, tailings dams or haul roads, shipping only finished metal.

R25
MECHANISM: Concentrate is sealed in polyethylene sacks with an air pocket, then repeatedly wetted and frozen in -40°C air to build a smooth ice shell, giving spheres about 1.5 m across that carry roughly a tonne each and are just slightly buoyant. Through the winter they are slipped through a hole into a large river and roll along the underside of the ice cover with the current, in 0°C water that barely melts or thickens them, covering 30-50 km a day without fuel or vehicles. Downstream, an angled under-ice boom funnels them to a heated slot where a conveyor lifts them out; acoustic tags on a sample of spheres report transit times, and shell thickness is retuned whenever spheres stall or ground.
VALUE: Frozen rivers become free conveyors for the seven months when barges are laid up, moving mine output to the nearest railhead.

R26
MECHANISM: In winter, sealed and buoyant pods built to withstand ice pressure are filled with dense concentrate that does not spoil (nickel matte, rare-earth or tin concentrate) and placed on fast ice off the Lena delta. The Transpolar Drift, the current that carried Nansen's Fram, then moves them across the Arctic Basin to Fram Strait in roughly one to three years. Each pod carries a satellite beacon, and a drift model kept updated with beacon tracks and ice forecasts predicts where pods will exit. One ice-class vessel can then collect each season's arrivals in the East Greenland Current. Because the pods float if their floe breaks up, losses are limited to beacon failures and pods crushed in pressure ridges.
VALUE: Moves concentrate from the Siberian shelf to Atlantic markets with no fuel and no icebreaker escort, trading speed for a slow but predictable supply pipeline.

R27
MECHANISM: Sealed plastic capsules of ore concentrate are lowered through an ice slot near the mine and carried north for months by the current still flowing under frozen Siberian rivers. Each trails a heavy guide chain: if it sinks, more chain rests on the riverbed and it gets lighter, so it holds height between bed and ice unpowered. A net hung from the ice downstream funnels capsules to a cut hole, acoustic tags track each one, and the port stockpiles them for summer shipping.
VALUE: Turns the eight frozen months, when rivers carry nothing, into fuel-free freight far cheaper than winter-road trucking.

R28
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: nickel goes to market as Ni(CO)4 vapour carried in a trunk gas line's own flow, and is split back to nickel pellets at the gas plant where the line ends. The repair caps the dose at 0.04 mol%, not 0.1 mol%. At 9.8 MPa a 0.1% dose condenses below about -13 °C, while 0.04% stays at 70% of saturation at -20 °C. That still carries about 37 kt Ni a year on a 38 bcm line. The carbonyl is pumped in as a liquid (3.4 kg/s), the way methanol is injected now, together with 0.15 mol% CO, which keeps it thermodynamically stable up to 80 °C in compressor stations. At the gas plant a recuperated decomposer at 180-200 °C splits out 99.97% of it. It burns 0.12% of the line's gas and costs about $440/t Ni. Carbon guard beds are dropped because activated carbon is poor at holding Ni(CO)4. The best feed is a sulfur-free Ni-Fe material such as awaruite or a pre-reduced calcine, because then there is no SO2 to handle. Injection should be as close to the plant as possible, so the toxic section of line stays short.
VALUE: Moving the nickel costs about $7/t Ni in pipeline share against $1,067/t Ni by truck and rail (about 150x cheaper), but the whole chain is only 1.0-1.8x cheaper than selling concentrate for sulfide feed and 2.0-2.35x for awaruite-type feed.

R29
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: nickel travels as Ni(CO)4 vapour (0.04 mol%) inside a trunk gas line's own flow and is split back to metal where the line ends. Injection now goes in only downstream of the last local offtake. Before any planned vent, injection stops for one transit time (10 h per 300 km, 72 h for the full 2,159 km at 8.3 m/s) and Gazprom's mobile compressor units pump the section down, so planned vents release almost no carbonyl. Compressor discharge is capped at 50 °C, which cuts the CO stabiliser from 0.15% to 0.05% (equilibrium needs 0.032%), so CO use falls from 3.70 to 2.51 t per t Ni, against a hard minimum of 1.91. A 250 °C nickel-bed polisher after the decomposer leaves 0.09 ppb Ni(CO)4 in the sales gas at equilibrium (4.5 ppb at 200 °C). Unsolved: a rupture still releases about 12 t Ni(CO)4 per 30 km section, which is 8.8e8 m³ of air at the IDLH. Its 40-60 s half-life in air means only 0.3-2% survives 1 km downwind, but CO in the plume can slow the breakdown to minutes.
VALUE: The pipeline-specific cost is $109-947/t Ni (one-way CO plus lost line capacity, minus the CO's value as fuel), against about $100-150/t Ni to ship pellets or matte, so it loses to best practice unless CO is a free by-product costing under about $56/t; it beats only trucking concentrate ($1,067/t Ni), by 0.8-1.9x.

R30
MECHANISM: In winter, crews at the mine's riverbank spray water with about 14% sawdust into hollow barge moulds cut in shore ice, freezing thick hulls around sealed concentrate containers. After the spring ice run, the flood wave floats them free; one light tug pushes a convoy to the river mouth, where cargo moves to sea ships and hulls are broken up to melt. The sawdust's insulating skin slows melting enough for a two-to-four-week trip, and wall thickness is set from forecast water temperature and voyage length.
VALUE: Lets mines on tributaries navigable only at flood peak ship bulk cargo with no steel fleet or return voyages.

R31
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: nickel travels as Ni(CO)4 vapour in the Power of Siberia line's own gas flow (9.8 MPa, 38 bcm/yr) and is split back out at the Amur gas plant where the line ends. Repaired design: 0.027 mol% carries 25 kt Ni/yr, which is Kun-Manie scale. That dose is at a saturation ratio of 0.25 at -10 °C. Compressor discharge is capped at 45 °C, so only 0.035% CO is needed to keep the carbonyl stable (the original 0.15% was sized for 80 °C). At the plant, a cold-oil absorber (20-60 kg/s of oil, 10-16 stages) catches 99.999% and leaves 2.7 ppb in sales gas, instead of heating the whole 879 kg/s stream (423 MW gross). The concentrated carbonyl is then decomposed at 200 °C. Propane hydrate does not apply here; the binding cost is CO make-up of 2.53 t CO per t Ni (about $514/t Ni).
VALUE: The physics works, but the pipeline leg costs about $960/t Ni more than running the same carbonyl refinery at the BAM/PoS junction and shipping pellets by rail, which saves only $27/t Ni of freight (about 0.03x). The whole chain at $6.6-8.4k/t Ni is no cheaper than selling concentrate at 70-75% payability ($5.4-6.1k/t Ni lost), so it FAILS on cost.

R32
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: sun and wind sublimate the ice cement of frozen pay with no liquid water, and wind carries off the freed fines while gold stays. In the repaired design, drained pay is piled in autumn into windrows with 37° south faces (~0.06 pore ice instead of 0.15-0.30). The faces shed snow, clear themselves of loose grains, and get 2.6x the irradiance of flat ground. Sorting no longer relies on a threshold window. The saltating sand and any mobile gold (up to ~100-200 µm) settle in the trough between ridges. Only the suspended fraction leaves the site: quartz finer than 63-97 µm and gold finer than 23-35 µm. An hourly energy balance for Tiksi gives a face sublimation of 0.08-0.13 kg/m²/day in March and 0.41-0.67 in April 1-20. The window closes on Apr 20 because faces reach -2 °C or warmer after that. A 0.5 mm dry skin halves the rate and a 3 mm lag cuts it 6x. The season totals 5.2 kg/m², so faces retreat only 0.02-0.09 m.
VALUE: 100,000 m³/yr needs about 0.9 km² raked daily or 213 km of ridges, at $4.4-12.1/m³ ($7.4-20.3/g) against $1.7-6.0/m³ ($2.9-10.1/g) for conventional washing. That is about 0.5x, so it costs more, and only a small niche at waterless sites remains.

R33
MECHANISM: Sprinkler rigs build a narrow, graded ice trough along a fixed line from a deposit to a railhead or river. They refresh it automatically whenever the temperature drops enough. Steel-runner ore sleds clip onto an endless steel cable loop, like a ski lift, driven by motors at the two ends. Because runners on ice have friction close to rail, a single loop moves a continuous stream of cargo without trucks, drivers or road surface. Sensors on the track trigger re-spraying wherever the ice wears down.
VALUE: Gives near-railway freight costs every winter for the price of a cable, two winches and a water pump.

R34
MECHANISM: Strings of sledges on low-friction plastic runners carry concentrate, coal or equipment across frozen tundra, rivers and coastal sea ice. They are pulled by automated traction kites of 100–300 m² flying figure-eights, using the same control method already proven for kites that tow ships. Cold, dense air at −30 °C and the kites' figure-eight flight produce tens of kilonewtons of pull from the ordinary 6–8 m/s winter winds of Yamal and Taimyr, enough for tens of tonnes per kite. A small control unit plans routes from wind forecasts and satellite ice maps and can sail up to about 60° toward the wind. It anchors the train when the wind drops and follows GPS-marked snow corridors, so no graded road is needed. One remote operator supervises dozens of trains.
VALUE: Deposits on the tundra and coasts of Yamal, Taimyr and Chukotka get freight with no fuel and no roads, at a fraction of what diesel trucks cost on winter roads.

R35
MECHANISM: In winter, crews cast barge hulls on the frozen river surface near the mine. The hulls are made of pykrete: water frozen with 10-15% local larch sawdust or pulp, which is several times tougher than plain ice. They are filled with ore concentrate or coal and capped with a sprayed ice-and-sawdust lid, insulated with a layer of sawdust. When the ice breaks up in spring, the flood floats them north to an estuary. GPS beacons report their drift, and a few tugs gather them for transfer to sea ships. The hulls are never brought back: they melt into the river, so the transport fleet costs almost nothing to build and needs no return trip. The hull wall thickness and the cargo load are set each year from flood forecasts and from strength tests of the frozen hulls.
VALUE: Any deposit near a north-flowing river can ship bulk cargo once a year with no road, railway or permanent fleet.

R36
MECHANISM: Anchor: crushed ore plus dry snow sealed in an ice shell, carried by the current under the winter river ice to a downstream slot, with no vessel and no return trip. Snow needed per tonne is 0.28 m³ for coal, 0.74 for rock ore and 0.86 for sulphide ore. The original strongly buoyant pods fail: pressed against ice near 0 °C (μ about 0.5 at slow speed), a 1 t pod at 0.1-0.3 m/s can carry only 1-12 kg of net buoyancy before it stalls, and a stalled pod freezes into the ice cover at 1-2 cm/day. The repair uses 10 t broadside "fin" pods (5.0 × 2.2 × 1.2 m), soaked to 0 °C and trimmed in the launch slot with a crane load cell to +3 to +9 kg net buoyancy. That keeps drag (about 170 N at 0.16 m/s) larger than buoyancy, so pods dive under ice roughness instead of sticking. Routes are limited to reaches with smooth thermal ice and under-ice velocity of at least 0.2 m/s, mainly regulated rivers below hydropower dams (Kolyma, Vilyuy, Yenisei). An 8-10 cm shell survives transit (6 cm melt in 45 days), 700 km takes 41-68 days, and a pair of slots moves 1,200 t/day, or 144 kt per season.
VALUE: Handling costs about $7.3/t including 5% lost pods, against $59-176/t by winter road over 500-1,500 km (8-24x cheaper) and 1.7-5x cheaper than summer barges, with coal as the best cargo.

R37
MECHANISM: In winter, crews repeatedly flood thin layers of river water mixed with wood-chip waste onto riverbank ice. This builds pykrete slabs about 100x30x5 m, each able to float roughly 1,000 t of bagged ore concentrate. The cargo is loaded on top while everything is frozen. At spring breakup the slab floats free, and small tugs steer it down the Lena or Yenisei to an Arctic transshipment port. There it is unloaded and left to melt, so no barge has to go back upstream.
VALUE: Each spring flood becomes a one-way bulk fleet whose only materials are water, sawdust and frost.

R38
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: freezing stored copper leach liquor both drives a heat engine (the heat of freezing boils the working fluid, which condenses in Arctic air) and crystallises CuSO4·5H2O. Propane is replaced by n-butane, which forms no hydrate on its own and was the direct-contact fluid in the 1960s-70s secondary-refrigerant freeze-desalination pilots. It boils at 0.87-0.97 bar against the liquor and condenses at 0.19-0.30 bar. Freezing is staged so that most ice forms while the liquor is still dilute, which keeps the ice-weighted freezing point at -0.8 °C. Copper recovery is capped at 80% and feed acid at 2.5 g/L, and the 90% case is dropped. Fans would eat about half the output, so a 60-80 m natural-draft dry tower (47-52 Pa of draft at -35 °C) replaces them, and freezing is scheduled into the coldest hours. That gives 6-9 kJ per kg of ice, enough for 42-63% of electrowinning power at Udachny-type cold but only 24-44% at Chara (Udokan). A richer 10 g/L leach solution cuts ice from 492 to 123 kg per kg Cu and the freeze step to $105-165/t Cu, but then the engine covers only about 12% of electrowinning power.
VALUE: At 2.5 g/L the route is 0.56-0.86x as cheap as grid-powered SX-EW at Udokan (i.e. dearer) and 0.83-1.08x at an off-grid diesel site; only an off-grid, low-Mg, 10 g/L case reaches about 1.1-1.4x cheaper.

R39
MECHANISM: Each winter, pumps on a floodplain next to the mine's river spray water mixed with sawdust from local sawmills into moulds. It freezes layer by layer into pykrete hulls (ice with about 14% wood fibre, several times stronger than plain ice and much slower to melt), each carrying a few thousand tonnes. Concentrate or coal stockpiled over winter is loaded straight in, and the spring flood lifts the hulls off the floodplain and carries them north at 100–200 km/day, with one small pusher boat steering each convoy. At the delta port the cargo is moved to ships and the hull is left to melt; wall thickness is set each year from the forecast water temperature and the route length.
VALUE: Winter cold and the spring flood become a free, throwaway fleet that moves bulk cargo from roadless interiors to Arctic ports with no steel hulls to build and no return trip.

R40
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: freezing the stored copper leach liquor both powers the plant (the heat released by freezing boils propane, which drives a turbine and condenses in -35 to -45 °C air) and pushes CuSO4·5H2O out of solution. Corrected numbers: the engine gives 12 kJ of electricity per kg of ice at -35 °C air, 15 at -40 °C and 19 at -45 °C, not the claimed 15-30. Dilute liquor (2.5 g/L Cu, 80% recovery) needs 494 kg of ice per kg Cu. That covers 80-104% of the electrowinning power only in -35 to -40 °C air. Ice grown in place on panels in the pond would need 2.55 km² of panels and fails. The repaired design boils propane directly in the liquor (direct-contact crystallizer), separates ice in a wash column, and condenses in air-cooled condensers. For 10 kt Cu a year that is 127 MW of heat, 4.5 MW of electricity and $63M of capital. Iron and acid set how far the liquor can be concentrated, so the process is limited to oxide-ore liquor that is low in iron (Fe ≤ 0.5-1 g/L, acid ≤ 2-3 g/L, iron kept ferric). R41
MECHANISM: Diamond pits on the Siberian Platform, such as Udachny, already pump out very salty brines and inject them back underground. These brines are rich in lithium, bromine, strontium and magnesium. In winter they are sprayed through snowmaking nozzles into −40 °C air. Almost pure water freezes out of the spray, and the salts stay behind in the liquid that has not frozen. That liquid drains from the bottom of the ice pile and is pumped to the next spray stage. A few stages reach concentrations that solar evaporation ponds take months to produce. The concentrate then goes to standard lithium and bromine recovery, and in spring the ice pile melts into nearly fresh water.
VALUE: A waste stream that costs money to dispose of becomes a source of lithium and bromine, with winter cold doing the job of an evaporation pond and energy needed only for pumping.

R42
MECHANISM: Blasting breaks up the ore body underground, and circulating warm brine thaws only a bounded block of it. A leaching solution is then pumped through the block to dissolve the metal, and wells recover it. Any liquid that leaks toward the edge of the block refreezes on contact with the surrounding frozen ground, so the permafrost acts as a self-healing container. Temperature readings at the boundary show where the seal is. Only the recovered metal, often under 1% of the ore's mass, is ever transported.
VALUE: Extracts metals from remote frozen deposits with no pit, no ore haulage and no artificial liner.

R43
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: crushed ore or coal and snow sealed in an ice shell drift with the current under winter ice, with no vessel and no return trip. The rigid ice shell is essential: in a flexible bag, a 0.7 m change in ice draft would change buoyancy by 390 kg. An HDPE skid on top (μ 0.15, assumed) widens the trim window to 90-404 kg. Pods are filled in autumn at -4 °C, which adds only +10 kg of cold-reserve buoyancy against +65 kg for contents at -25 °C, so no open soaking slot is needed. Pods are 1.2 m tall with a 7-13 cm shell. At 3.7, 11 or 18.4 kg/day of buoyancy loss (water +0.01, +0.03 or +0.05 K), usable trips are only 76-428 km. Coal pods carry 8 t with flooded voids and a 1.76 m³ snow pocket. R44
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: grains charge themselves on the chute lining, and the bins that catch them, wired across to the opposite plates, build the separating field with no power supply, like a Kelvin water dropper. The field is reset to 150 kV/m across a 0.5 m gap (75 kV, 5% of air breakdown), which gives 17-23 cm of drift per side in a drag-included simulation without grains hitting the plates (200 kV/m would hit them). The sorted charge delivers only 0.4-1.4 µA per metre of plate width, so self-excitation needs at least 5e10 Ω of leakage resistance per 10 m channel. Space charge caps each channel at 0.17-0.5 t/h per metre of plate width, so each stage is a stack of alternating-polarity plates at 0.5 m pitch: 170-525 channels, each 10 m wide, for 1 Mt/yr. Ore moisture is removed by a closed air loop through an outdoor cold trap at -30 °C, which gives 2% RH at the separator. R45
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: the frozen ore pile holds the forebay water, and warm water run over its downstream face thaws it, so feed = flow × ΔT / thaw energy. The regime where this is strongest is a loose winter-hoisted pile (bulk ice 0.15, 50-55 MJ/m³). With creek or forebay water at 5-7 °C it needs 2.2-3.2 m³ of water per m³, which matches what a sluice needs (about 3 m³/m³). The original ice-cemented pile (101 MJ/m³) needs 3.8-8 m³/m³ and is water-limited. A loose pile leaks: its cold content refreezes only 5-16% of its voids. The repair is a 3 m upstream ore skin, spray-sealed in winter (a 0.4 m lift freezes in 10-19 h), under a 0.8-1 m saturated-peat blanket that thaws only 0.42 m in 90 days. The forebay is kept at 3 m or less (sliding safety factor about 5, Froehlich breach peak 34 m³/s against 144 m³/s for a season-long reservoir). Above 8-9 °C cold creek water is blended in, because the water ratio would otherwise fall below 2. The face can thaw about 70 m/day, against the 0.8 m/day needed.
VALUE: Summer washing of winter-mined stockpiles costs about $2.0/m³ ($2.95/g at 0.8 g/m³) against about $4.65/m³ ($6.8/g) for natural thaw plus dozer ripping plus loader plus pumped sluice, or for hydromonitor plus gravel pump, so 2.3x cheaper (not 5x).

R46
MECHANISM: In winter, shallow moulds on the frozen banks of a river near the mine are flooded with river water mixed with sawdust, and the cold freezes them into thick pykrete hulls. Ore concentrate is sealed inside these hulls in waterproof bags. At spring breakup the hulls float free and ride the flood hundreds of kilometres downstream to an Arctic port or rail point. There the cargo is lifted out and the hull is simply left to melt. A short GPS and ice-thickness log for each hull is used to adjust next year's hull thickness and launch dates to the river's real flood behaviour.

R47
MECHANISM: Each winter, crews build barge hulls from pykrete (ice reinforced with sawdust, much stronger and tougher than plain ice) right on a frozen tributary near the deposit. They need only spray nozzles, local sawdust and -40°C air, and they load ore concentrate into the hulls on the spot. A forecast built from snowpack and river-gauge data sets the loading schedule for the spring breakup flood, which briefly makes thousands of otherwise unnavigable creeks deep enough to float the barges. A small pusher tows them to a railhead or Arctic river port, where they are emptied and left to melt. A hull costs little more than water, sawdust and labour, so the cost per tonne is a few weeks of towing rather than a road kept open all year.
VALUE: Turns the spring flood on every remote tributary into near-free, single-use bulk freight capacity with no road, dredging or shipyard.

R48
MECHANISM: Anchor kept: bottom-fast pond ice freezes onto the boulder tops and a spring water rise floats them off. The original timing (ice reaching the boulders in March) fails.
