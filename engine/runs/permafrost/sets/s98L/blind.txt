W01
MECHANISM: All winter, frozen placer pay gravel is ripped and spread a few centimetres thick over a broad pad of flat, windy tundra next to the cut. The dry −30 °C wind sublimes the pore ice from the top of the layer, freeing up to about a millimetre a day, and because no liquid water ever forms, the freed silt and light quartz and feldspar sand blow off loose into a snow fence. Gold, cassiterite, wolframite and other heavy grains stay behind; a daily harrow exposes fresh frozen layer, and the residue is swept up and dry-screened to remove the gravel. Because the rate is fixed per square metre, the pad is sized by area (about a square kilometre handles a season's pay gravel), and the silt-free residue, a small fraction of the original volume, is stockpiled so that the short summer wash plant finishes the whole year's ore in a few weeks of clear water, with no mud reaching the river.
VALUE: Placer mines gain a second production season in midwinter and finish all their ore in a few clean-water summer weeks, with no water, reagent or silt discharge and almost no fuel for the winter concentration.

W02
MECHANISM: This is set up beside a trunk line that ends at a gas processing or LNG plant. A small side stream of the line's own gas is partly burned to carbon monoxide and hydrogen. This gas first reduces roasted nickel ore to metal. Then, cooled to about 50 °C at a few bar, it carries the nickel off as nickel tetracarbonyl vapour, while iron, copper and rock stay behind. The vapour is metered into the line at about 0.1 mol%, which stays below its condensation pressure even in permafrost-chilled sections. A little extra carbon monoxide stops it breaking down in compressor stations, and the line runs under the same rules already used for lines carrying toxic sour gas. At the plant, heated beds in the inlet train, where mercury guard beds already sit, split the vapour into pure nickel pellets and carbon monoxide. The carbonyl never leaves the plant fence and no customer ever receives it.
VALUE: A line already carrying about 38 billion m³ of gas a year could also deliver about 100,000 t of refined nickel a year from a deposit along its route. It needs no new transport route and no reagents brought in, and nothing changes for gas customers.

W03
MECHANISM: Rigid spheres about 1 m across, with closed-cell foam shells, sealed holds and no gas, valves or electronics, are trimmed to about 0.5% positive buoyancy and clipped about every 20 m onto a light floating rope, in strings of a hundred or more. A string rides against the smooth underside of a big Siberian river's winter ice and rolls downstream with the current; because the drag on the whole string pulls each sphere along, none can stall in slack water or behind an ice keel long enough to freeze in, and the flow pushes the string down and around ridges and hanging dams. Since these rivers flow north from the railway, strings pushed through a slot at an upstream railhead carry diesel, brought to neutral buoyancy by dense reagents such as acid or lime in a second hold, down to a riverside mine. There they are emptied, refilled with concentrate, re-trimmed and pushed back under the ice to a boom at a downstream port that catches each string by its lead rope, and the empties go back upriver by barge in summer.
VALUE: Through the seven months when nothing else moves on the river, the current under the ice both supplies a riverside mine with fuel and reagents and carries its concentrate to port, without losing cargo to freeze-in and with no vehicles, fuel or tending.

W04
MECHANISM: Oil fields, gas fields and deep mines on the Siberian Platform already pump up strong calcium-chloride brine (often 0.2–0.5 g/L lithium) and must dispose of it. In winter this brine runs through a chain of shallow trays covered with polymer radiative-cooling film under an infrared-clear windshield. The film sheds heat through the 8–13 µm atmospheric window, so the trays sit about 10 K below air that is already at −40 to −50 °C. Each night every tray freezes past the calcium chloride–ice eutectic (about −50 °C). Ice and calcium chloride hexahydrate lock up together as a crust, while lithium chloride, which stays liquid down to about −75 °C, collects in a shrinking leftover liquid that drains by gravity into the next tray. After a few nights and steps, the liquid is tens of times smaller in volume and richer in lithium and bromide, and goes to an existing lithium plant. The spent crust melts in spring and goes back down the field's own disposal wells.
VALUE: Brine that Siberian oil, gas and mine operators now pay to reinject becomes a large-scale lithium concentrate, made with only the winter sky and the wells they already run.

W05
MECHANISM: In early winter, the pay gravel stockpiled in autumn is laid across a creek in layers and sprayed with water, so the dam itself is frozen ore. A peat cover on its crest and upstream face keeps it frozen while spring snowmelt fills the basin behind it. All summer a fixed timber gate lets a steady flow of sun-warmed basin water run down the dam's bare downstream face. This water melts the ice cement at a rate set by the flow, so gravel drops into the water at roughly the right ratio for washing. The mix runs straight down a riffled chute, where cassiterite, wolframite or gold settle and the light gravel goes to a pit. The stored water and its head do both the feeding and the separation, with no loaders, pumps, fuel or reagents.
VALUE: A roadless placer can run a full washing season unattended from one autumn stockpile and one winter's snow, cutting out the summer fuel, pumps and loaders that make up most of its cost.

W06
MECHANISM: At freeze-up a plow makes a single pass down the fast main channel of a big frozen river. It heaps the thin early snow into a low berm along that line and leaves the ice bare on either side. The berm insulates the ice beneath it, so through the early winter the bare sides grow downward much faster. This leaves a smooth upward channel in the ice's underside, and later snow, which falls evenly, does not erase it, so no more clearing is needed. Slightly buoyant pods of crushed high-grade ore are put in through a slot. They rise into the channel and ride the current inside it, so they cannot stray into slow water near the banks or catch on rough ice. A slot at the downstream plant or port lifts them out for concentration where power and reagents already exist.
VALUE: After one plow pass, any large frozen river becomes a self-maintaining, fuel-free winter lane that carries ore from a riverside mine to an existing plant, with no road, vessel or processing plant at the mine.

W07
MECHANISM: Dry crushed ore slides down a chute lined with a chosen material, so each mineral picks up its own sign of charge, and in −40 °C air, which holds almost no water vapour, that charge does not leak away. The grains fall between two plates into two metal-lined bins, each wired to the plate on the far side, so the charge carried into the bins keeps driving the plates up to a spark-gap cap of tens of kilovolts, as in a Kelvin water dropper. One unpowered pass gives only a modest upgrade, but each stage costs only a lining, two plates and a few metres of drop, so a dozen stages are stacked down a hillside or pit wall: each stage's concentrate bin feeds the next stage's chute, and a small conveyor lifts the reject of the lower stages back into the feed. The cascade reaches shipping grade with no power supply, water or reagent for the separation, and its only settings are the splitters, moved according to the grade measured in each bin.
VALUE: Remote deposits make shipping-grade concentrate on site all winter from dry ore, with no water, reagent or power for the separation, cutting the tonnage to be hauled by 5–20 times.

W08
MECHANISM: Copper-bearing liquor from a summer heap leach is stored in a pond, where it stays liquid at 0–4 °C under the winter ice. Through the winter nearly all of it is frozen in successive passes over coils, and the heat given off as it freezes boils propane, which drives a turbine and condenses in finned radiators open to −35 to −50 °C air, giving roughly 15–30 kJ of electricity per kilogram frozen. Each pass leaves a stronger drained brine, until copper sulfate reaches saturation and crystallises out as blue vitriol while the far more soluble ferric sulfate stays dissolved, so freezing both powers the plant and separates the copper from the iron. The crystals are redissolved in the spent electrolyte of a small, clean electrowinning cell run on the same power, and in spring the ferric, acidic brine and the ice meltwater, with any copper trapped in it, return to the heap as its oxidant and acid.
VALUE: A remote copper heap leach makes its own winter power, separates its copper from iron and wins clean cathode copper with no solvent extraction, no diesel and almost no acid shipped in.

W09
MECHANISM: Pods are made on the spot at a slot cut in the main-channel ice of a big north-flowing river. Crushed high-grade ore and enough dry snow to float it (roughly a cubic metre of snow per tonne of ore) are packed into a mould and sprayed with river water. The −40 °C air freezes this overnight into a sealed ice shell. The pods float up against the smooth underside of the ice and ride the current, out of reach of wind and waves, to a boom and recovery slot at a plant or year-round port downstream. There they are lifted out and cracked open, and the ice is thrown away. The 0 °C river cannot melt the pods, they cost nothing and they never go back upstream, so throughput is limited only by how fast the two slots work. Whole ore can therefore travel, and separation stays at an existing concentrator.
VALUE: A riverside deposit can send ore all winter to a downstream plant or port with no vessels, fuel, pod fleet or return haul, using only snow, river water and cold.

W10
MECHANISM: Over a stripped, ice-rich placer, a spillway holds a shallow pond of the ground's own meltwater. The pond absorbs the round-the-clock summer sun and thaws the gravel beneath it, wind waves lift silt and sand over the spillway, and their pressure pulses jig gold and heavy sand down through the stones onto the frozen floor. Each autumn the pond is set just shallower than the winter ice will grow, so the ice freezes onto the tops of the stones. In spring the spillway is closed, snowmelt lifts the bottom-fast ice, and the wind pushes the floes and their stones onto a dump shore, the way lake ice builds boulder ramparts. The spillway is lowered each year so the thin heavy layer rides down with the thaw front to bedrock, where only that layer is dug and washed.
VALUE: Even in the cobbly gravels typical of Siberian placers, sun, wind and winter ice remove the silt, sand and stones in place, so only a few percent of the deposit is ever dug and washed, with no pumps, washing-plant fuel or reagents.
