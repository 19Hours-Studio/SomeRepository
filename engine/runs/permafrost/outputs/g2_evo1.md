**Pair 1.** Parent A uses an insulating mask to control where thaw happens. Parent B uses a smooth ice ceiling as the guide for its sacks. The move they share is to control where water freezes or melts with cheap insulation, and let the climate shape a free conveyor. The offspring applies the mask to river ice instead of yedoma. Where snow is cleared, ice grows thicker, so its underside hangs lower. Two cleared strips make two downward keels with a groove between them. Simple buoyant pods stay in that groove, so they need no valves, gas cartridges or channel digging. The closest existing work is clearing snow to thicken winter ice roads. That practice was never used to shape the underside of the ice as a guide.

**Pair 2.** Both parents treat winter as the working season and use ice as a helpful material instead of an obstacle. The offspring drops the dry plant and the pod logistics. A sluice box hangs in the current below a slot cut in river ice. The river's under-ice flow does the washing all winter, with no pumps and no freezing. The closest existing work is winter through-ice dredging off Nome. That method dredges the seabed with divers. This one washes gravel brought from a land deposit, using only the current.

**Pair 3.** Both parents let a slow, free Arctic condition replace energy and water: years of drift in one, dry cold air in the other. The offspring uses dry, windy −30 °C air to sublimate the ice out of frozen ore. Frozen placer and ore can be 20–50% ice, so this cuts haul weight and leaves material dry enough for dry separation. A rough estimate: about 1.3 km² of pad dries roughly 1 Mt of ore per winter. That is an order-of-magnitude figure, not a sized design. The closest existing work is freeze-thaw dewatering of sludge and tailings. That needs a thaw. This uses direct sublimation, with no thaw at all.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Snow-Masked Under-Ice Guide Groove
MECHANISM: In early winter, two parallel strips are cleared of snow along the fast main channel of a frozen river, while the snow between and beside them is left as insulation. Ice grows faster under the cleared strips, forming two downward keels with a smooth groove between them on the underside of the ice. Passive, slightly buoyant concentrate pods put in through a slot ride the groove on the current without drifting into slow water near the banks. Ice-thickness readings taken through the ice guide where the strips are cleared again after each snowfall.
VALUE: It turns any large frozen river into a self-built winter conveyor for ore with no fuel, vessels or moving parts.

IDEA 2
NAME: Current-Washed Under-Ice Sluice
MECHANISM: A long riffled sluice box is hung beneath a slot cut in the ice of a river that keeps flowing all winter. Frozen placer gravel from a nearby deposit is crushed and dropped into the slot. The river's own 0 °C current, kept liquid by the ice lid, washes the gravel. Gold and heavy minerals settle in the riffles, and fine tailings disperse in the low winter flow. The sluice is lifted weekly through the slot to collect the concentrate, and its angle is set from flow speeds measured through the ice.
VALUE: Placer gold can be washed through the whole winter with no pumps, heated plant or tailings ponds.

IDEA 3
NAME: Wind-Sublimation Ore Drying
MECHANISM: Frozen, ice-rich ore or wet concentrate is spread in thin layers on pads exposed to the dry, windy −30 °C air of deep winter. Ice turns straight into vapour without melting, at roughly 1 kg/m² a day, and a harrow turns the layers. Moisture probes decide when each strip is dry enough to be swept up and the next layer laid down.
VALUE: It removes 20–50% of the hauled weight and gives dry material ready for dry separation, with no fuel or thermal dryers.
