Siberia's extremes can work as free infrastructure instead of obstacles: deep winter cold, the spring flood and steady tundra wind. Here are three ways to use them.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Disposable pykrete flood barges
MECHANISM: Each winter, barge hulls 2–3 m thick are frozen in place at a mine's landing on a north-flowing river (Lena, Vilyuy, Yenisei, Kolyma). Water mixed with about 14% sawmill pulp is sprayed onto a mould layer by layer, and the −40 °C air does the freezing for free. The result, pykrete, is several times tougher than plain ice and melts slowly because a crust of insulating fibre forms on its surface. Ore concentrate or coal is loaded onto the deck during winter. The spring flood floats the hulls, and a few tugs guide convoys through the cold early meltwater to a transfer point in the river delta on the Northern Sea Route. There the cargo is lifted off and the hulls are left to melt. A hull costs only water, sawdust and pump fuel: no steel fleet sits idle, no barge makes an empty trip back upstream, and shallow tributaries are used only while the flood makes them deep.
VALUE: Bulk cargo from deposits with no roads reaches Arctic shipping at almost no cost for the hulls.

IDEA 2
NAME: Spray-freeze mineral brine concentration
MECHANISM: Diamond pits on the Siberian Platform, such as Udachny, already pump out very salty brines and inject them back underground. These brines are rich in lithium, bromine, strontium and magnesium. In winter they are sprayed through snowmaking nozzles into −40 °C air. Almost pure water freezes out of the spray, and the salts stay behind in the liquid that has not frozen. That liquid drains from the bottom of the ice pile and is pumped to the next spray stage. A few stages reach concentrations that solar evaporation ponds take months to produce. The concentrate then goes to standard lithium and bromine recovery, and in spring the ice pile melts into nearly fresh water.
VALUE: A waste stream that costs money to dispose of becomes a source of lithium and bromine, with winter cold doing the job of an evaporation pond and energy needed only for pumping.

IDEA 3
NAME: Autonomous kite-hauled sledge trains
MECHANISM: Strings of sledges on low-friction plastic runners carry concentrate, coal or equipment across frozen tundra, rivers and coastal sea ice. They are pulled by automated traction kites of 100–300 m² flying figure-eights, using the same control method already proven for kites that tow ships. Cold, dense air at −30 °C and the kites' figure-eight flight produce tens of kilonewtons of pull from the ordinary 6–8 m/s winter winds of Yamal and Taimyr, enough for tens of tonnes per kite. A small control unit plans routes from wind forecasts and satellite ice maps and can sail up to about 60° toward the wind. It anchors the train when the wind drops and follows GPS-marked snow corridors, so no graded road is needed. One remote operator supervises dozens of trains.
VALUE: Deposits on the tundra and coasts of Yamal, Taimyr and Chukotka get freight with no fuel and no roads, at a fraction of what diesel trucks cost on winter roads.
