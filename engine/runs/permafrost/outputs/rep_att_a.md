=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Year-Round Under-Ice Talik Crawler
MECHANISM: Anchor: mine the natural thaw bulb under a lake from beneath the intact winter ice, using the ice as roof and work platform. The 40-t crawler cannot fit through a 1.4×1.4 m hole, so it drives in from the shore before freeze-up. It carries a submerged jig, so only about 1% concentrate goes up the 2 m² moon-pool, which takes about 2 kW to keep open. Tailings go by tremie pipe into the crawler's own pit, inside a silt-curtain cell hung from the ice. Without the cell, 540 t/day of fines would add about 3,400 mg/L per day to the ~160k m³ of water under the ice. Load on the ice stays below 25 t until the ice reaches 1.1 m (mid-December at Yakutsk, Stefan a=2.7). The same unit works open water in summer: 270 d/yr at 150 m³/h is about 0.6 Mm³/yr. That needs about 9 ha of lake floor over talik per year, with a 7 m dig against a talik of 25 m or more.
VALUE: About 1,600 rub/g recovered, against about 2,140 for a summer suction dredge and about 1,930 for draining the lake and working it with dozers and a sluice. That is 1.2–1.35× cheaper, not several times, and only for lake placers that cannot be drained.

IDEA 2
NAME: Thermal Drift-and-Drop Placer Caving
MECHANISM: Anchor: water thaws only the pay layer, and the frozen overburden then falls under its own weight without any machine handling it. Toppling the face outward fails, because 62 m³ of rubble per metre of face lands in front of the next niche. So a trench is stripped instead (about 9% of the overburden), and remote low-pressure monitors drive 2-m drifts 30–60 m into the frozen pay from its side. A hydro-elevator at the face sends the slurry back to the trench. Each finished drift is widened past its long-term breaking span: 11–18 m for 6 m of overburden at 0.2–0.5 MPa. It stays open while being worked because a short-term strength of about 1 MPa lets it span about 25 m. The roof then drops 2 m in place, and mining retreats along the trench. Thaw rate is 5–10 m/day in theory and 0.15–5 m/day in field data. Water use is 3–8 m³ per m³ of pay, and fuel is about 60 rub/m³. It works best where overburden is 8–15 m thick.
VALUE: With 10 m of overburden, about 1,930 rub/g, against 2,270–3,980 for open cut with dozers and 3,500–7,000 for underground placer mining (1.2–3.6× cheaper). There is no gain with 6 m of overburden or less.

IDEA 3
NAME: Barge-Mounted Bluff Auger Coal
MECHANISM: Anchor: a spudded floating auger bores a frozen seam at water level in a river bluff and discharges straight into a hold, so no overburden is moved. At 20 kt/yr the rig sits idle most of the year and costs about 4,100 rub/t. The repaired version runs the whole ~100-day navigation season at about 60 t/h, for about 80 kt/yr. That rate sits between historical augers (21.5 t/h) and highwall miners (100–140 t/h). It fills four hopper barges moored alongside instead of its own hold. Thrust of 150–300 kN is taken by a frame bolted to the face, because three spuds (about 360 kN) are marginal. The frame has about 3 m of vertical travel to follow the 1–3 m drop in low-water stage during a campaign. Holes rise slightly so they drain. Face recovery is 62%, about 190 t per 80 m hole, which uses about 0.9 km of bluff a year.
VALUE: About 1,800 rub/t at a village bank within 150 km. That compares with 2,800–3,700 rub/t for a pit at the bank under a 20–30 m bluff (1.6–2× cheaper) and 7,100–11,600 rub/t for coal hauled 2,000+ km, as from Dzhebariki-Khaya (4–6× cheaper). There is no gain under a bluff of 10 m or less.

IDEA 4
NAME: Wintering Barge Bunker Swap
MECHANISM: Anchor: a floating hull is the village's coal bunker and feeds the boiler directly; hulls are swapped, so the coal never touches the shore. Keeping hulls dedicated all year costs 5,700 rub/t with used hulls and 27,000 with new ones, against 1,100–3,400 rub/t saved. So the swap uses the river fleet's idle winter instead. The season's last-voyage barge winters at the village berth as a sealed bunker and leaves after ice-out for summer work. In a sealed hold, only about 1,800 m³ of air enters as coal is drawn, so oxidation adds about 0.3 kW on average and self-heating is negligible. The gas that replaces the coal must be flue gas dried to about 0 °C: raw flue gas is wet, and at 6–9% O₂ it is not inert. As coal is drawn, buoyancy pushes up on the ice at about 80 kN/m, which would crack it. A 55 kW heated moat slot around the hull plus 27 kW of hold heat (3.4% of a 2.4 MW boiler, taken from flue-gas waste heat) prevent this. Draft limits capacity: at 1.2 m of water a 1,500-t hull carries only about 660 t.
VALUE: About 1,360 rub/t where a natural backwater lies within about 150 m of the main boiler. The costs it avoids are transshipment, rehandling, trucking and 5–15% stockpile loss, together 1,100–3,400 rub/t (0.8–2.5×). It loses money if a basin has to be dredged (2,226 rub/t) or a heat main is needed (+1,440 rub/t).

IDEA 1. Anchor kept: yes, mining the talik under intact ice.
- Decisive numbers:
  - Talik forms when the lake is deeper than about 2–3 m of winter ice (Lachenbruch, cited in bg-12-5941).
  - About 25 m of talik under a Yakutian lake, with 0.1–2 °C water under 2 m of ice (SPbU abstract); TEM surveys found 80–91 m elsewhere.
  - Industry cost shares: sluice operations about 40% of gold value, dredges about 25% (Kommersant).
  - Ice needed for a load (Gold formula, A=3.5): 107 cm for 40 t.
- Rounds:
  - R1 as proposed: 3,199 rub/g, 1.66× dearer than draining the lake (fails).
  - R2 with the submerged jig, winter only: 2,115 rub/g, break-even.
  - R3 year-round: 1,611 rub/g.
- Baselines: summer suction dredge 2,138 rub/g; drain the lake then dozer and sluice 1,932; dozer and sluice in general 1,591–2,557 (bottom-up) or about 3,840 (Kommersant 40%).
- Consistency checks:
  - The crawler fits no 2 m² hole, so it enters from the shore.
  - Ice near shore freezes to the bottom, so the crawler cannot leave in winter: one lake per winter, which must hold at least 0.38 Mm³.
  - The liquid gap under 2 m of ice is too small for the crawler, so it works from its own pit.
  - Surface load is limited until mid-December.
  - Fines would reach about 3,400 mg/L per day unless the curtain cell is used.
- Hazards: ice break-through, a frozen umbilical, low oxygen and fish kill, turbid outflow at spring break-up.
- Deciding unknown: recovery of fine gold by a submerged jig at 0–2 °C. Experiment: about 20 paired bulk samples of 5–10 m³, jig against a reference sluice plus tailings assay, enough to resolve 10 points of recovery at σ≈10%. Second unknown: the share of placer reserves under lakes that cannot be drained (TEM survey of about 10 lakes).
- Closest work: summer dredging of lake taliks, draining lakes before placer mining, Komatsu's underwater dozer, research on extending the dredge season (Nafikov). I found no under-ice placer mining.
- Verdict: NICHE.

IDEA 2. Anchor kept: yes, only the pay is thawed and gravity moves the overburden. The design moved from toppling the face to settling the roof in place.
- Decisive numbers:
  - Breaking span from beam formulas with frozen-ground tensile strength: 0.2–0.5 MPa long-term, about 1 MPa short-term [E].
  - Thaw (ablation) rate from a heat-transfer model; Douglas et al. 2023 measured rates about 3× the older theory.
  - Field rates: up to 5 m/day for Bylot Island scarps, 15–20 cm/day lateral erosion on Yamal.
  - Water heat budget: 3–8 m³ of water per m³ of pay.
- Rounds:
  - R1 fails on geometry.
  - R2 at 6 m overburden: 1,767–2,019 rub/g against 1,818–2,841 for dozers, so marginal.
  - R3 at 10 m overburden: 1,933 against 2,273–3,977 for open cut and 3,529–7,059 for underground mining.
- Consistency checks:
  - Drift span must be below the short-term breaking span while open and above the long-term one when finished.
  - Valley floors slope only 0.5–1%, too little for gravel to flow out, so a hydro-elevator is needed at the face.
  - Thawing of the drift roof is negligible over the 10–30 days a drift is open, provided water does not reach the roof.
  - The 2 m void is filled after the roof bulks by about 1.33.
- Hazards: unpredictable collapse timing (no one enters a drift; monitors operate from the trench), turbid discharge, 2 m of surface subsidence, bedrock gold left under the collapsed roof.
- Deciding unknown: long-term breaking span and recovery from bedrock. Experiment: 8–10 instrumented drifts at one placer.
- Closest work: hydraulic undercutting of banks (19th-century California), Klondike steam-point drift mining, underground mining of frozen placers (RD 06-326-99), Rochev 2002 (frozen rock breaking up in water), US5031963 borehole hydraulic mining. I found no surface-trench drift-and-drop.
- Verdict: NICHE (works only with 8–15 m of overburden).

IDEA 3. Anchor kept: yes, auger on a floating platform, coal straight into a hold. The hold is now that of barges moored alongside.
- Decisive numbers:
  - Auger rate: 21.5 t/h for a two-man crew (Pike County history); highwall miners 27–36 t per man-hour with 3–4 crew (Terex); augers up to 1.83 m in diameter and 244 m deep (NIOSH).
  - 178 kt of coal shipped to Arctic Yakutia and Chukotka by the river carrier LORP; supply partly from Dzhebariki-Khaya, with undersupply blamed on late loading and low water (ulus.media, neftegaz).
- Rounds:
  - R1: 4,138 rub/t, so the 1,000 rub/t claim fails.
  - R2: 80 kt at 40 t/h needs 143 days, more than the 100-day season, so inconsistent.
  - R3: 60 t/h, 95 days, 1,798 rub/t.
- Baselines: pit at the bank plus barge 1,939–3,717 rub/t depending on bluff height; small pit plus 150 km winter road 3,850–6,950; central mine plus 2,000–2,500 km river haul 7,100–11,600 [E rates]. The cost of coal delivered to the Arctic was not found in any source.
- Consistency checks: spud capacity is marginal, so the reaction frame is bolted to the face; the 1–3 m stage drop is covered by frame travel; the first 10–30 m of each hole may be in thawed, wet river talik.
- Hazards: bluff blocks falling onto the rig, hole collapse, water inrush, fires in open holes (portals must be sealed), fine auger coal lost in grate-fired boilers.
- Deciding unknown: whether a seam of at least 1.2 m sits at water level under a bluff of at least 20 m within 300 km of users. Experiment: a bank survey of Lena and Kolyma outcrops, then 10–20 test holes to measure penetration rate in frozen coal.
- Closest work: land highwall augers; I found no barge-mounted auger.
- Verdict: WORKS, but only at such a site.

IDEA 4. Anchor kept: yes, the hull is the bunker and is swapped. The N+1 relay survives only for villages heated more than about 285 days a year.
- Decisive numbers:
  - Barge class: 75×17.2 m, 1.8 m draft, 1,500 t (Nibulon V1500).
  - Coal freezes into blocks above 4–5% surface moisture (US4421791).
  - Inert-gas limit of 5% O₂ (SOLAS) [E].
  - Six Yakut districts nearly ran out of boiler coal in December 2025 (istories), which supports the security value.
- Rounds:
  - R1: 5,736 rub/t with used hulls, 26,736 with new (fails).
  - R2: 3,186 rub/t.
  - R3: 1,362 rub/t at a natural backwater, 2,226 with a dredged basin.
- Consistency checks: draft-limited loads, ice cracking from buoyancy uplift (729 kNm/m bending against 225 kNm/m capacity, hence the moat), heat balance, frost from wet flue gas, a summer gap of 35–125 days.
- Hazards: ice drift and flood at break-up, grounding as winter water levels fall, CO/CO₂ in the hold, a holed hull leaving the village with no coal.
- Deciding unknown: the real stockpile loss and handling cost per village. Experiment: utility records from about 10 villages, plus one instrumented winter with a hull.
- Closest work: fleet wintering in zatons, buried coal stores proposed for remote Yakutia (sibran/FTPRPI). I found no barge used as a village bunker.
- Verdict: NICHE.
