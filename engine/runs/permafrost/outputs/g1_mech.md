The approach is to use free flows that already exist and that current practice ignores: river current under the ice all winter, dry and still winter air, and meltwater held back until it's needed.

**Mechanics first**

1. **Packets under the ice.** Sealed sacks of about 1 t hold ground-up valuable material, and each has a small gas bladder. Under the ice cover, the river still flows at 0.3–0.6 m/s all winter. Each bladder is set so its sack is slightly lighter than water, so it presses against the smooth underside of the ice instead of dragging along the rocky bed. The push from the current is greater than the friction against the ice, so each sack covers about 25–50 km a day without fuel or a vessel. If a pressure sensor detects a stall, a valve lets out some gas so the sack drops into the flow, then refills from a cartridge. A net under a slot cut in the ice catches the sacks downstream.

2. **Sorting by charge in winter air.** At −40 °C the air holds almost no water and is usually still. Crushed dry rock is tumbled against a plate. Different minerals pick up different electric charges, and with no film of moisture the charge doesn't leak away. The grains then fall between two charged plates, and each is pushed toward one bin or another by its charge. Readings taken from each bin adjust the plate voltage and the position of the dividers between bins. Valuable grains are separated from waste on site, without water, through the winter months when water-based plants freeze. Only the small valuable fraction gets hauled.

3. **A stored water pulse.** In winter, water is sprayed across a small creek valley to build an ice wall, which is then covered with peat so it melts slowly. Spring snowmelt fills the basin behind it. In midsummer the creek is too shallow for boats. A gate is cut, and the released water (a volume V let out over a time T) forms a crest that raises the water level along the channel. Flat-bottomed barges loaded at the gate ride that crest down to a river big enough for ships. Depth gauges downstream set how wide the gate opens, so the crest stays deeper than the barges' draft for the whole distance. Each year's snowmelt refills the basin, and it can be released in several pulses.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Buoyant Under-Ice Current Packets
MECHANISM: Sealed sacks of about 1 t of ground valuable material each carry a gas bladder, set so the sack is slightly lighter than water and rides pressed against the smooth underside of a frozen river. The current under the ice, which flows all winter, pushes them 25–50 km a day with no fuel or vessel. If a pressure sensor detects a stall, a valve lets out gas so the sack drops back into the flow, then refills it from a cartridge. A net under a slot cut in the ice collects the sacks far downstream.
VALUE: Freight moves along frozen rivers through the whole winter at almost no energy cost, when today nothing moves on them.

IDEA 2
NAME: Cold-Dry-Air Charge Sorting
MECHANISM: Crushed dry rock is tumbled against a plate so that different minerals pick up different electric charges. The near-zero moisture of −40 °C air stops that charge from leaking away. The grains fall between two charged plates and are pushed into separate bins according to their charge. Readings from each bin continuously adjust the plate voltage and the dividers between bins.
VALUE: Valuable material is separated from waste on site through the winter with no water, cutting the tonnage to be hauled by 5–20 times.

IDEA 3
NAME: Stored Meltwater Barge Pulse
MECHANISM: In winter, sprayed water builds an ice wall across a small creek, and a peat cover keeps it from melting. Snowmelt fills the basin behind it, and the water is released through a gate in midsummer as a timed crest. Flat-bottomed barges ride the crest down to a river deep enough for ships. Downstream depth gauges control the gate opening so the crest stays deeper than the barges' draft for the whole distance.
VALUE: Creeks that are too shallow for boats become seasonal freight routes from remote deposits, with no road or dredging.
