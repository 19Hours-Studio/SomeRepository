GOALS (from the center)
1. A bulk corridor from the deposit region to a river, sea or railhead, without building roads or rail.
2. Getting ore off small, stranded deposits that will never justify any corridor.
3. Keeping the ground frozen under everything built. Thaw settlement drives foundation cost and failures.

PARADIGM: Mainstream approaches put fixed, fuel-powered structures and machines on the ground and fight the permafrost with mechanical force. Mechanical earthmoving and fuelled haulage over built routes are forbidden as the mechanism.

PERIPHERY PORTS
G1, from microfabrication wet etching: a mask, an exposure, an etchant, and an etch-stop layer. Ice-rich yedoma (50-90% ice) acts as the "resist". The exposure is stripping or darkening a strip of the surface. The sun and ponded meltwater are the etchant: ponding lowers albedo and deepens the thaw, which feeds back on itself. Insulating mats on the banks are the mask, and ice-poor ground underneath is the etch-stop. The Batagaika megaslump, which started from forest clearing, shows that metres per year is achievable.
Honesty check: the closest work is thermokarst science and Klondike cold-water thawing of placer gravels. I know of no one who has used thermokarst to cut transport channels, so I kept it. Caveat: it releases carbon and methane and loads rivers with sediment.

G2, from artillery fire control. The state is a firing table plus weather data. The update is the radar-measured miss distance, which corrects elevation and charge for the next round. At about 1.5 km/s the energy is about 0.3 kWh/kg, roughly $50/t at 30% efficiency. That compares with $200-400M to build 100 km of all-season road.
Honesty check: the closest work is O'Neill's lunar mass driver, SpinLaunch and the Paris Gun. None of them carried terrestrial bulk freight, so I kept it. Caveats: barrel wear and airspace.

G3, from building photonics: daytime radiative-cooling films reflect sunlight and radiate heat through the 8-13 µm sky window, so they get below air temperature with no power. Very dry Arctic air keeps that window wide open.
Honesty check: the closest work is white or sunshade embankments in Alaska and on the Qinghai-Tibet line. Those only cut heat gain and cannot get below ambient, so I kept it, though it is the most incremental of the three.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Etched thermokarst freight canals
MECHANISM: The surface of ice-rich yedoma is stripped or darkened along a planned line toward a navigable river, while insulating mats protect the banks as a mask. Each summer, meltwater ponds in the strip, absorbs more sun and deepens the thaw. Mud drains away until thawing stops at ice-poor ground, leaving a channel deep enough for barges. Ground-temperature probes guide each year's re-darkening and widening, like etch-rate control.
VALUE: Solar heat does the digging, so lowland deposits get a waterway for the cost of surface treatment instead of a road at millions of dollars per kilometre.

IDEA 2
NAME: Fire-controlled ballistic ore transfer
MECHANISM: A gas or electric gun on the deposit fires steel-cased slugs of 100-500 kg of concentrate at about 1.5 km/s into a sand-and-water catch basin at a railhead or river port more than 100 km away. Radar measures where each round lands, and that miss distance corrects elevation and charge for the next round, exactly like artillery adjusting fire. Casings are recovered and sent back on the seasonal supply route.
VALUE: A stranded deposit is connected to market by two fixed installations and no corridor at all.

IDEA 3
NAME: Sub-ambient photonic permafrost shields
MECHANISM: Pads, embankments, tailings and foundations are covered with polymer radiative-cooling film. It reflects sunlight and radiates heat through the 8-13 µm atmospheric window, so the surface stays below air temperature even in the 24-hour summer sun. Buried thermistors show where the ground is warming, and more film layers or wider coverage go there.
VALUE: Ice-rich ground stays frozen with no power, piles or thermosyphons, so foundations cost far less and stop failing.
