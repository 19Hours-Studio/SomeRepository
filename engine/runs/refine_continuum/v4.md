Continuum is a 3D-SRAM inference accelerator for serving 1.6T-parameter mixture-of-experts (MoE) models at long context and high per-user speed. In a fair model it beats Nvidia Rubin by about 1.0–1.6× on tokens per provisioned MW. The win depends on one unmeasured number: how much latency each layer exposes. It is not shown to be "best in the world". There is no silicon, and every Continuum number below is model output.

# Continuum: a weight-stationary 3D-SRAM inference accelerator for long-context, interactive frontier-MoE decode

## 0. Verdict (round 4)

**What changed since round 3**

1. **GPU all-to-all bandwidth is now modelled.** Expert-parallel dispatch and combine are bounded by scale-up link bandwidth. This does not bind at any tier. On Rubin it reaches 54–65% of compute time, so imperfect overlap would hurt GPUs a little.
2. **Per-layer exposed latency τ is now set per architecture, from measurements.** Round 3 gave every system τ = 8 µs. That value is 5–12× below every GPU figure that can be derived from published speeds, and slightly below the measured wafer-scale figure (§2.2). The headline now uses τ = 20 µs for GPUs/TPUs (2–5× better than measured today) and 10 µs for Continuum (inside the measured Groq-3 LPX and Cerebras range). It also shows the full τ × τ matrix.
3. **The GPU model is checked against InferenceX.** It is within 0.6–2× of measured GB300 points, with errors in both directions. Continuum has no such check.

**Defensible claim**

- Continuum is the strongest design I can put numbers to for one niche: HBM-constrained serving of 0.5–1.6 TB MoE models at ≥128K context and ≥800 tok/s/user.
- There it leads the best GPU by **~1.3–1.6× in tok/MW**, and by **2.2–2.7× in tokens per HBM stack**. This holds only if Continuum's exposed per-layer latency stays at least ~1.5× below a Rubin-class GPU's.
- At throughput tiers (≤500 tok/s/user) it **ties** the best GPU. If GPU software reaches ~8 µs per layer, it **loses by 6–13%**.
- It is not "best in the world" in general, and nothing here proves it is.

**Why the lead is bounded (physics)**

- Once each system picks its own speculation depth, every system is compute-bound. Drafting k tokens multiplies FLOPs per accepted token by (k+1)/E[accepted]: 1.11, 1.23, 1.36 and 1.49 for k = 1–4 at p = 0.8.
- At ≥1,000 tok/s/user, Continuum runs at k = 2 and Rubin needs k = 4. That costs Rubin 1.49/1.23 ≈ 1.2× in compute.
- The rest of the lead comes from three things:
  - the latency budget: 62 × 20 µs = 1.24 ms of Rubin's 3.1 ms step;
  - FLOPs per provisioned watt: 13.1 PF/kW for Continuum against 11.4 for Rubin 35 PF and 16.3 for Rubin 50 PF;
  - HBM stacks.
- None of the three is a 4× effect.

## 1. Target workload and metrics

**Workload**

- **Primary: decode of a DeepSeek-V4-Pro-class MoE.**
  - 1.6T total and 49B active parameters, 61 layers, hidden size 7168.
  - 384 routed experts (top-6) plus 1 shared expert; expert FFN size 3072.
  - 128 query heads × 512 sharing one KV head.
  - Compressed sparse attention (CSA: 4× compression, top-1024 selection) and heavily compressed attention (HCA: 128× compression), split ~30/31 across layers, with a 128-token window.
  - KV cache in FP8 with BF16 RoPE dimensions.
  - Context 128K (also 8K and 1M).
- **Speculation:** a block drafter costing one MoE layer, which drafts all k tokens in one pass (DSpark/DFlash-style). Acceptance is geometric with p = 0.8 or 0.6.
  - Reported acceptance lengths are 3.1–3.85 at depth 5, which fits p ≈ 0.8 on predictable output.
  - Depth-1 multi-token prediction (MTP) gains only ~0–10% on free prose.
- **Secondary:** agentic turns, i.e. 2K new prefill tokens per 1K output tokens over a cached 128K prefix.
- **Out of scope:**
  - Training and edge.
  - Models under ~200B parameters. These already fit in SRAM-only systems: LPX reached 3,431 tok/s/user on Gemma 4 31B.

**Metrics**

| Metric | Why it defines "best" here |
|---|---|
| **Output tok/s per provisioned MW at a per-user speed floor** (200 … 2,000 tok/s/user), each system at its own best speculation depth | Sites are power-limited. Without a speed floor, any chip looks efficient at 5 tok/s/user. Without best-k for each system, the comparison favours whichever chip reads weights cheapest (round 2's mistake). |
| Output tok/s per HBM stack | HBM4 is sold out. A 12-high stack is reported at ~$600 in 2026 and forecast at ~$1,300 in 2027 |
| Activity energy per token | Physical consistency check |
| Max tok/s per user | The latency-tier race |
| Cost per token | Rough only. Capex per MW is assumed similar within ±30% |

**Why interactive long context.** A 20K-token chain of thought takes 20 s at 1,000 tok/s/user and 100 s at 200. Agents carry 100K+ contexts.

- Multi-GPU NVLink domains measure **~45–110 µs per layer** today.
- What I assume going forward:
  - The GPU gap is mostly software and dynamic all-to-all synchronisation. Persistent megakernels and CUDA graphs could cut it 2–5×, which is the basis for the GPU central τ = 20 µs.
  - Continuum adds two package-to-package collectives per layer on top of an LPX-class design, which is the basis for its central τ = 10 µs.
- Neither τ is measured on the actual hardware (§10, tests 1 and 5).

### 2.4 Process, packaging and supply facts

- TSMC HD SRAM bitcell is 0.021 µm² (N5/N3); the N2 macro reaches 38.1 Mb/mm².
- HBM4 is 2048 bits wide, ~2–3.3 TB/s per stack.
- CoWoS is ~130k wafers per month by late 2026, 10–20% short of demand.
- SoIC:
  - Supply-chain reports put capacity at ~10–15k wafers per month in 2026. TSMC does not publish an official figure.
  - Bond pitch has moved from 9 µm to 6 µm.
  - Capex is reported at ~$6.8–7B per 10k wafers per month.

## 3. Architecture

### 3.1 Package blocks

**C-Die (compute)**

- 2 per package, ~750 mm² each on N2. 144 tiles per die, 288 per package.
- Each tile has a ~111 TF FP4 tensor engine plus an FP32 vector/softmax unit.
- **32 PF FP4 / 16 PF FP8 dense per package.**
- Attention runs on the tensor engines. All 128 query heads share one KV head, so each KV entry feeds a GEMM with M = 128 × positions.
- Compute energy is assumed at 0.04 pJ/FLOP, giving 1.28 kW. For comparison, Rubin's whole chip runs at ~0.05–0.065 pJ/FLOP all-in on N3P, and N2 is roughly 25–30% lower power at the same performance.

**Dataflow**

- Activations stay in registers and weights stream past them.
- At the central operating points each expert slice sees **~10–17 positions per stage** (`ops4.txt`).
- Per tile, the W-Tier feeds 0.89 TB/s, or ~1.6 T FP4 weights/s. The ridge is ~35 positions per expert, so the W-Tier is deliberately over-provisioned relative to compute at these batch sizes. W-Tier time is 19 µs against 29–52 µs of compute per stage.

**W-Tier (weight SRAM)**

- 2 tiers under each C-Die, 4 per package. Each is an N4-class, ~750 mm² SRAM-only die, hybrid-bonded face-to-back (SoIC) at ~9 µm pitch (6 µm is available).
- **Capacity: 2.3 GB per tier, 9.2 GB per package (~32 MB under each tile).**
  - Derivation: 31.8 Mb/mm² macro × 0.89 (ECC) × 0.90 (TSV keep-out) × 0.97 (repair) ≈ 24.7 Mb/mm².
- **Bandwidth: 256 TB/s per package at 0.15 pJ/bit** (range 0.08–0.3), which is 307 W. Power sets this cap, not the bonds.
- Energy per bit:
  - macro read ~0.03–0.05 pJ;
  - ~1 mm of wire at ~0.04 pJ/bit/mm;
  - bond ~0.01 pJ.
- Bonds: ~4.7k signal bonds per tile at 1.5 Gb/s, taking ~0.4 mm² of a ~5 mm² tile.

**K-Mem (KV cache only)**

- 4 × HBM4 12-high: 144 GB at ~10 TB/s. Weights never touch HBM.

**Link**

- 64 lanes of 224G per direction, 1.8 TB/s per direction (85% usable in the model).
- Allocation: 2 × 12 lanes within the layer group, 30 lanes to the next stage, 10 lanes to a switched plane for spare remapping.
- Modelled link time per stage is 7.6–13.5 µs against 29–52 µs of compute. It never binds.

**Power**

| Item | W |
|---|---|
| Compute | 1,280 |
| W-Tier | 307 |
| HBM | 320 |
| Links | 144 |
| Leakage, clock, other | 250 |
| **Peak** | **~2.3 kW** |

- TDP is 2.1 kW.
- **The provisioned slot is 2.45 kW**, including the share of hosts, switches and power conversion. **This number is an assumption, and it carries ~1.25× of the result** (§7).

**Thermal**

- The C-Die sits on top, against the cold plate. The stack runs at ~1.05–1.2 W/mm², GPU-class, so there is **no thermal advantage**.
- About 700 A per C-Die reaches it through TSVs in two tiers.

**Packaging**

- 2 SoIC stacks plus 4 HBM4 sites on CoWoS-L, an interposer of ~3 reticles (Rubin uses ~4).

**Pod**

- **192 packages: 187 active (62 layers incl. drafter × 3, plus 1 for the LM head) and 5 spare.**
- 3 liquid-cooled racks, ~470 kW in total.
- 1.77 TB of SRAM and 27.6 TB of HBM.

### 3.2 Mapping: 3 packages per layer, static point-to-point traffic

**Layer placement**

- One V4-Pro layer is 14.65 GB: 14.3 GB of NVFP4 experts plus ~0.43 GB of FP8 attention and shared expert.
- At g = 3 packages per layer, SRAM is 53% full.

**Within a layer group**

- Every expert is sliced into thirds of its intermediate dimension across the 3 packages.
- Attention is data-parallel: each package holds 1/3 of the users' KV.
- FP8 hidden states are all-gathered within the group, and BF16 partial sums are reduce-scattered to the next group.

**Why this matters for latency (the structural argument)**

- A GPU at large batch must use expert-parallel all-to-all. Each step needs data-dependent token counts exchanged first, then variable-size transfers among 72 endpoints. Tensor-parallel slicing of experts across 72 GPUs would cost ~1 GB of collectives per GPU per layer at B·pos ≈ 46k (~0.55 ms per layer), so it is not an option.
- Continuum's group is 3 packages, and each microbatch holds only ~400–1,100 positions. Its collectives therefore have fixed size, fixed peers and compile-time schedules, whatever the routing.
- That is the reason to expect Continuum's τ to sit nearer LPX (~5–15 µs) than NVL72 (~45–110 µs). It is an argument, not a measurement.

## 6. Where Continuum loses or ties

1. **Throughput tiers against Rubin50:** a tie (1.00 at 128K, 0.99 at 8K).
2. **If GPU software reaches τ ≈ 8 µs:** Continuum loses by 6–13% at all tiers. This is round 3's result.
3. **If Continuum's own τ is ~20 µs:** it ties or loses (0.86–1.01) unless GPUs stay above ~30 µs.
4. **Prefill:** 0.90–1.14×, a tie.
5. **Peak single-stream speed:** about a tie with CS-4 (5.3k against 5.6k modelled). On models ≤200B, LPX and Cerebras probably win outright.
6. **Deployment unit:** about 470 kW and 192 packages. Small models waste it, and models above ~1.6 TB of weights need a second pod.
7. **Training:** not a target. Use GPUs or TPU 8t.
8. **Software maturity:** GPUs gained ~5× on V4 in 43 days. Continuum would start that curve from zero, and every GPU software gain reduces the per-layer latency gap that Continuum depends on.

| Models stay sparse MoE with compressed attention, 0.5–1.6 TB of weights | | | Dense long-context attention makes decode HBM-bound: Continuum has 10 TB/s, Rubin 22 |

**Reading.** Continuum wins only if three conditions hold together:

- Rubin-class GPUs keep ≥ ~15–20 µs of exposed latency per layer while Continuum achieves ≤ ~10 µs;
- tiles reach ≥55% of peak at ~10 positions per expert slice;
- the provisioned slot is ≤ 2.45 kW.

If any one fails, Continuum is just another inference chip.

## 8. Risks and physical, process and supply limits

- **SoIC capacity (largest supply risk).**
  - 1 GW is ~408k packages: ~816k C-Dies, each with 2 tier bonds.
  - At ~70 C-Die candidates per wafer that is ~23k wafer-level bonding passes, **about 1.5–2.3 months of TSMC's entire reported 2026 SoIC capacity**, most of it already committed to others.
  - The 1-tier SKU halves this, at ≥0.95× of the primary's tok/MW up to 1,500 tok/s/user at p = 0.8.
  - Tiers are tested as known-good dies before bonding.
- **CoWoS-L:** a ~3-reticle interposer gives ~20 per wafer, ~20k wafers per GW. Rubin needs about the same per GW.
- **HBM4:** ~1.6M stacks per GW (1.2M with the 3-stack SKU) against ~2.6M for Rubin. **This is Continuum's most robust advantage.**
- **Thermal and IR drop.**
  - ~1.05–1.2 W/mm², with ~700 A per C-Die through TSV-perforated tiers.
  - SRAM leakage is ~100–200 W at high temperature.
  - N2 has no backside power delivery; A16 would help.
- **Yield.**
  - A 750 mm² N2 die at D0 ≈ 0.1/cm² yields ~47% raw, ~65% with tile harvesting.
  - Tiers yield ~90% after repair; each bond interface ~98–99%.
  - A package with one dead tier still runs at g ≥ 3.
- **Pipeline fragility:** one failed package stalls 1/187 of the pipeline, so the 5 spares and the switched remap plane are mandatory.
- **Workload drift.**
  - **Better drafters** (p ≈ 0.9) shrink the interactive lead toward the FLOPs-per-watt ratio, ~1.0–1.15×.
  - More active parameters push work toward compute, where Continuum only ties.

## 9. Software and programming model

- **Compiler path:** PyTorch or JAX → `torch.export` / StableHLO → Continuum compiler. The compiler:
  - chooses g and assigns the 61 layers, the drafter and the LM head to stage groups;
  - slices experts into thirds;
  - keeps vendor FP4 checkpoints, or quantizes to NVFP4 behind an accuracy gate;
  - emits static per-stage schedules for tiles, W-Tier reads, HBM KV pages and link slots;
  - chunks microbatches so link transfers overlap compute.
- **Dynamic MoE routing** is resolved inside a stage. Because every expert spans all 3 packages, routing changes only which W-Tier rows each tile reads. There are no runtime collectives and no count exchange, which is the source of the latency advantage.
- **Speculation:**
  - The block drafter is one more pipeline stage.
  - Depth k is chosen per speed tier at runtime (k = 1–2 typical).
  - Tree drafts are extra positions inside a microbatch slot.
- **Serving:** a vLLM/SGLang out-of-tree platform plugin, the same route TPU and Ascend use. It provides continuous batching into fixed microbatch slots, prefix caching in HBM and host memory, and speculative decoding.
- **What happens to existing models and frameworks:**
  - Standard PyTorch/JAX models that export cleanly run unchanged at the framework level.
  - Custom CUDA/Triton kernels do not port.
  - Dynamic shapes are padded into buckets.
  - New attention variants need compiler support before they run.
  - Non-FP4/FP8 checkpoints need post-training quantization.

## 10. Tests that could prove the design wrong

| # | Test | Tool | Kill criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Continuum's exposed per-layer latency: chunked all-gather and reduce-scatter across 3 packages, a hop to the next stage, and the layer's dependent operator chain | FPGA or test-chip loopback with 224G SerDes; ASTRA-sim | τ > 12 µs |
| 2 | **Rubin per-layer latency:** batch-1 and batch-64 V4-Pro decode on NVL72 with MTP or block drafters; fit τ from step time minus HBM and compute time | Own runs or InferenceX | Rubin τ ≤ 12 µs → lead against Rubin50 falls below 1.15×; do not tape out |
| 3 | Tile efficiency at M = 4–40, FP4 with E4M3 scales | Timeloop + RTL of one tile | η < 0.55 |
| 4 | W-Tier bandwidth and energy through the bond path | CACTI/DESTINY + Accelergy; ISSCC macro data | < 180 TB/s or > 0.3 pJ/bit per package |
| 5 | Thermal and IR drop for a C-Die over 2 tiers | HotSpot / 3D-ICE; PDN solver | Tj > 105 °C, IR drop > 5%, or retention failures |
| 6 | Real draft acceptance on production traffic, at depths 1–5 | GPU runs with DSpark-class drafters | Mean p ≥ 0.85 → lead ≤ 1.15× |
| 7 | GPU model calibration at 100–300 tok/s/user, with the token basis confirmed | InferenceX raw data | If the model is >2× off in either direction after fixing the token basis, re-derive derates for all systems |
| 8 | End-to-end pipeline with V4 routing traces: expert skew, sequence-length variance, acceptance variance across microbatches | Event simulator or LLMCompass | < 70% of the `model4` operating point |
| 9 | Provisioned slot power from a full PDN and host design | Bottom-up power model | > 2.8 kW |

None of these could be run here: there is no silicon, and model downloads are blocked. Everything above is analytical.
