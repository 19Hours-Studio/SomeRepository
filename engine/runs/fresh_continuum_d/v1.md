The answer is an unusually honest and internally consistent model, but the gain it actually supports is small and narrow. On its own metric (decode tokens/s per provisioned watt against a power-trimmed Rubin NVL72, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, 128K context) it supports about **1.3× at ≥1,000 tokens/s per user**, and **roughly a tie (about 1.0–1.1×) at normal serving speeds**. All of it is model output, with no simulation or silicon behind it.

**What I checked**
- **Reproduction:** I re-ran the model. It reproduces the published output exactly: 55.2 tok/s/W, and ratios of 1.13 / 1.25 / 1.51 / 1.71 / 2.51× at the ≥200 / 500 / 1,000 / 1,500 / 2,000 tok/s/user floors. The arithmetic is consistent. Capacity limits, the slowest pipeline stage, and the power and voltage rules are applied to both systems.
- **Bulk speeds are a tie:** I added floors of 20, 50 and 100 tok/s/user, which is where measured GPU decode fleets actually run (34–70 tok/s/user in its own calibration points). The ratio stays at 1.13×. On the same process node it is 1.02×, and the answer itself calls this a tie. So against best current practice at normal serving speeds there is essentially no gain.
- **Part of the headline is just a newer process:** the 1.51× assumes N2 against Rubin's N3P. On the same node it is 1.36×.
- **The on-chip SRAM capacity looks optimistic:** 18.4 GB on eight 750 mm² N4 tiers implies about 24.5 Mb/mm². AMD's stacked V-Cache, scaled from N7 to N5, gives about 18 Mb/mm². At that density the SRAM is about 13.8 GB, and the "indexer keys in SRAM" lever disappears because the CSA packages were already 94% full. Re-running with that change:
  - ≥1,000 drops to 1.42×, or 1.28× on the same node;
  - with the answer's own η = 0.5 case on top, ≥1,000 is 1.12× and ≥200 is 0.84×.
- **The biggest unknown is untested:** GPUs reach only 0.29–0.34 of what this model predicts in decode. The design's gain needs Continuum to reach at least 66% of that fraction, and nothing tests this.
- **Weaker competitor comparisons:**
  - Against the 4-die Rubin Ultra, the gain is 1.12–1.14× at ≥1,000, and it loses at bulk speeds (0.92×).
  - The Rubin+LPX comparison (8.5×) is not credible. The model gives LPX only 72 Rubins for attention, which makes it about 5.6× worse than Rubin alone at high speed. That contradicts Nvidia's positioning of LPX for high-speed decode, with its claim of up to 35× throughput per MW ([Nvidia LPX](https://nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/lpx), [Nvidia developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/?p=114202)). That claim is unverified, and Nvidia doesn't say what it is measured against.
  - The large ratios at ≥2,000 mostly reflect Rubin running into its speed ceiling, as the answer itself says.
- **Site level:** if prefill stays on GPUs, the gain fades toward 1.0×. It also comes at 2.4–3.2× the silicon per token and depends on SoIC hybrid-bonding capacity, which the answer itself names as the binding supply limit.

**What's credible:** it uses about 6–8× fewer tokens' worth of HBM, and it gives a plausible efficiency edge of about 1.3× in the high-speed tier. It is not "best in the world", which the answer admits.

HONEST GAIN: ~1.3× decode tok/s/W vs Rubin at ≥1,000 tok/s/user (same node, model-only, unvalidated); ~1.0× (none) at bulk serving speeds
