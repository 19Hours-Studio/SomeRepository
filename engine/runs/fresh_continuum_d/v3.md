The answer supports a modelled gain of about 1.3–1.5× over Rubin, not its 1.64× headline. That gain holds only at ≥1,000 tok/s/user, and nothing has been measured. The design is physically plausible, and the answer is unusually candid about its limits.

**What I checked.** I re-ran the answer's own model (`model10.py`) and got its base case exactly: 60.0 against 36.6 tok/s/W, or 1.27 / 1.40 / 1.64 / 1.92 / 2.90 across the speed tiers (≥200 to ≥2,000 tok/s/user). **What shrinks the headline:**
1. **Process node.** About 1.19× of the 1.64× comes from assuming Continuum is built on TSMC's newer N2 process while Rubin is on N3P. That is a process advantage, not an architectural one. On the same node the answer's own figure is 1.48×.
2. **Zero-skip.** Continuum is credited with skipping zero weights for a 1.43× compute boost. For 4-bit block-scaled weights I estimate only about 6% round to zero, which is closer to ×1.06. Without that boost the ratio falls to 1.34×.
3. **The fast tier has never been measured.** No hardware has served DeepSeek-V4-Pro at 1,000 tok/s/user. Measured GB300 points top out around 190 tok/s/user at about 500 tok/s per GPU ([InferenceX](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/compare/deepseek-v4-gb300-vs-h200), [B300 run](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/run/deepseek-v4-on-b300)); the model puts Rubin at 57,000. So the comparison only holds if both systems reach the same fraction of their modelled speed, at a tier where neither has been measured.
4. **Continuum would ship in 2028.** Against designs from the same period it roughly ties: about 1.24× the 4-die Rubin Ultra, falling to 0.88× under its worst adverse assumptions, and 1.02–1.13× an Nvidia Feynman stand-in on the same node.
5. **No gain at bulk speeds.** At ≥200 tok/s/user the ratio is 0.95–1.27×.

**What holds up:**
- The arithmetic is internally consistent and reproduces exactly.
- It uses 7.5× fewer HBM gigabytes per token at ≥1,000. That is a real advantage while HBM supply is the bottleneck, though it is paid for in stacked-SRAM packaging capacity.
- The answer separates facts from assumptions and already admits most of these limits itself (§0.3, §7).

The idea of keeping expert weights stationary in on-chip SRAM across a pipeline of chips is not new. Groq and Cerebras already do it, and Nvidia's Rubin with Groq's LPX splits decode work between GPU and SRAM chips. What Continuum adds is stacking SRAM under the compute dies and keeping HBM for the attention cache.
