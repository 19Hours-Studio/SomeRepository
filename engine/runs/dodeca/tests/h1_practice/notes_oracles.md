## A. Germanic and Gaulish lots (the only direct NW-European descriptions)

### A1. Tacitus, Germania 10 (written AD 98) **SS**
Latin (thelatinlibrary.com/tacitus/tac.ger.shtml; `sources/tac_ger.html`):
> "Auspicia sortesque ut qui maxime observant: sortium consuetudo simplex. Virgam frugiferae arbori decisam in surculos
> amputant eosque notis quibusdam discretos super candidam vestem temere ac fortuito spargunt. Mox, si publice
> consultetur, sacerdos civitatis, sin privatim, ipse pater familiae, precatus deos caelumque suspiciens ter singulos
> tollit, sublatos secundum impressam ante notam interpretatur. Si prohibuerunt, nulla de eadem re in eundem diem
> consultatio; sin permissum, auspiciorum adhuc fides exigitur."

Steps (my tr. of the passage above):
1. A branch is cut from a fruit-bearing tree and cut into slips (surculi). **SS**
2. The slips are told apart by "certain marks" (notis quibusdam discretos). The marks are not described. **SS**; what they were: **ND**
3. They are scattered "at random and by chance" on a white cloth (super candidam vestem). **SS**
4. Who reads: for public questions the priest of the civitas (sacerdos civitatis); for private ones the father of the
   household himself (pater familiae). **SS**
5. He prays to the gods, looks up at the sky, and picks up three slips one at a time (ter singulos tollit). **SS**
   Note: "ter singulos tollit" is read by most as "picks up one three times" (three slips). Thomas Gordon's old
   translation (Gutenberg #2995, `sources/gutenberg_2995.txt`) renders it "takes up every piece thrice". The Latin
   allows both, so the exact count is a translation issue. **SS (ambiguity)**
6. He interprets them by the mark cut on them earlier. **SS**
7. A negative result: no further question on the same matter that day. A positive result still had to be confirmed by
   auspices. **SS**
Relevance to H1: the device is sticks plus a cloth and selection by hand; nothing is "thrown and read from the top". The
answer comes from three signs together, not from one face. **INF**

### A2. Caesar, Bellum Gallicum 1.50 and 1.53 (Ariovistus' Germans, 58 BC) **SS**
Latin (thelatinlibrary.com/caesar/gall1.shtml; `sources/caesar_bg1.html`):
- 1.50: "...apud Germanos ea consuetudo esset ut matres familiae eorum sortibus et vaticinationibus declararent utrum
  proelium committi ex usu esset necne; eas ita dicere: non esse fas Germanos superare, si ante novam lunam proelio
  contendissent." This shows the matres familiae declaring by lots and prophecies whether to give battle. **SS**
- 1.53: "Is se praesente de se ter sortibus consultum dicebat, utrum igni statim necaretur an in aliud tempus
  reservaretur: sortium beneficio se esse incolumem." The captive Procillus reported that lots were consulted three times
  in his presence on whether to burn him at once. **SS**
- Device, surface and face reading: **ND** (Caesar does not describe them).

### A3. Ammianus Marcellinus 31.2.24 (Alans, written c. 390) **SS**
Latin (thelatinlibrary.com/ammianus/31.shtml; `sources/amm31.html`): "futura miro praesagiunt modo. nam rectiores virgas
vimineas colligentes, easque cum incantamentis quibusdam secretis praestituto tempore discernentes, aperte quid
portendatur norunt." This is a steppe people, not the NW provinces. They used straight willow rods, sorted with secret
incantations at a set time. **SS**

### A4. Late Gaul (5th-6th c., after the dodecahedra period)
- Council of Vannes (465) condemned the use of the (Latin) Sortes Sanctorum, which were consulted with dice. The Greek
  original had 216 answers for three ordered throws of one die; it was later reduced to 56 answers for one throw of
  three dice. **SS (secondary)**: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_Sanctorum, citing Klingshirn 2002, JECS 10.1:77-130.
- The Sortes Sangallenses survive on a composite palimpsest manuscript "datable to c. AD 600". Klingshirn argued that
  they "were too Roman, too secular to survive into late Merovingian and early Carolingian Gaul". **SS (review)**:
  A. Nice, BMCR 2006.04.10 (bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2006/2006.04.10), reviewing Klingshirn in Johnston & Struck (eds.),
  *Mantike* (2005) 99-128.
- Isidore, Etym. 8.9.28 (Visigothic Spain, early 7th c.): "Sortilegi sunt qui sub nomine fictae religionis per quasdam,
  quas sanctorum sortes vocant, divinationis scientiam profitentur, aut quarumcumque scripturarum inspectione futura
  promittunt." Sortilegi are defined as people who profess divination through the "lots of the saints" or the
  inspection of writings. Isidore 8.9.23 (genethliaci) also says "Geneses enim hominum per duodecim caeli signa
  describunt". **SS**: thelatinlibrary.com/isidore/8.shtml (`sources/isid8.html`).

## B. Roman and Italic lots

### B1. Praeneste (Fortuna Primigenia); Cicero, De divinatione 2.85-87 (44 BC) **SS**
Latin (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/divinatione2.shtml; `sources/cic_div2.html`):
- 2.85: "Quid enim sors est? Idem prope modum quod micare, quod talos iacere, quod tesseras..." Cicero equates a lot
  with finger-flashing, throwing tali and throwing dice. The founding legend has lots bursting out of split rock,
  "in robore insculptas priscarum litterarum notis": carved in oak with marks of ancient letters. **SS**
- 2.86: a chest (arca) was made of olive wood and the lots were kept in it, "quae hodie Fortunae monitu tolluntur";
  "quae Fortunae monitu pueri manu miscentur atque ducuntur": they are mixed and drawn by a boy's hand at Fortuna's
  prompting. **SS**
- 2.86-87: the practice is now used by the common people ("atque id in volgus"); "Quis enim magistratus aut quis vir
  inlustrior utitur sortibus?" Elsewhere lots "plane refrixerunt" (have gone quite cold). **SS**
- Steps: (1) the enquirer comes to the sanctuary; (2) a boy mixes the oak lots in the olive-wood chest; (3) he draws one;
  (4) the inscribed lot is read. **SS** for the boy, the mixing and drawing, and the inscribed oak lots. Who interpreted
  the lot and whether there was a fee: **ND** in Cicero.
- Lots are drawn, not thrown; no face is read; the lots carry letters. **SS** (the letters); **INF** (contrast with H1).

### B2. Other Italic lots: Caere and Falerii (Livy) **SS**
thelatinlibrary.com/livy/liv.21.shtml and liv.22.shtml (`sources/liv21.html`, `liv22.html`):
- Livy 21.62 (prodigies of 218 BC): "Caere sortes extenuatas"; later "lectisternium Caere, ubi sortes attenuatae erant".
- Livy 22.1: at Falerii "sortes sua sponte attenuatas unamque excidisse ita scriptam: 'Mauors telum suum concutit'". The
  lots shrank by themselves and one fell out bearing the inscription "Mars shakes his spear". These are written lots,
  kept as a set at a sanctuary. **SS**. (Section numbers within the chapters were not checked; the chapters were.)

### B3. Cicero, De divinatione 1 (Quintus' side) **SS** (`sources/cic_div1.html`)
- 1.34: "non ea quae aequatis sortibus ducuntur" means lots drawn after being made equal; Quintus nevertheless respects
  ancient lots "quas e terra editas accepimus". **SS**
- 1.76: at Dodona the Spartan envoys set down the vessel (vas) "in quo inerant sortes"; the king's ape scattered the lots.
  The lots were kept in a vessel and drawn. **SS**
- 1.23 and 2.48: four tali can give a Venus throw by chance, but not a hundred Venus throws out of 400 tali. 2.121:
  "Quid est tam incertum quam talorum iactus?" **SS**

### B4. Golden tali in the Aponus spring (Geryon oracle near Patavium); Suetonius, Tiberius 14.3 **SS**
Latin (thelatinlibrary.com/suetonius/suet.tib.html; `sources/suet_tib.html`): "cum Illyricum petens iuxta Patavium adisset
Geryonis oraculum, sorte tracta, qua monebatur ut de consultationibus in Aponi fontem talos aureos iaceret, evenit ut
summum numerum iacti ab eo ostenderent; hodieque sub aqua visuntur hi tali."
Steps: (1) the enquirer (Tiberius, before AD 2) draws a lot (sorte tracta); (2) the lot tells him to throw golden tali
into the spring; (3) the tali "showed" the highest number; (4) they stayed visible under the water (Suetonius' own time).
**SS**. The number counted was the one the tali showed as they lay, seen from above through the water. The upward face
counted. **INF** (from "ostenderent ... sub aqua visuntur"). This is the clearest ancient case of tali **thrown and
read as they came to rest** for a divinatory answer. It is in N Italy, not the NW provinces.

### B5. Venus throw and gaming (not divination); Suetonius, Augustus 71 **SS**
(`sources/suet_aug.html`): "talis enim iactatis, ut quisque canem aut senionem miserat, in singulos talos singulos
denarios in medium conferebat, quos tollebat universos, qui Venerem iecerat." Four tali; the "dog" and the "six" are
losing throws; the Venus throw takes the pot. This is gaming at dinner, not divination. **SS**

### B6. Sortes Vergilianae; Historia Augusta, Hadrian 2.8 **SS**
(thelatinlibrary.com/sha/hadr.shtml; `sources/sha_hadr.html`): "cum sollicitus de imperatoris erga se iudicio Vergilianas
sortes consuleret ... sors excidit" (Aen. 6.808-812 follows). The device is a book or written verses; "sors excidit"
("the lot fell out") suggests a drawn written lot. **SS (text)**; the exact mechanics: **ND**.

### B7. Laws: Codex Theodosianus 9.16.1-12 (319-409) **SS**
Text: thelatinlibrary.com/theodosius/theod09.shtml (`sources/cth09.html`, extract `sources/cth_9_16.txt`).
- 9.16.1-2 (319): haruspices may not enter private houses, though public rites are allowed.
- 9.16.4 (dat. viii kal. febr. Mediolano, Constantius IX and Julian II coss = 25 Jan 357): "nemo haruspicem consulat aut
  mathematicum, nemo hariolum ... sileat omnibus perpetuo divinandi curiositas"; the penalty is the sword.
- 9.16.8 (370/373): mathematici (astrologers) are banned.
- 9.16.9 (29 May 371, dat. **Treviris**): haruspicina itself is not a crime, only its harmful use.
- 9.16.12 (409): mathematici are expelled unless they burn their books before bishops.
- **Text check:** none of the twelve laws names sors, sortes, sortilegus, alea or tali (string search of the Latin Library
  text). Lot divination falls only under the general "divinandi curiositas" of 9.16.4. **SS** (for the absence in this text).

## C. Book-of-fate oracles (numbered answers)

### C1. Sortes Astrampsychi **SS (reviews and summaries of the Teubner edition)**
Edition: G. M. Browne (vol. I, 1983) and R. Stewart (vol. II, ecdosis altera, 2001), Teubner. Review: P. W. van der Horst,
BMCR 2001.10.04 (bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.10.04).
Steps (BMCR 2001.10.04; repeated in BMCR 2010.08.43):
1. The enquirer finds his question in a list of 92 numbered questions.
2. He adds a number from 1 to 10, obtained by "some kind of sortition" or chosen "in his mind".
3. He looks up the sum in a list of oracular gods; a concordance turns it into a "decade" number.
4. The answer is the one at the chosen number (1-10) within that decade. There are 1,030 answers.
5. Worked example: question 29 + 7 = 36 → Hephaestus → decade 27.
- Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_Astrampsychi): the questioner "spoke a number from one to ten" while "calling
  on divine inspiration"; the system did not use dice; it survives in "thirteen papyrus fragments of the third to sixth
  centuries". **SS (secondary)**
- Dates: original work 2nd or 3rd c. AD; the ecdosis altera is in 8 papyri of the 3rd-5th c. (5 from Oxyrhynchus); the
  pagan papyri name gods, the medieval manuscripts biblical figures. **SS** (BMCR 2001.10.04)
- Clients (Naether 2010, via van der Horst, BMCR 2010.08.43, bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2010/2010.08.43): "männlich, mittleren
  Alters, gut situiert, verheiratet", Roman elite, often travellers, holders of honorary office. **SS**
- Whether a professional operated it: **ND** in the reviews; Wikipedia's "oracle-monger" wording only implies one.
- Physical device for the 1-10 number: **ND** (spoken, or lot; no dice attested).

### C2. Sortes Sangallenses **SS (edition) / NA (modern editions)**
- Manuscript: St. Gallen, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. Sang. 908, palimpsest. e-codices: lower script "6th-7th centuries";
  the summary calls the Sortes Sangallenses "(6th century)" (e-codices.unifr.ch/en/list/one/csg/0908). BMCR 2006.04.10:
  "datable to c. AD 600". Chaniotis 2006 p. 208 gives the text's date as "third century A.D.?" with a query.
- Structure: answers in groups of twelve (dodecads), numbered I-XII. Winnefeld could not tell whether the dodecads
  themselves carried numbers ("hae dodecades num et ipsae numeris fuerint signatae, adfirmare nequeo"). **SS**: H.
  Winnefeld, *Sortes Sangallenses* (Bonn 1887), intro pp. 3-5; archive.org/details/sortessangallen00winngoog
  (`sources/winnefeld1887_sortes_sangallenses_djvu.txt`).
- **Consultation device, as stated by the first editor**, Winnefeld 1887 p. 11:
  > "quaestiones numeris distinctae responsorum collectioni praemissae erant ut apud Astrampsychum; qui consulere sortes
  > uolebat, alia quam quae continebantur illis formulis, quaerere non potuit; ubi quaestionem protulerat, **aut alea aut
  > sortibus aut simili quadam ratione numerus aliquis ei eligendus fuit inter I et XII positus**; tunc is qui responsa
  > edebat, eam euoluebat dodecadem cuius primo loco quaestioni respondebatur, et secundum numerum a quaerente electum
  > ... tamquam numinis responsum pronuntiabat."

  (my tr.: the numbered questions came before the answers, as in Astrampsychus; after putting his question the enquirer
  had to obtain a number from I to XII "by a die/dice, or by lots, or some similar method"; then "the one who issued the
  answers" opened the dodecad and pronounced the answer "as the response of the deity".) This is Winnefeld's
  reconstruction by analogy with Astrampsychus: the surviving text has no preface and no list of questions. **SS**
  (that this is the editor's view). He leaves the device open: "alea" (die or dice), lots, or similar.
- Winnefeld p. 12 conjectures that the lost preface contained prayers "quae sortes adeunti et responsa edenti dicendae
  essent", i.e. to be said by the enquirer and by the answer-giver. **SS (conjecture by Winnefeld)**
- **Dodecahedral die:** Chaniotis 2006 p. 208 (`sources/chaniotis_text.txt`) reports that F. Heinevetter (1912, p. 52)
  saw the Sortes Sangallenses as consulted with a dodecahedral die. Chaniotis writes "The enquirer used a dodecahedral
  die in order to find the response", citing Dold 1948 p. 10 "for the method of divination". Dornseiff 1925 p. 152
  followed Heinevetter. On the same page Chaniotis adds that none of the practices with numbered answers (astragal
  oracles, Homeromanteia, Astrampsychi, alphabet oracles) "involved the use of a dodecahedron" (p. 209). **SS (as
  reported by Chaniotis)**. I could not read Dold 1948, Meister 1951, Klingshirn 2005, Montero Cartelle or Meyer
  1952: **NA**.
- **Do the 12 answers map to dice sums?** No source I read says so. Winnefeld p. 11 maps them to a chosen number I-XII
  directly. A search-engine summary claimed that modern scholars infer "one throw with two dice or two throws with one
  die" from the I-XII numbering, but I could not trace that sentence to any author: **unverified, do not use**.
  Arithmetic note (**INF**): two cubic dice summed give 2-12, eleven values, so I (=1) cannot come from a two-dice sum;
  two throws of one die give 36 ordered or 21 unordered results, not 12. A single 12-faced die, or a draw from 12 lots,
  gives I-XII directly. So the I-XII numbering fits a 12-outcome device or a lot draw better than a two-dice sum; this
  arithmetic is mine, not a published argument.
- Clientele (Naether 2010 via BMCR 2010.08.43): different from Astrampsychi, "more rural and dependent". **SS**
  The Latin text concerns curiales, decurions, aediles, legacies, partnerships (e.g. Winnefeld's dodecad XXX, codex p. 197:
  "si uis decem primus esse" [OCR reads "decempnmus"], "aeris aedilis", "curialis"). That is municipal life in a Latin-speaking province. **SS (text)**; which
  province: **ND**.
- Related: Winnefeld p. 12 also cites a Munich lot collection (Clm 14846; Thurneysen 1885, "Altirische und brittische
  Wörter in einer Sortes-Sammlung der Münchener Bibliothek") "in qua trium alearum iactu de rebus futuris quaerebatur et
  uni cuique numerorum combinationi paucae atque certae respondebant sententiae". That is three dice with fixed answers
  per combination, in a medieval manuscript with Old Irish and British words. **SS**; date of that text: **ND** here.

## D. Astragal and alphabet oracles of SW Asia Minor (2nd - early 3rd c. AD)

### D1. Five-astragal oracle **SS**
Sources: Prikhodko 2020, RSUH/RGGU Bulletin, Lit. Theory... 2020 no. 10(2): 246-256, doi 10.28995/2686-7249-2020-10-246-256
(history.rsuh.ru/jour/article/download/1534/1350; `sources/prikhodko2020_rsuh.txt`); Costanza 2023, *I Quaderni del
Ramo d'Oro on-line* 15: 21-42 (qro.unisi.it/frontend/sites/default/files/02 Costanza Venus and Astragals QRO.pdf;
`sources/costanza_venus_astragals_qro.txt`); both rely on Nollé 2007, *Kleinasiatische Losorakel* (Vestigia 57) and
Graf 2005 (in *Mantike* 51-97), which I could not read (**NA**).
1. Device: five astragals per throw. Costanza p. 24: "We may infer that the enquirer rolled five astragals with each
   throw". Prikhodko p. 248: the enquirer made a throw of five astragals. **SS**. Whether one astragal thrown five times
   was also allowed: **ND** in what I read.
2. Values: an astragal can land on only one of four sides (the two small rounded ends are too small); each side has its
   own shape, so "even without additional signs" the sides carried fixed values 1, 3, 4, 6, with opposite sides adding
   to 7 (Prikhodko p. 248; Costanza pp. 22-23, citing Pollux 9.100). **SS**. Unmarked, shape-coded faces were read as
   numbers. Last 2000 (ZPE 130: 248; `sources/zpe130_248.txt`) says the same: "Knucklebones required no marks of value on
   them, since their sides were naturally distinguishable." **SS**
3. Outcomes: five astragals give 56 combinations, so 56 answers (Prikhodko p. 248). Sums run from 5 to 30. Combinations
   with the same sum are ordered by side (Costanza p. 24 n. 13, citing Graf 2005 pp. 62-63). **SS**
4. Reading: each strophe has five lines. Line 1 gives the values thrown, their sum and the god's name, e.g. "11113 7
   [of] Athena Areia"; line 2 describes the throw in a hexameter; lines 3-5 are the oracle (Prikhodko pp. 248-250).
   Costanza p. 26 quotes a Greek line with "πέμπτος τέσσαρα πίπτων" ("the fifth falling [as] four"). The enquirer added
   up the values and found the strophe (Costanza p. 24). **SS**
5. Which face counted (top or bottom): **ND** explicitly. The texts speak of how each astragal "falls". **INF**:
   conventionally the upward side was named, but I found no statement of it.
6. Gods: each combination is linked to a deity (Zeus, Athena, Apollo, Hermes, Aphrodite, Tyche, Nemesis, Moirai, Isis,
   Sarapis, Zeus Ammon ...; Costanza p. 24, citing Nollé 2007 pp. 106-110). **SS**
7. Setting: inscribed on tall (about 2 m) rectangular pillars, set up in city centres, often on the agora, "since the
   oracle was consulted above all on commercial questions". Some pillars carried a statue of Hermes (Kremna, Kitanaura),
   and Perge's is headed "Ἑρμοῦ ἀστραγαλομαντεῖον" (Prikhodko pp. 249-251, citing Nollé 2007 pp. 70, 86, 94). **SS**
8. Donors: a former agoranomos at Termessos set one up near the theatre. At **Kremna** (a Roman colony) the dedication is
   in Latin: on the order of L. Fabricius Longus, his wife Vibia Tatia and daughter Fabricia Lucilla dedicated a Mercury
   (Prikhodko p. 251, citing Nollé 2007 p. 70). **SS**
9. Number and dates: 18 five-astragal oracles known (17 in Nollé 2007 pp. 31-103, 192-211, plus İplikçioğlu 2021). Seven-
   astragal oracles (120 strophes) survive only as fragments of three (Nollé 2007 pp. 211-221). All date to the 2nd -
   early 3rd c. AD and come from Pisidia, Phrygia, Pamphylia, Kabalis, E Lycia and W Cilicia (Prikhodko pp. 248-249).
   Costanza p. 26 gives Kremna as Hadrianic (117-138). **SS**
10. Clients (Graf 2005 via Nice, BMCR 2006.04.10): "primarily businessmen who tried to regulate the forces of chance";
    they "prayed to Hermes", who relayed Apollo's words. **SS**
11. Who threw: the enquirer (Costanza and Prikhodko); no priest is attested at the pillar. **SS** (enquirer);
    professional: **ND**.
12. Throwing surface (ground, board or tray at the pillar): **ND**.
- A further example: a 56-answer astragal-oracle monument from Kibyra, funded by a benefactor together with a Hermes
  statue (popular report: ancient-origins.net/news-history-archaeology/knucklebone-oracle-kibyra-00103255; primary
  publication not read). **SS (popular source, low weight)**

### D2. Alphabet oracles **SS**
Prikhodko 2024, *Vestnik Mosk. Univ. Ser. 9 Philol.* 2024 no. 5: 170-181 (vestnik.philol.msu.ru/issues/VMU_9_Philol__2024_05_13.pdf;
`sources/vmu2024_05_13_alphabet_oracles.txt`), p. 172: twelve inscriptions of the 2nd - early 3rd c. (Nollé 2007

Points that bear on H1 (all **INF**):
- The only explicit procedure in which thrown objects come to rest and are **read as they lie** is the tali or astragal
  type (Suet. Tib. 14.3; Asia Minor pillars). In those, **unmarked shape-coded faces** were standard (Prikhodko p. 248;
  Last 2000). This is the closest ancient parallel for reading an unlettered dodecahedron by face shape or size. But the
  answer text was always held elsewhere (pillar, book) and keyed by numbers.
- Every known oracle with 12 or 10 numbered answers (Sangallenses, Astrampsychi) needs a book and an operator. A
  12-outcome device fits the Sangallenses I-XII numbering (Winnefeld's "aut alea aut sortibus" leaves the device open;
  Heinevetter's dodecahedral die is a 1912 conjecture, reported by Chaniotis 2006 p. 208). No source ties a hollow
  bronze dodecahedron to it.
- The NW-European sources (Tacitus, Caesar) describe slips and selection by hand on a cloth, done by a priest,
  paterfamilias or matres familiae, three times. They describe no thrown polyhedron and no reading of a top face.
- Soldiers using lot divination in the NW provinces: **ND** (no source found in this pass).
- Roman law (CTh 9.16) never names lots or dice; dice divination is caught only by the general 357 ban (9.16.4).
