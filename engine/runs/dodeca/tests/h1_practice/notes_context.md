## A. Dice towers (turricula / pyrgus), cups, boards, dice

A1. Vettweiss-Froitzheim tower (LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn). SOURCE-SUPPORTED, with conflicts noted.
- The front inscription reads PICTOS VICTOS / HOSTIS DELETA / LVDITE SECVRI ("the Picts are defeated, the enemy destroyed, play
  in safety"). The top of the other three faces reads VTERE FELIX VIVAS. Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vettweiss-Froitzheim_Dice_Tower;
  vici.org/vici/16354; the EDH record HD018228 (edh-www.adw.uni-heidelberg.de/edh/inschrift/HD018228), seen only as a search
  snippet because the page itself was bot-blocked. The EDH snippet gives the find year as 1983, the date as AD 301-400, and Bonn
  as the location.
- Find year conflict: Wikipedia says 1985; EDH and roger-pearse.com (2016-02-03 post) say 1983.
- Context: a villa site (vici.org; Wikipedia, which says "the site was a villa", Germania Inferior near Cologne).
- Construction: copper-alloy plates; an open top; three levels of baffles; dice leave at the base down a little flight of steps;
  three bells over the exit, one surviving (Wikipedia). Pearse adds bronze, found as plates and reconstructed, 4th century by its
  lattice style, originally with battlements and four pine cones, and dolphins flanking a bell.
- Size: 22.5 cm high, 9.5 x 9.5 cm. This is SOFT: Pearse quotes it from a forum post.
- Main publication: H. G. Horn, "Si per me misit, nil nisi vota feret. Ein römischer Spielturm aus Froitzheim", Bonner Jahrbücher
  189 (1989) 139-160 (cited by Cobbett 2020, sources/Lucerna-58.pdf p. 13). NO ACCESS: the Heidelberg journal page
  journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/bjb/article/view/66190/59071 is behind an Anubis bot check.
- The tower's motto is Martial 14.16, line 2 (see A3).

A2. Other towers. SOURCE-SUPPORTED: Cobbett 2008 abstract; Cobbett 2020 (Lucerna 58 pp. 10-13, sources/Lucerna-58.pdf).
- Cobbett 2008, "A Dice Tower from Richborough", Britannia 39 (2008) 219-235, doi 10.3815/006811308785917169. The abstract says
  the dice tower was "an everyday object in the Roman world, but up to now only two towers, from Germany and Egypt, have been
  found". Bone box-casings from Richborough fort are reinterpreted as a tower, and its rosettes are linked to Duodecim Scripta.
- Cobbett 2020 gives the following list:
  - Bronze towers with inscriptions: Froitzheim; Chaves, Portugal (from the Aquae Flaviae spa baths, which collapsed in the 390s
    or 400s); and a fragment from Palencia, Spain (La Olmeda villa bath-house).
  - Wood and ivory, complete: Qustul, Egypt, a grave gift.
  - Wood and bone:
    - Richborough: the Stone Fort inner ditch, 4th century; separate staircase plates from the 4th-century bath-house and from
      4th-century levels. Cobbett says this may represent as many as three towers.
    - Colliton Park, Dorchester: a 4th-century townhouse; staircase plates 71 x 44 and 68 x 48 mm; the tower reconstructed at
      about 190 mm high and 85 mm deep.
    - Piddington villa, 4th century.
    - Great Casterton villa: a stokehole destroyed after AD 392.
- Cobbett 2020 also says: "The location of a villa seems to fit in with other places where dice towers have been found", and bath
  houses are the other recurrent context.
- Chaourse: NO DATA. No source links a dice tower to Chaourse, and it is not in Cobbett's 2020 list.
- Literary sources for towers, as cited by Cobbett 2020:
  - Sidonius Apollinaris, Ep. 8.12.5: "tessera frequens eboratis resultatura pyrgorum gradibus", quoted via
    sidonapol.org/cobbett-on-dice-towers.
  - Ausonius, Commemoratio Professorum Burdigalensium 1.28: "excisi per cava buxa gradus". Ausonius was a 4th-century Gallic
    (Bordeaux) author.
  - Agathias, Anthologia Palatina 9.482.23-4: "the hidden staircase of the wooden pyrgus".
  - INFERENCE: two of these three literary witnesses are Gallic (Sidonius at Clermont/Lyon, Ausonius at Bordeaux).
- Isidore, Etymologiae 18.60-61 (thelatinlibrary.com/isidore/18.shtml): "Tabula luditur pyrgo, calculis tesserisque" and "Pyrgus
  dictus quod per eum tesserae pergant, sive quod turris speciem habeat." SOURCE-SUPPORTED.
- How a tower worked: dice dropped in the top, tumbled down internal steps or baffles, and came out at the base. SOURCE-SUPPORTED
  for construction (Wikipedia; Cobbett 2020, staircase plates; Isidore "per eum tesserae pergant"). NO DATA for an ancient text
  describing a separate tray.

A3. Martial and Juvenal. SOURCE-SUPPORTED (thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart14.shtml; thelatinlibrary.com/juvenal/14.shtml and
/1.shtml).
- Martial 14.16 Turricula: "Quae scit compositos manus improba mittere talos, / Si per me misit, nil nisi vota feret." The tower
  is presented as a guard against a cheating hand. Note that it is used here with tali (knucklebones).
- Martial 14.14 Tali eborei: "Cum steterit nullus vultu tibi talus eodem, ...", the "Venus" throw with all faces different.
  INFERENCE: "steterit ... vultu" means the face a knucklebone comes to rest showing, i.e. reading the face it shows, presumably
  the top. NO DATA for an explicit ancient statement that dice were read on the top face.
- Martial 14.15 Tesserae; 14.17 Tabula lusoria: "Hac mihi bis seno numeratur tessera puncto". Martial 14.1.3: "moto spectare
  fritillo", the shaken dice-cup.
- Juvenal 14.4-5: "si damnosa senem iuvat alea, ludit et heres / bullatus parvoque eadem movet arma fritillo". Juvenal 1.89-90:
  "...neque enim loculis comitantibus itur / ad casum tabulae, posita sed luditur arca".
- Smith's Dictionary (1875; penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Fritillus.html) treats fritillus,
  turricula and pyrgus as one cylindrical box with internal gradus. INFERENCE: this is an older conflation. Modern work (Cobbett,
  Horn) treats the tower as a separate device.
- Isidore 18.63-66: tesserae are named for being square. Throws are named by number (unio ... senio; later canis = 1). Skilled
  players can "compose" a throw: "Iactus tesserarum ita a peritis aleatoribus conponitur ut adferat quod voluerit".
  SOURCE-SUPPORTED.

A4. Roman dice in the NW provinces. SOURCE-SUPPORTED: Eerkens & de Voogt 2022, Archaeol. Anthropol. Sci. 14:134,
doi 10.1007/s12520-022-01599-y (sources/eerkens_devoogt_2022_roman_dice.pdf).
- Sample: 28 well-dated Roman-period dice from 13 Dutch sites, 10 from north and 18 from south of the limes. They include the
  forts at Valkenburg, Vechten, Woerden and Roomburg, the Castra at Nijmegen, and Ulpia Noviomagus.
- Materials: 21 bone or antler, 4 ceramic, 3 bronze.
- Dimensions (Table 1): mostly 10-18 mm per side, with a range of 5.6 mm (Dieserstraat) to 27.1 x 26.1 x 18.5 mm (Ulpia
  Noviomagus).
- Pips are "dot and circle" (D-R) or "double circle and dot" (D-R-R). INFERENCE: concentric-ring marks are the normal pip style
  on dice in this region. This is only a stylistic parallel to the ringed holes on the dodecahedra.
- 24 of the 28 are visibly non-cubic. 80-90% of Roman dice are asymmetrical (Schmid 1978, legionary camp in Switzerland; Swift
  2017b). The most lopsided die raises one pair of faces to about 43%.
- The authors argue that users saw outcomes as "fate" rather than probability, so unequal odds were tolerated (pp. 9-10). They
  name divination as one die use, citing Graf 2005 and Klingshirn 2005.
- INFERENCE for H1: in this culture an unequal-odds 12-face object would not have been rejected for unfairness.
- Counts of dice at Vindolanda or other forts: NO DATA. Searches gave only gaming boards and counters; Vindolanda has the largest
  collection of gaming boards in Britain, per romanarmymuseum.com.

## B. Laws against divination

B1. Codex Theodosianus 9.16, De maleficis et mathematicis et ceteris similibus. SOURCE-SUPPORTED from the Latin text at
ancientrome.ru/ius/library/codex/theod/liber09.htm (Mommsen-Meyer dates in brackets).
- 9.16.1, Constantine to Maximus, AD 319 (Feb 1, Rome): a haruspex may not enter another person's house. Burning for the
  haruspex, deportation for whoever summoned him. Public rites stay allowed.
- 9.16.2, ad populum, 319 (May 15): the same ban on haruspices and priests entering private houses. "adite aras publicas".
- 9.16.3, Constantine to Bassus, Aquileia (consulship of Crispus and Constantine Caesars; year not printed on the page):
  - Magic against health or chastity is punished.
  - Healing remedies and rural weather charms "innocenter adhibita" are exempt.
  - Included in the Breviary as brev. 9.13.1.
- 9.16.4, Constantius and Julian ad populum, Milan, "VIII kal. febr. ... Constantio a. IX et Iuliano caes. II coss." This is
  25 Jan 357: the next law, 9.16.5 under the same consuls, is printed as 357.
  - Text: "Nemo haruspicem consulat aut mathematicum, nemo hariolum. Augurum et vatum prava confessio conticescat. Chaldaei ac
    magi et ceteri, quos maleficos ... vulgus appellat, nec ad hanc partem aliquid moliantur. Sileat omnibus perpetuo divinandi
    curiositas." The penalty is death by the sword.
  - The Breviary interpretatio (brev. 9.13.2) reads: whoever "pro curiositate futurorum" consults
    invokers of demons, divini or haruspices "capite punietur".
- 9.16.5, 357 (Dec 4): magicians who disturb the elements.
- 9.16.6, to Taurus PPO, Rimini, 358 (Jul 5): even dignitaries may be tortured if caught at court practising magic, haruspicy,
  augury, astrology, "narrandis somniis occultans artem aliquam divinandi", "aut certe aliquid horum simile".
- 9.16.7, Valentinian and Valens, 364: no nocturnal "nefarias preces aut magicos apparatus aut sacrificia funesta"
  (brev. 9.13.3).
- 9.16.8, to Modestus PPO, Constantinople, 370/373: "Cesset mathematicorum tractatus". Capital punishment, public or private, for
  teacher and learner alike.
- 9.16.9, ad senatum, issued at TRIER (Treviris), 29 May 371: "Haruspicinam ego nullum cum maleficiorum causis habere consortium
  iudico ... Nec haruspicinam reprehendimus, sed nocenter exerceri vetamus." Haruspicy is tolerated unless used to harm.
- 9.16.10, 371: magic charges against senators go to the urban prefecture.
- 9.16.11, 389: anyone who catches a maleficus must bring him to court.
- 9.16.12, Honorius and Theodosius to Caecilianus PPO, Ravenna, 409 (Feb 1): mathematici are expelled from Rome and all cities
  unless they burn their books before the bishops and convert. Otherwise deportation.
- KEY POINT: none of 9.16.1-12 names sortes, sortilegi or dice. The named targets are haruspices, mathematici, harioli, augures,
  vates, Chaldaei, magi, malefici, dream-interpreters, and "aliquid horum simile" (9.16.6). INFERENCE: lot-casting would fall
  only under the catch-all and the general "divinandi curiositas" (9.16.4).
- INFERENCE: 9.16.3, 9.16.4 and 9.16.7 passed into the Breviary of Alaric (506), so these bans stayed in force in Visigothic
  south-west Gaul.

B2. CTh 16.10, pagan cult (same site, liber16.htm). SOURCE-SUPPORTED.
- 16.10.1 (320/1): haruspices may still be consulted publicly about lightning strikes on public buildings. Domestic sacrifices
  are banned.
- 16.10.4 (346/354?): temples closed; sacrifice is capital.
- 16.10.6 (356, Milan): death for sacrificing or worshipping images.
- 16.10.7 (381): proscription for anyone who becomes "incertorum consultorem" through forbidden sacrifices.
- 16.10.9 (385): no sacrifice to read the liver or entrails, or to learn the future, under heavier penalty.
- 16.10.10, to Albinus, Milan, 24 Feb 391: no sacrifice, no visiting shrines; fines on judges.
- 16.10.11, to Evagrius and Romanus, Aquileia, 16 Jun 391: the same, for Egypt.
- 16.10.12, to Rufinus PPO, Constantinople, 8 Nov 392: a total ban, including private household cult (lar, genius, penates),
  incense and garlands. 16.10.12.1 says consulting "spirantia exta" is to be treated like treason, "etiamsi nihil contra salutem
  principum aut de salute quaesierit".
- None of these names lots either.

B3. Earlier jurists. SOURCE-SUPPORTED.
- Pauli Sententiae 5.21, De vaticinatoribus et mathematicis (ancientrome.ru/ius/library/paul/paul5.htm):
  - 5.21.1: vaticinatores "qui se deo plenos adsimulant" are beaten and expelled.
  - 5.21.3: "Qui de salute principis vel summa rei publicae mathematicos hariolos haruspices vaticinatores consulit, cum eo qui
    responderit capite punitur".
  - 5.21.4: it is better to abstain from divination and even from "ipsa scientia eiusque libris". Slaves who consult about their
    masters' health are crucified.
- Ulpian, De officio proconsulis 7, in Collatio 15.2 (ancientrome.ru/ius/library/collatio/collatio.htm):
  - 15.2.1 cites a senatus consultum of AD 17 that interdicted mathematici, Chaldaei, arioli "et ceteri".
  - 15.2.3: punishment varies "pro mensura consultationis". Questions about the emperor's health are capital; questions "de sua
    suorumque" are punished more lightly.
  - 15.2.4: "Extat denique decretum divi Pii ad Pacatum legatum provinciae Lugdunensis". Antoninus Pius ruled on this matter for
    the governor of Gallia Lugdunensis, a NW province, in the 2nd century.
  - 15.1.1 and 15.1.5 (the Mosaic law in the Collatio) explicitly include lots: "divinus apud quem sortes tollas"; "auguria et
    sortes et divinationes audiebant".
- INFERENCE for H1: private questions about oneself and one's household were the lowest-risk category before 357.

B4. Enforcement. SOURCE-SUPPORTED except where marked.
- Scythopolis trials (Ammianus 19.12, penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/19*.html):
  - 19.12.3-5: written questions left at the oracle of Besa at Abydos were sent to Constantius and triggered treason trials.
  - 19.12.14: people were condemned for wearing an amulet against quartan fever or for passing a tomb at evening.
  - Ammianus gives no year in that chapter; the trials were in the East.
- Ammianus 29.1.29-32: the Antioch letter-oracle.
  - A laurel tripod carried a round metal plate with the 24 letters engraved on the rim. A ring on a linen thread spelled out
    ΘΕΟ(Δ), which led to treason trials.
  - INFERENCE: this is an inscribed metal divination device, so letters were used when an answer had to be spelled out.
- Ammianus 21.1.6-14 and 21.2.4-5: Julian in Gaul (Vienne, 360/361) secretly practised haruspicy and augury while posing as a
  Christian.
  - Ammianus' list of legitimate divination methods (21.1.8-12) covers augury, haruspicy, inspired utterance, Sibyls, thunder,
    lightning, stars and dreams. It does NOT include lots.
  - INFERENCE: even a Caesar in Gaul concealed divination after the 357 law.
- Priscillian:
  - Sulpicius Severus, Chronica 2.50.8 (thelatinlibrary.com/sulpiciusseveruschron2.html): "convictumque maleficii" before the
    prefect Euodius at Trier. 2.51.2-3: Priscillian, Felicissimus, Armenius, Latronianus and Euchrotia were executed ("gladio
    perempti").
  - The date 385 and the place Trier are from en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priscillian (Burrus 1995; Hughes 1979).
  - INFERENCE: this is a capital magic conviction at the western imperial residence in the NW.
- Gallic church councils against lot divination:
  - The Council of Vannes (465) condemned the "sortes sanctorum" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_Sanctorum; Klingshirn 2002,
    JECS 10.1:77-130). The canon number (16) and the Latin text were NOT verified; Klingshirn 2002 was NO ACCESS.
  - Agde (506) and Orléans (511) are named only in a search snippet. NO DATA verified.
  - INFERENCE: lot-casting persisted in Gaul after the pagan bans, in Christianised form.
- Prosecutions in the NW for lots or dice divination specifically: NO DATA.

## C. Astrology, zodiac and twelve-fold schemes in the NW provinces

C1. The Grand (Vosges) zodiacal diptychs. SOURCE-SUPPORTED: Musée d'Archéologie nationale, "L'objet du mois: Les tablettes
zodiacales de Grand" (sources/MAN_ODM_grand.pdf, pp. 2-3); isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/time-cosmos/objects/astrologer-zodiacal-boards.
- Found 1967-68 in a well at Grand. They had been deliberately broken and dumped at 8 m depth with 2nd- and 3rd-century domestic
  refuse. Pottery dates the well fill to the 3rd century.
- They are two ivory diptychs with covers, painted red (cinnabar), black and yellow, and gilded.
- Design: Sol and Luna in the centre; the 12 zodiac signs, each topped by 5 Greek letters; 36 Egyptianising decan figures with
  names "en copte"; four winds.
- Holders: Diptych A is at the MAN (Saint-Germain-en-Laye); Diptych B is at the Musée départemental, Épinal (ISAW gives inventory
  2013.0.217, 18.6 x 28 cm, "2nd century CE").
- Publication: J.-H. Abry (ed.), Les tablettes astrologiques de Grand (Vosges) et l'astrologie en Gaule romaine (Table ronde 18
  March 1992; Lyon-Paris 1993). It includes Abry, "Les tablettes de Grand: mode d'emploi à travers les écrits des astrologues",
  pp. 141-160 (ptolemaeus.badw.de/astrobibl/section/D).
- INFERENCE: professional horoscopic astrology with a 12-sign board was practised in a NW sanctuary town.

C2. Mithraic zodiac reliefs. SOURCE-SUPPORTED.
- Housesteads, Hadrian's Wall (found 1822, early 3rd century, Great North Museum, CIMRM 860): Mithras rising from an egg inside
  "an egg-shaped zodiac representing the cosmos"
  (english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/mithras-and-eastern-religion/).
- Trier, Altbachtal (found 1928, RLM Trier, CIMRM 985, TNMM 312): rock-birth of Mithras holding a zodiac ring with six signs,
  Aries to Virgo (mithraeum.eu/monument/312, per search summary).
- Osterburken: a zodiac is NOT confirmed (NO DATA).
- Trier Monnus mosaic: twelve MONTH panels and four seasons, not zodiac signs (search summary of RLM Trier materials).

C3. Jupiter columns and the week-gods stone. SOURCE-SUPPORTED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_Column (Bauchhenß 1976; Bauchhenß &
Noelke 1981; Woolf 2001).
- 2nd-3rd centuries, in the Germanic provinces, with some in Gaul and Britain.
- The Wochengötterstein shows the seven day-gods (Saturn, Sol, Luna, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus), per a search summary of
  Stuttgart museum records.
- INFERENCE: this is a 7-fold planetary-week scheme, not a 12-fold one.

C4. Coligny calendar. SOURCE-SUPPORTED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coligny_calendar, which carries 2026 reliability tags.
- Found 1897 at Coligny (Ain) with fragments of a bronze Mars-like statue. A bronze plaque, 73 fragments, originally about
  134.8 x 78 cm. End of the 2nd century AD.
- Gaulish language in Latin letters. A 5-year lunisolar cycle. Months are marked MAT/ANM, i.e. auspicious and inauspicious.
  Held at the Lugdunum museum.
- INFERENCE: a native Gaulish system of lucky and unlucky days was still inscribed in the Roman period.

C5. Twelve-fold divine and astrological schemes. SOURCE-SUPPORTED (Latin Library texts).
- Ennius' twelve gods, in Apuleius, De deo Socratis 2: "Iuno, Vesta, Minerva, Ceres, Diana, Venus, Mars, Mercurius, Iovis,
  Neptunus, Vulcanus, Apollo".
- Varro, RR 1.1 (conventionally 1.1.4) mentions the twelve dei Consentes "quorum imagines ad forum auratae stant, sex mares et
  feminae totidem".
- Manilius 2.439-447 (Latin Library line numbers) assigns the same twelve as tutelary gods of the twelve signs: Pallas-Aries,
  Venus-Taurus, Phoebus-Gemini, Mercury-Cancer, Jupiter-Leo, Ceres-Virgo, Vulcan-Libra, Mars-Scorpio, Diana-Sagittarius,
  Vesta-Capricorn, Juno-Aquarius, Neptune-Pisces. INFERENCE: twelve gods, twelve signs and twelve faces map one to one.
- Manilius 2.856-967 is NOT a twelve-place scheme. The section ends "[cui parti nomen posuit, qui condidit artem, / octotropos]",
  i.e. an EIGHT-place system. This corrects the task premise.
- Manilius 3.67-163 sets out twelve domains of life called "sortes" ("horum operum sortes ad singula signa locavit", 3.75) and
  "athla vocant Grai ... in genera et partes bis sex divisa". INFERENCE: a Latin twelve-fold set of life-topics called "lots"
  exists. It is astrological allotment, not thrown lots.
- Dodecatropos and Firmicus Maternus (the twelve places): NO DATA verified. No Latin text was reachable and the search budget was
  exhausted.
