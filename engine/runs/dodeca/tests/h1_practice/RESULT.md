# H1 test: the hollow dodecahedron as a thrown lot / divination die

Tags: **SS** = SOURCE-SUPPORTED (source and page given); **INF** = INFERENCE (mine, from the sources named);
**ND** = NO DATA (looked for, not found); **NA** = source known but not read. "Late parallel" = medieval or
early-modern evidence, not evidence for Roman practice.
Supporting files in this folder: `notes_oracles.md` (lot procedures, Latin texts quoted in full),
`notes_context.md` (dice towers, laws, astrology in the NW provinces), `distinct.py` / `distinct_out.txt` (how many
faces could be told apart by hole size), `sources/` (local copies of every primary text and article used).
FACT numbers refer to `engine/runs/dodeca/evidence.md`.

## 0. Bottom line

- **For H1 (SS):** a 4th-century dodecahedral die from the same broad region exists. It is the Geneva piece: silver
  over lead, 35 mm, 297 g, with the twelve zodiac names. Its publisher reads it as a divination die, partly because of
  wear on its edges and faces (Cervi-Brunier 1985, p. 155). Twelve-faced dice certainly existed in the Roman world:
  Crete, Patrae, Antioch, Egypt (Chaniotis 2006, pp. 207-209). One of them lay in a rich grave beside two silver
  astragals and rings with prophetic imagery (Patrae). Unmarked, shape-coded faces were standard on the most common
  ancient lot, the astragal. And a 12-answer book of lots in Latin, the Sortes Sangallenses, needed a number from I
  to XII. Its first editor thought that number came "aut alea aut sortibus" (by die, by lots, or similar).
- **Against H1 (SS + INF):**
  - Every known Roman 12-faced or polyhedral die, the Geneva one included, carries letters, numerals or names. So do
    Gallo-Roman special dice (the Autun/Trier/London word dice). The hollow pieces carry none (FACT 19).
  - On the three measured hollow pieces, hole sizes give at most about 6-9 visually distinct values, not 12.
  - At Jublains the two hemispheres repeat the same size pattern, which makes faces pairwise alike.
  - The one corpus specialist quoted on the point rejects dice: the knobs "impede their ability to roll", and the
    pieces are "much more delicate than people realize".
  - Wear is reported as rare on the hollow pieces (FACT 20), while the Geneva die shows it.
  - No NW-European text describes a thrown polyhedral lot. Tacitus and Caesar describe marked slips drawn by hand.
- **Net:** H1 is possible but weakly supported. The strongest pillar, the Geneva die, is a different object class:
  solid, small, inscribed, unknobbed and unperforated. Its own publisher says it is not directly related to the
  hollow series (p. 154).

## 1. Reconstruction of a consultation (as far as the sources allow)

No source describes the use of a hollow dodecahedron (FACT 18), so every step below is assembled from **analogues**.
Each step says which analogue supports it and whether it can be transferred.

| # | Step | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who consults.** A private person (head of household, a woman of standing) or a professional "answer-giver" with a book. | INF from SS analogues | Tacitus, *Germ.* 10: "sacerdos civitatis" for public questions, "ipse pater familiae" for private ones. Caesar, *BG* 1.50: Germanic *matres familiae* decide "sortibus et vaticinationibus". Winnefeld 1887 p. 11 (Sortes Sangallenses): "is qui responsa edebat", an operator who reads out the answer. Astragal pillars: the enquirer throws, and no priest is attested (Prikhodko 2020 p. 248; Costanza 2023 p. 24). Clients of the Sortes Astrampsychi were well-off married men; those of the Sangallenses "more rural and dependent" (Naether 2010 via BMCR 2010.08.43). Rich graves of both sexes with dodecahedra (FACT 15, 17) fit private owners. Soldiers diced: dice come from the forts at Valkenburg, Vechten, Woerden, Roomburg and Nijmegen (Eerkens & de Voogt 2022, Table 1). But no source shows soldiers using lots for divination in the NW (ND). Before 357, Roman law punished questions "de sua suorumque" (about oneself and one's household) more lightly than questions about the emperor (Ulpian in *Collatio* 15.2.3). A thin bronze shell (0.6-1.6 mm wall, FACT 7) of up to 580 g suits a cloth or sand better than stone. |

Hitchens (Boston Globe, 30 Apr 2024): the knobs "impede their ability to roll" (journalist's paraphrase). That is an argument against free rolling, not against coming to rest. |

Sortes Astrampsychi: question + number 1-10, then a table of gods, then a decade and an answer (BMCR 2001.10.04). Sangallenses: a number I-XII picks the answer within a dodecad (Winnefeld 1887 p. 11). Dodechedron: house + question + die value lead through a 12×12 table to a leaf and verse (1613 preface). **INF:** the hollow piece needs an external key of the same kind, because its faces carry no text. No such key survives (ND). |
| 8 | **Repetition and confirmation.** Three consultations; a "no" closes the matter for the day; a "yes" is checked by another method. | SS (NW analogues) | Caesar *BG* 1.53: "ter sortibus consultum". Tacitus *Germ.* 10: "ter singulos tollit"; "si prohibuerunt, nulla de eadem re in eundem diem consultatio; sin permissum, auspiciorum adhuc fides exigitur". |
| 9 | **Afterwards.** The device is kept as a valued personal object, buried with the owner or hoarded. | SS (analogue); INF (transfer) | Patrae: a rock-crystal dodecahedral die in a young man's grave with two silver astragals, a silver crepitaculum and rings showing a prophet's head and a tripod (Chaniotis 2006 p. 210, citing Papapostolou 1983). Hollow dodecahedra occur in rich graves and hoards (FACT 13, 15). |

**What cannot be reconstructed (ND):**
- the answer list;
- whether top or bottom face was read on any Roman device;
- the number of throws per question for a polyhedral die;
- any NW-provincial instance of a thrown polyhedral oracle.

## 2. What each feature would do under H1, with the evidence

### 2.1 Twelve faces = twelve outcomes
- **SS:** Twelve-faced numbered dice existed.
  - Chaniotis 2006 pp. 207-209 lists examples: Germany (bronze, Latin numerals; von Cohausen 1879 p. 393, van Vleuten
    1876 p. 193; NA), Antioch (red stone, Greek numerals; Heinevetter 1912 p. 51; NA) and Egypt (black stone, Greek
    numerals; Perdrizet 1930 no. 15; NA).
  - Idaean Cave, Crete (rock crystal, 2 cm, numbers 1-10, 15, 20; c. 1st c. AD).
  - Patrae (rock crystal, grave, 1st c. AD, numbers 1, 2, 3, 6, 7?, 10, 11, 15, 18, 20, 30, 40).
  - Petrie Museum UC 44998, "a steatite pentagonal dodecahedron with Greek numerals", Roman period (Minas-Nerpel 2007
    p. 145 n. 19).
- **Caution (SS, with INF):**
  - Chaniotis's German examples rest on van Vleuten 1876 and von Cohausen 1879. Noll ("Seltsame Spielwürfel", BJb,
    pp. [567]-569) cites the same two works when discussing "Roman" multi-faced letter dice: 26 faces, of which 12
    carry 1-12, 6 carry letter pairs (NG, NH, SZ, LS, TA ...) and 8 are blank.
  - Noll shows that these are post-antique toys. The letter pairs were explained in a Vienna inventory as "Nimm,
    Setze, Lasse ...", and such pieces were made up to the 20th c. by the Serpentinstein-Gesellschaft at Zöblitz
    (Saxony).
  - Chaniotis's 18-faced German die, with 12 numbered and 6 lettered sides (van Vleuten 1876 p. 192), has the same
    numbering scheme (INF).
  - So the German bronze 12-faced die is **unverified** as Roman until van Vleuten and von Cohausen are checked (NA).
- **Twelve as a divinatory set (SS):**
  - zodiac signs (Geneva die);
  - the twelve tutelary gods of the signs: Manilius 2.439-447, "Lanigerum Pallas, Taurum Cytherea tuetur ...
    agnoscitque suos Neptunus in aethere Pisces", followed at 2.448 by "hinc quoque magna tibi venient momenta
    futuri";
  - the same twelve gods as the dii consentes: Ennius' list in Apuleius, *De deo Socratis* 2; Varro *RR* 1.1.4,
    "sex mares et feminae totidem" (`notes_context.md` C5);
  - Manilius 3.67-163: twelve life-domains (athla) that Manilius himself calls *sortes*, "horum operum sortes ad
    singula signa locavit" (3.75). This is astrological allotment, not a thrown lot;
  - Isidore *Etym.* 8.9.23: genethliaci "per duodecim caeli signa describunt";
  - the twelve answers per dodecad of the Sortes Sangallenses (Winnefeld 1887).
- **Dodecahedron = zodiac (SS):** Plutarch, *Plat. Quaest.* 5.1 (Mor. 1003c-d): each of the 12 pentagons "consists of
  thirty of the first scalene triangles. Therefore it seems to resemble both the Zodiac and the year". Cervi-Brunier
  1985 p. 154 quotes the same passage for the Geneva die. Late parallel: the *Dodechedron of Fortune* preface (1613)
  repeats the 12 × 30 = 360 argument and says "I have seen the play called the Dodechedron, marked with the twelve
  Signs of the zodiac".
- **Correction to the brief (SS):** Manilius 2.856-967 is not a twelve-place scheme. It ends "[cui parti nomen
  posuit, qui condidit artem, / octotropos]", an eight-place system (Latin Library text, line printed near 2.969).
  The twelve-place *dodecatropos* (Firmicus Maternus etc.) was not verified here (ND).
- **Unknown (ND):** which twelve meanings a hollow piece would have carried. The Geneva die is the only
  zodiac-labelled one, and no hollow piece is labelled.

### 2.2 Hole sizes as face codes
- **What H1 needs (INF):** about 12 distinguishable faces, or at least a stable smaller set of outcomes.
- **Visual limit (SS):** humans reliably see a size difference of about 5% or more between two objects
  (Eerkens & de Voogt 2022 p. 1, citing Eerkens 2000 and Norwich 1983).
- **Applied to the three objects with per-hole data** (derived, `distinct_out.txt`; data and sources in
  `../holes/data.py`):

  | Object | Faces measured | Faces within 5% of another face | Distinct size levels (upper bound) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Jublains | 12 | 9 | ≤6 |
  | Tongeren | 12 | 7 | ≤9 |
  | Romford | 9 of 12 | 5 | ≤6 |

  At a 10% threshold the bounds fall to ≤4, ≤7 and ≤5.
- **Jublains, pole and hemisphere symmetry (SS data, INF reading):**
  - The two unringed poles are 26×21.5 and 25.5×21.5 mm, so they are indistinguishable (FACT 31).
  - Both rings carry the same cyclic class pattern, small-medium-large-medium-large. The pattern maps onto itself
    under a pole-swapping two-fold rotation, which a random placement of the same 2 S + 4 M + 4 L classes does with
    P = 0.048 (FACT 44; `../holes/results.txt`).
  - Each face therefore has a size twin in the other hemisphere. The poles differ by only 2%, below the 5% threshold,
    so the reader cannot tell which hemisphere is up.
  - By size class alone Jublains gives 4 outcomes (pole, small, medium, large). With neighbour context it gives at
    most 6 (six twin pairs), not 12. The symmetry looks designed (FACT 44), and a design that pairs faces is the
    opposite of what a 12-outcome die needs. It would suit a 6- or 4-outcome lot (INF).
- **Further pieces:**
  - Tongeren has near-equal values (13.8/14.0, 20.3/20.5, 25.2/26.3/27.0).
  - Goodrich Castle is reported with all holes equal (Guggenberger 2000, second-hand via `../holes/data.py`).
  - Schwarzenacker has "no two equal" (DDB, ibid.), the only piece that fits a 12-code without help.
- **Pro-H1 rescue (INF):** irregular sizes still make every face unique by its neighbours, provided the piece has no
  rotational symmetry. But that needs a learned map of each object; no such map or key exists (ND). It also fails at
  Jublains, which has the symmetry.
- **Ancient tolerance of unequal outcomes (SS):** 80-90% of Roman cubic dice are visibly lopsided and favour 1 and 6.
  Eerkens & de Voogt 2022 (abstract) argue that users "understood roll outcomes as the product of fate, rather than
  chance". Minas-Nerpel 2007 p. 148 notes that on the uneven Dakhleh icosahedron "some deities would ... never have
  been selected". Unequal face sizes and probabilities are therefore no objection to H1. Indistinguishable faces are.

### 2.3 Rings and the unringed "pole" pair
- **SS:** on type 2a, ten holes are ringed by (usually three) circles and one opposite pair is larger, unringed and
  often oval (FACT 3). The corpus specialists read the unringed pair as a production feature (FACT 33).
- **H1 reading (INF):** the rings split "pole" outcomes (2 faces) from "ring" outcomes (10 faces) and give an
  orientation axis. The Geneva die is organised the same way: two opposite pole faces (Virgo, Pisces), each with the
  signs following "dans l'ordre, en deux groupes de 5" in the two rings (Cervi-Brunier 1985 p. 154). So is the Idaean
  die: top 1, two zones of five, bottom 4 (Chaniotis 2006 p. 207).
- **Caveat (INF):** any dodecahedron splits geometrically into pole + 5 + 5 + pole about any face axis, so this
  parallel shows only that ancient makers *used* that axis, not that the hollow pole pair is a die feature.
- **Ring-and-dot pips (SS):** ring-and-dot ("dot and circle", "double circle and dot") pips are the usual motif on
  Roman dice from the Netherlands (Eerkens & de Voogt 2022, Table 1 notes). A ringed hole echoes that motif (INF,
  weak).
- **Unlettered, shape-coded lots (SS):** "Knucklebones required no marks of value on them, since their sides were
  naturally distinguishable" (Last 2000, ZPE 130 p. 248). The four astragal sides carried fixed values 1, 3, 4, 6 by
  shape alone, and the Asia Minor oracles keyed 56 answers to them (Prikhodko 2020 p. 248; Costanza 2023 pp. 22-24).
  This is the best ancient parallel for reading an unmarked face by its form.
- **Contrast (SS):** the Germanic slips had "notae" (Tacitus) and Praeneste's lots had letters (Cicero *Div.* 2.85).

### 2.4 Twenty knobs
- **H1 (INF):**
  - The knobs give a five-point stance on every face, so one face is unambiguously uppermost (FACT 34).
  - They also stop a near-spherical body from rolling too far, and they protect the engraved rings and hole rims
    from abrasion.
- **Against (SS):** Hitchens, as reported in the Boston Globe (30 Apr 2024), says the knobs "impede their ability to
  roll" and rejects dice use.
- **Against (INF):**
  - Knobs are recorded as breaking off (Deonica, Kenchester; `../wear/RESULT.md`), which is a risk for a thrown
    object.
  - The related Arloff icosahedron has three larger knobs round one face, giving it a preferred standing position
    (Wikipedia, citing Guggenberger & Leach 2025 n. 43). A preferred rest position defeats a lot, so at least that
    sister piece looks made to stand, not to be thrown.

### 2.5 Size 4-11 cm and weight 35-580 g
- **SS comparison:**
  - Roman cubic dice from the Netherlands are mostly 12-17 mm, with a range of 5.6-27.1 mm (Eerkens & de Voogt 2022,
    Table 1).
  - Lettered polyhedral dice, used or proposed for games or oracles, were 1-6.5 cm:
    - Louvre rock-crystal icosahedron, 1 cm (Pearse blog citing the RMN record; weak);
    - Idaean Cave dodecahedron, 2 cm;
    - Met 10.130.1158 serpentinite icosahedron, 3.2 cm;
    - Geneva, 3.5 cm and 297 g;
    - British Museum lettered polyhedra from Egypt, 4.2-6 cm wide;
    - Petrie icosahedron UC 44999, 5.4 cm;
    - Dakhleh icosahedron, 5 × 6 cm;
    - a faience icosahedron sold by Cahn in 2002 and catalogued as a "dodecahedron", 6.5 cm.
    Sources: Minas-Nerpel 2007 pp. 137 n. 2 and 145 nn. 19-21; Chaniotis 2006 p. 206; Met record.
- **INF:**
  - The small hollow pieces (4-6 cm) fall inside the range of oracle polyhedra. The large ones (up to 11 cm, 553 g at
    Fishguard, `../wear/RESULT.md`) are well above it.
  - A 1 mm bronze shell weighing hundreds of grams, thrown repeatedly, should dent or lose knobs unless it lands on
    something soft.
  - The spread of sizes with no standard module (FACT 5) does not matter to a lot.

## 3. Why only the NW provinces and the 2nd-4th centuries (under H1)

### 3.1 Supporting facts (SS)
- **A regional die culture with its own special types.**
  - Slate word-dice made at Autun (Aeduan territory) in the 2nd c. carry the values as words of 1-6 letters (I / VA /
    EST / ORTI / VRBIS / VOLOTE). They reached Trier, Lyon, London (Southwark) and Budapest (Schwinden 2003, FuA Trier
    35, pp. 36-45).
  - Trier has monumental gaming tables of the late 3rd-4th c. with the inscription "VIRTVS IMPERI / HOSTES VINCTI /
    LVDANT ROMANI" (Merten 2006, FuA Trier 38, pp. 34-35).
  - The 4th-c. Vettweiss-Froitzheim dice tower (FACT 35; `notes_context.md`).
  - So the NW produced regionally distinctive dice and exported them. A regional bronze "oracle die" would fit that
    pattern (INF).
- **Lots and divination among Gauls and Germans:** see section 1 for Tacitus *Germ.* 10 and Caesar *BG* 1.50 and
  1.53. Druids foretold the future "partim auguriis, partim coniectura" (Cicero *Div.* 1.90) and taught "multa ... de
  sideribus atque eorum motu" (Caesar *BG* 6.14.6). None of these texts mentions a thrown polyhedron.
- **Astrology and the zodiac in the region** (`notes_context.md` C1-C4):
  - The Geneva zodiac die, 4th c. (Cervi-Brunier 1985).
  - The Grand (Vosges) ivory zodiac diptychs: 12 signs, each with 5 Greek letters, and 36 decans. They were broken
    and dumped in a well with 3rd-c. fill (MAN, "L'objet du mois"; Abry ed. 1993). This is professional horoscopic
    astrology in a NW sanctuary town.
  - Mithraic zodiac reliefs: Housesteads (CIMRM 860) and Trier-Altbachtal (CIMRM 985).
  - The Coligny calendar: Gaulish, late 2nd c., with months marked MAT/ANM (lucky/unlucky), a native system of
    auspicious times.
  - Week-god stones on Jupiter columns: a 7-fold scheme, not 12-fold.
  - So 12-fold astrology and day-lot thinking are documented in the region in the 2nd-4th c. (SS). A thrown
    12-outcome device tied to them is not (ND).
- **Lot divination in general:** "arguably the simplest, cheapest, and most widespread form of divination in
  antiquity" (Luijendijk & Klingshirn 2019 p. 1, via RBL 12/2020 review).

### 3.2 Why texts and images would be missing (INF)
- Lot devices are poorly documented even where lot-books survive:
  - The ancient copies of the Sortes Astrampsychi are papyri from Egypt; the rest are medieval manuscripts (BMCR
    2001.10.04).
  - The Sangallenses survive in one palimpsest (Winnefeld 1887).
  - The Geneva die is unique. No ancient text names a dodecahedral lot: Chaniotis 2006 p. 209 says none of the known
    practices "involved the use of a dodecahedron". The earliest text I found that does is the medieval
    *Dodechedron*.
- A device that needed an oral or book key leaves no inscription on itself.
- The end in the 4th c. fits CTh 9.16.4 (25 Jan 357), which silences "divinandi curiositas" for everyone on pain of
  death, and the anti-pagan laws of 391-392 (`notes_context.md`).
- Enforcement reached the NW (SS, `notes_context.md` B4):
  - Julian, as Caesar in Gaul (360/361), hid his haruspicy and augury (Ammianus 21.1-2).
  - Priscillian was convicted of *maleficium* at Trier and beheaded (Sulpicius Severus, *Chron.* 2.50-51; 385 per
    Wikipedia).
  - CTh 9.16.4 passed into the Breviary of Alaric (506).
- But note the limits of the laws:
  - CTh 9.16 never names lots, dice or *sortes* (string check, `notes_oracles.md` B7; `notes_context.md` B1). Nor
    does 16.10.10-12 (391-392). Ammianus' list of divination methods (21.1.8-12) leaves out lots.
  - 9.16.9 (371), issued at Trier, explicitly allows haruspicy unless used to harm.
  - No prosecution for lot or dice divination is known in the NW (ND).
  - Lot-books kept circulating in Gaul until the Council of Vannes (465) condemned the dice-based Sortes Sanctorum.
  - So the laws do not explain a clean 4th-c. end of a lot device in the NW. At most they made it riskier and less
    likely to be displayed.

### 3.3 Against the H1 explanation of the map (INF from SS)
- The places where 12-faced numbered dice and lot oracles are best attested are the eastern Mediterranean: Crete,
  Patrae, Antioch, Egypt and the Asia Minor astragal pillars. There are no hollow dodecahedra there (FACT 11).
- In the NW, the attested Germanic and Gaulish methods are slips drawn by hand. Tacitus describes exactly the people of
  free Germania, where hollow dodecahedra are almost absent (FACT 12, 36).
- H1 therefore has to explain the map by bronze-craft regionalism, not by a regional *divinatory* practice that we
  can document.

## 4. Evidence against H1 (stated as strictly as the evidence for it)

1. **No marks on any hollow piece** (FACT 19, HARD).
   - Every Roman polyhedral die I found carries numerals, letters or divine names: Idaean, Patrae, Antioch, Egypt,
     Met, Louvre, Dakhleh and Geneva.
   - So do the Gallo-Roman special dice (Autun group) and the Trier dice culture (Schwinden 2003).
   - Even the Germanic slips had *notae* (Tacitus).
   - The 4th-c. Antioch oracle device was a metal plate with the 24 letters engraved on it (Ammianus 29.1.29-32).
   - A twelve-outcome lot without marks would be unique. The only unmarked parallel is the four-valued astragal. Its
     four faces differ grossly in shape (SS, Last 2000), far more than 10-20% hole-size steps do (INF).
2. **Hole sizes do not give twelve readable codes** on any of the three measured objects (section 2.2). Jublains has
   symmetric hemispheres. Goodrich Castle reportedly has all holes equal. Opposite holes are near-equal on Tongeren
   (`../holes/results.txt`, P = 0.0005 for near-equal opposite pairs). That is irrelevant to a die read on one face;
   it fits a through-axis (sighting or mounting) better (INF).
3. **Wear.**
   - The hollow pieces "rarely show signs of wear" (FACT 20). Norton Disney has "no evidence of any wear at all".
     Hitchens says they "don't have the kind of use wear you'd expect from a tool" (Boston Globe 2024).
   - In contrast, the Geneva die's publisher cites "les traces d'usure que ses arêtes et ses faces comportent" as a
     reason to call it a die (Cervi-Brunier 1985 p. 155).
   - Mitigation (SS): Minas-Nerpel 2007 p. 148 notes an oracle icosahedron that "does not look particularly worn" and
     suggests a soft surface. Eye-based "no wear" is weak (`../wear/RESULT.md`).
4. **Fragility and rolling.** Hitchens: "much more delicate than people realize ... They would be broken very
   quickly", said against tool use; the knobs "impede their ability to roll" (Boston Globe 2024). Knobs are found broken off (Deonica,
   Kenchester). Shells 0.6-1.6 mm thick (FACT 7).
5. **Size and weight.** The upper half of the range (8-11 cm, 250-580 g) is above every known Roman oracle die
   (section 2.5). The region's own dice apparatus was built for 10-18 mm dice. Dice towers like the reconstructed
   Dorchester one (about 190 mm high, stair plates about 70 × 45 mm) could not take a 40-100 mm knobbed body (INF
   from Cobbett 2020).
6. **Contexts are neutral to weakly negative.** Rich graves and hoards suit any valued object. A die in a rich grave
   has a parallel (Patrae), so this is not evidence against. But "over half from civil settlements, under a fifth
   military, ~8.5% sacred" (FACT 14) gives H1 no specific support. No hollow piece has been found with astragals,
   dice or lot-books.
7. **The Geneva die is not the same kind of object.** It is solid, 35 mm, unknobbed and unperforated, inscribed, and
   made of silver sheet over lead. Cervi-Brunier herself says it "ne se rattache pas directement" to the hollow
   series, and that "ni la matière, ni le décor, ni la facture" match (p. 154). It shows that a 12-faced zodiac die
   was used in the region in the 4th c. It does not show that the hollow objects were dice. Indeed, a maker of a
   divination die in the same region and period chose to *write* the outcomes.
8. **No NW text or image** shows a thrown polyhedral lot. The only explicit regional lot texts describe slips on a
   cloth (FACT 36). The only medieval 12-faced divination die (the *Dodechedron*) is numbered.
9. **Arloff icosahedron** (sister object): enlarged knobs give it a preferred resting face, which a lot should not
   have (section 2.4).
10. **Chronology is untested.** No hollow piece is dated by its own manufacture (FACT 13, 16), so the "end in the 4th
    c. because of the laws" argument rests on deposit dates only.

### 4a. Tests that would decide (INF)
- Map hole diameters for all published pieces, and count distinct size levels at a 5% threshold, as `distinct.py`
  does. If most pieces give fewer than about 8 levels, or symmetric hemispheres as at Jublains, H1 in its 12-outcome
  form fails. If many give 12 distinct, asymmetric codes, it survives.
- Compare knob-tip wear across all 20 knobs, using `../wear/` method. Uniform rounding of all tips supports throwing;
  pristine tips argue against.
- Re-examine the Geneva die's wear with the same method, as a positive control for a known die.
- Read Chimier et al. 2024 (Instrumentum 59 p. 40, "Un dé à jouer dodécaédrique en os découvert à Esvres"; cited by
  Wikipedia, NA here). A marked or unmarked Gaulish bone dodecahedral die would bear directly on point 1.

## 5. The Geneva die: facts (all SS, Cervi-Brunier 1985 unless noted)

- **Publication:** I. Cervi-Brunier, "Le dodécaèdre en argent trouvé à Saint-Pierre de Genève", *Zeitschrift für
  schweizerische Archäologie und Kunstgeschichte* 42 (1985), no. 3, pp. 153-156, doi 10.5169/seals-168626.
- **Find:**
  - Found in November 1982, south of Saint-Pierre cathedral, in the rue Farel sector, near the bishop's reception hall
    with its mosaic floor.
  - It lay "dans un remblai daté, par sa position stratigraphique et la céramique récoltée, du IVe siècle" (p. 153).
  - Excavations of the Bureau cantonal d'archéologie under Ch. Bonnet; found by J. Montesino (p. 156 n.).
- **Size and mass:** mean distance between parallel faces 35 mm; edge about 15 mm; weight 297 g (p. 153).
- **Material and make:**
  - An outer layer of silver with a small percentage of copper, filled with lead.
  - Formed from one flat silver sheet, folded and soldered at the edges, then filled with molten lead.
  - The filling hole was closed with a silver nail. Its head forms the centre of the O of LEO, and its hammered point
    sits on the opposite face, SAGIT.
  - There is a crack caused by the corroded lead expanding (pp. 153-154).
  - The ge.ch page (2021) says "en plomb recouvert d'argent".
- **Inscription (p. 154):** letters 5-6 mm high reading ARIES · TAURI · GEM/INI · KAN/CRI · LEO · VIR/GO · LIBRA ·
  SCOR/PIO · SAGIT · CAPRI/CORNI · AQUA/RIUS · PISCES.
  - SAGIT is the only abbreviation.
  - TAURI, KANCRI and CAPRICORNI are genitives.
  - The date rests on stratigraphy and pottery. The unusual K of KANCRI is cited only as confirming it.
- **Arrangement (p. 154):**
  - Virgo and Pisces are the only pair opposite both on the die and in the zodiac.
  - Taking them as poles, the other signs run in order in two rings of five: Aries, Tauri, Gemini, Kancri, Leo
    ("printemps et été") and Libra, Scorpio, Sagit, Capricorni, Aquarius ("automne et hiver").
  - Each pole is the last sign of its half-year.
  - The upper ring is engraved parallel to the pole; the lower is more irregular in orientation.
- **Interpretation:**
  - Cervi-Brunier (p. 155) sees only one plausible use, "à part une amulette un peu pesante": "un dé".
  - Her grounds are:
    - "la forme de l'objet";
    - "les traces d'usure que ses arêtes et ses faces comportent";
    - the zodiac names;
    - the existence of special dice (20-, 19- and 18-faced; dodecahedra numbered 1-12);
    - medieval zodiacal lot-books. The "Livre des Sorts d'Alfhadel, de Morenga", translated from Arabic into Latin
      by Gerard of Cremona in the 12th c., has 144 questions tied to the zodiac signs with 12 answers each. Its
      Provençal and French derivatives include the "Plaisant Jeu du Dodechedron de Fortune", which she says was
      printed in 1556 and "se jouait avec un dé en forme de dodécaèdre". She herself treats this as distant in time
      and space and infers ancient games only "sous toutes réserves".
  - She concludes "a dû être utilisé pour connaître le sort dans le cadre de jeux divinatoires fondés sur
    l'astrologie", but urges caution ("sous toutes réserves", "demeurer prudent").
  - The ge.ch page (2021) repeats this as a conjecture. The Grand Curtius museum (Liège) uses it as the main support
    for a divinatory reading of the hollow series.
- **Relation to the hollow series:** shares form, a 3rd-4th-c. date and a north-Alpine distribution, but "ni la
  matière, ni le décor, ni la facture" (p. 154).
- **Only comparison she names:** a rock-crystal icosahedron in Munich with the 12 zodiac figures, each with two Greek
  letters, on 12 faces; four faces repeat Gemini, Cancer, Leo and Scorpio; four are blank (citing Boll, *Sphaera*
  1903, pp. 469-470). Chaniotis 2006 p. 208 describes what is probably the same Munich piece as having 12 faces
  "inscribed with couples of letters, which again represent numbers" (Dornseiff 1925). The two descriptions differ;
  neither source was checked.
