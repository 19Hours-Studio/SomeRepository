=== COHERENCE ===
THE PICTURE: The dodecahedra were a regional Gallo-Germanic-British fashion of about AD 150-350, not a Roman-wide tool. Local smiths cast each by lost-wax as a hollow knobbed head, fixed on a short handle or rod through its two largest opposite holes. The ten ringed holes and twenty knobs were the type's ornament and identity, shared with the Arloff icosahedron, not working parts. Owners were mostly civilians of some means in towns, villas and shops, soldiers on the Limes before 260, and at least one rich woman c. 350. They valued it (repair, grave good) but handled it little. Its meaning is unknown; the 4th-century Geneva zodiac die shows the shape could carry a cosmic sense here, so it was plausibly a personal emblem or token of standing whose meaning could vary between owners and over time. Pieces ended as provincial bronzes did: lost in settlements, left in forts, hoarded, put in rivers, or buried with the dead. The map is real, not a recording artefact, and, like the Jupiter columns, the custom stayed invisible to Mediterranean writers. Why it began and why it faded remain unknown.

- Women's grave vs forts: not sex- or office-specific; only Gellep is a firm grave.
