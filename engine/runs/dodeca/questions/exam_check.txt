THE PICTURE: The hollow knobbed dodecahedra were a north-western provincial type, cast individually by lost-wax with no size module. They were in use by c. 200, probably from the later 2nd century, to the mid-4th, and were really absent from Italy, Iberia, Africa and the East. They were probably held on a rod or handle through the two largest opposite holes. Rings and knobs read as design, not as a measuring or dicing code. Owners were people of some means in towns, villas, shops and forts, later rich men and women buried with them. The pieces were rarely worn, sometimes repaired, and ended in settlements, pits, hoards, rivers and graves. Their meaning, any office, and why they began and ended remain unknown. The Gallic Empire did not create them, and the Geneva die does not explain them.
