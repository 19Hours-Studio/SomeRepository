=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Post-Druidic yes-or-no lot
MECHANISM: Lay Gaulish diviners, like the Historia Augusta's 3rd-century "dryades" (one at Tongeren), threw it; it rests on five knobs, one axis vertical. If the unringed ovals form that axis, a wide window opens through: "yes", one throw in six; ringed faces all mean "no", so sizes may repeat. Druidic lore was unwritten (Caesar, Gallic War 6.14), and banned Druidic sacrifice left former Druidic lands needing a bloodless lot until Christianisation.
VALUE: It derives map, dates, missing letters and household contexts from a tradition absent around the Mediterranean. Knob tips no more polished than protected recesses on 10+ pieces refute it.

IDEA 2
NAME: Bright-or-dark day-luck lot
MECHANISM: Thrown on pale cloth, it rests on knobs, one opposite pair vertical; the reader looks down through the top hole. A larger lower hole shows only bright cloth, "good"; a smaller one, a dark interior ring, "not good"; near-equal pairs void the throw, so no shared scheme is needed. It extended to single decisions the good/not-good (MAT/ANM) day-marking of Gaul's 2nd-century Coligny calendar, used only in Celtic-calendar lands until Christian reckoning.
VALUE: It makes variable opposite pairs and missing letters requirements, and ties the map to Gaulish time-reckoning. Measured opposite pairs (Jublains, Guggenberger's list) mostly equal within 1 mm refute it.

IDEA 3
NAME: Tallow-candle draught shade
MECHANISM: A home-dipped tallow candle passed up through whichever hole fitted its thickness, its foot on the table between the knobs; the flame burned draught-proof inside, the opposite hole venting smoke. Graded holes suit irregular candles, so no shared scheme was needed; the unringed core openings admitted a light, and rings mark designed holes. North of the olive line Baetican lamp oil dwindled after c. 200, making candles the light until c. 400.
VALUE: It derives hole range, knobs, scarce wax (tallow was normal) and map from lighting economics. Rims and interiors of 10+ pieces lacking both animal-fat lipids (GC-MS) and soot refute it.

IDEA 4
NAME: Festival ember carrier
MECHANISM: Yearly, in the Celtic custom (recorded later in Ireland) of quenching hearths and rekindling them from a sanctuary fire, each household carried a live coal home in it. The coal entered an unringed core opening; turning the shell set a larger or smaller ringed vent (rings mark designed vents) windward to keep it glowing, knobs giving cooler grips. Coligny's Samonios month attests this festival calendar in 2nd-century Gaul; Christianisation ended it.
VALUE: It derives household contexts, annual (hence unworn) use and the Celtic-calendar map from one rite. Interiors of 10+ pieces without heat tint, soot or ash refute it.

IDEA 5
NAME: Coursing hunter's personal whistle
MECHANISM: Blown across an unringed oval, all other holes open, it gives one note fixed by cavity volume and total hole area, so no hole is stopped by hand. Varying bodies and holes give each piece its own note for its owner's hounds to learn; a shared scheme would defeat that. Arrian (Cynegeticus, 2nd century) calls coursing with hounds a Celtic sport, unlike Mediterranean net-hunting, hence north-western villas and forts until c. 400.
VALUE: It makes continuous size spread and no shared hole scheme necessary. Unringed rims on 10+ pieces as blunt as ringed ones, with no sharp blowing edge, refute it.

IDEA 6
NAME: Sanctuary-theatre chorus pitch-giver
MECHANISM: At festivals in Gaul's and Britain's sanctuary theatres, a chorus leader gave the opening note by blowing across an unringed oval, other holes open. After casting, the maker reamed ringed holes wider until the piece gave its owner's note, so holes differ within every piece and follow no shared scheme; rings were layout lines. Choristers kept them at home, hence settlement finds; the temple closures of 391-392 ended them.
VALUE: It explains within-piece differences and the missing scheme as tuning, tied to a regional building type. Ringed-hole walls on 10+ pieces without post-casting reaming marks refute it.

IDEA 7
NAME: Native priest's radiate sceptre
MECHANISM: A short rod (as at Gellep) passed through the unringed core openings, so the ten ringed holes formed two radiating five-fold crowns, each holding a sacred-plant sprig; stems differed, hence varied holes and no shared scheme. Never grounded, it stayed unworn; knobs stopped it rolling on altars. Native priesthoods (Gaulish gutuatri; Romano-British priestly crowns and sceptres, known from finds, not images) existed only in the Celtic north-west and ended with the Theodosian bans.
VALUE: It makes the unringed axis, the five-fold form and graded holes necessary and derives art's silence. Ringed rims on 10+ pieces without stem scratches or plant residue refute it.

IDEA 8
NAME: Spring-cult lustral sprinkler
MECHANISM: On a short handle through the unringed core openings, the herb-packed head was dipped in a sacred spring, river or well and shaken, spraying from ten ringed outlets in two crowns; graded holes vary drop size, so no shared scheme mattered. Knobs let it stand to drain; water causes no wear. Celtic water cults flourished in 2nd-4th-century Gaul and Britain, hence river and well finds; Mediterranean lustration used a branch.
VALUE: It explains river and well finds, handle axis and graded outlets together. Interiors of 10+ dry-land pieces lacking both plant micro-remains and water scale refute it.
