**Physical constraints first**

- **Wall and alloy.** The shell is thin, about 0.6–1.6 mm, and made of leaded bronze cast by lost wax. That makes the objects good castings but poor load-bearers. They are useless as weapons, gauges or tools that must stay rigid.
- **Making it.** A hollow casting this thin needs a clay core held in place, and the core has to be removed afterwards. Twelve openings serve both needs, which may explain why no two hole sets match.
- **Knobs.** The 20 knobs let the object sit stably on any face, raised off the surface. They make it a poor die.
- **Hole sizes.** Diameters are unstandardised and unmarked, so precise measurement is ruled out.
- **Type 2a.** The commonest type has ten ringed holes and one opposite pair of larger, unringed holes. That pair is a structural axis.
- **Wear.** Little or none, so use was light or ceremonial.
- **Distribution.** It tracks a regional workshop tradition, not the army.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Staff finial on axial rod
MECHANISM: The object was the head of a ceremonial staff carried by local cult or collegium officials in Gaul, Germania and Britain. In the commonest type, the larger, unringed opposite pair of holes took the shaft, so the ringed holes faced outward as decoration. The variable sizes of that pair simply matched each staff's rod diameter. Knobs gave a decorative silhouette, and occasional handling plus burial with the holder explain low wear, graves and hoards.
VALUE: It alone explains why the commonest type marks one opposite pair as different, and it fits the Gellep rod. Test: microscopy and CT of the large pair's rims on about 20 type-2a pieces. If those rims show no more abrasion or organic or metal residue than the ringed holes, the idea is refuted.

IDEA 2
NAME: Regional bronze-casters' prestige piece
MECHANISM: The object was a virtuoso hollow casting from a Gallo-Roman workshop tradition, owned and given as a high-status display piece. The twelve holes were core prints, there to support and then clear the clay core, so their sizes followed each caster's core setup rather than any scheme. Engraved triple rings and knobs were finishing that showed off skill, and lead-rich alloy aided thin-wall flow. Distribution follows the workshops' market, and texts are silent because no one documented such a craft product.
VALUE: It explains the varied hole sizes, ~1 mm walls, the casting method, no wear and regional clustering without needing any function. Test: CT and pXRF of museum pieces. If holes show post-casting drilling or cutting rather than cast edges, or alloys show no regional groups, the idea is refuted.

IDEA 3
NAME: Footed charcoal incense burner
MECHANISM: A small ember with resin or wax was loaded through a large hole, and the object stood on its knobs. The knobs kept the hot shell off furniture. Which face sat down set the draught, because the holes differ in size, and smoke escaped through the rest. Its private household and ritual use explains settlement and sacred finds and the absence of military specificity.
VALUE: It explains the knobs as feet, the many unequal vents and the one reported wax residue, which rival readings dismiss as incidental. Test: SEM and GC-MS of interior crusts and checks for heat tint. If no soot, resin markers or heat discolouration are found across a dozen specimens, the idea is refuted.
