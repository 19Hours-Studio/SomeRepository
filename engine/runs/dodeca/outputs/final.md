=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Knob-footed twelve-face divination lot
MECHANISM: It was a bronze lot for private divination: the person consulting it threw it onto a cloth or table, it came to rest on the five knobs of one face, and the horizontal face opposite was read. Each face is told apart without letters by its hole (size class, ringed or not) and by where it sits relative to the larger unringed pair, which the specialists read as the casting-core axis and which serves as the pole. Because each piece carries its own code, holes differ within every object while no size scheme or module is shared between objects. The twelve outcomes stand for the twelve parts of the heavens: a 4th-century silver-coated lead dodecahedron from Geneva carries the twelve zodiac names in Latin, one per face, and is interpreted as a divination die, and Platonist tradition made the dodecahedron the figure of the whole cosmos (Timaeus 55c). The knobs are feet: only their tips touch the cloth, so the face to read is unambiguous and the ringed rims are spared. Owners were households and individuals across civil and military society, who treated the piece as a valuable; hence rich graves of both sexes, a coin hoard, shrine deposits, and the repair on the Kortessem piece. It was a regional product of the Gaulish, Rhenish and British bronze trade, which soldiers and traders carried to the Danube legionary bases; the Mediterranean cast lots with other devices (the oak lots of Praeneste, the 2nd-century knucklebone oracles of Asia Minor) and already knew both hollow lost-wax casting and the dodecahedron, so the map reflects a regional device, not a missing technique, and the type appears c. 150-200 while its 4th-century end coincides with imperial laws against divination (356-357) and pagan cult (391-392).
VALUE: It is the only explanation that turns four hard facts into one function: holes that vary within each object, no shared scheme, no inscriptions, and the fixed twelve-face template. It also rests on a dated regional parallel (Geneva) instead of the misread Germanic lots. Still unexplained: why only the north-west used this device (shown only by the distribution and the single Geneva die), the start c. 150-200, any role for the knobs beyond feet, and the Arloff icosahedron with holes in only two faces. Decisive test: on 10 or more pieces with surviving original surface, compare all 20 knob tips with their bases, plus the hole rims and interior, by stereo microscope or 3D scan. Throwing predicts rounding on every tip, roughly evenly, with clean rims and interiors; tips as sharp as their bases, or wear limited to one face's five knobs or to particular rims, refute it.

IDEA 2
NAME: Cosmic emblem, sometimes on a staff
MECHANISM: It was a devotional image of the cosmos, Plato's fifth figure that the god used "for the whole" (Timaeus 55c), owned by households and individuals of the north-western provinces rather than by priests. It was kept at home or in shrines, and sometimes carried on a short rod passed through the unringed core-axis pair; at Krefeld-Gellep a bone object, possibly a handle, lay beside the 4th-century piece in a rich woman's grave. It went with the dead, to sanctuaries (Lydney, Pfofeld) and into hoards. The form mattered, not a mechanism: the hollow knobbed icosahedron from Arloff has holes in only two opposite faces, so face holes were optional; rings and knobs are the ordinary decoration of provincial bronze; and hole sizes vary because nothing depended on them. It was rarely handled and never put to work, so it stayed largely unworn, and it needed no letters because the shape carried the meaning. Its region is that of a provincial taste in cult objects and a regional workshop network; the proposed Druid-Pythagorean root is weak, because the Druids were suppressed under Tiberius and Claudius, 150-300 years before the objects, and its end coincides with the closing of pagan cult in 391-392.
VALUE: It accounts for the low wear, the rarity, the valuable and sacred contexts and the two-holed icosahedron better than the lot does, and it drops the reviewed failures of the priest's-sceptre version (priestly regalia, sprig crowns, knobs as anti-roll stops). But it leaves the hard fact of hole variation within each object, and the three preset hole classes at Jublains, as unexplained ornament, and its regional cause is unsupported. On the same test it predicts unworn knob tips, or wear only on the five knobs of one display face, plus abrasion or mineral-preserved wood or bone inside the unringed pair where it was mounted on a staff; evenly rounded tips on all 20 knobs refute it.

ANCHORS CHECKED
The four explanations:
- Idea 1, DROPPED the Tacitus model. In Germania 10, slips cut from a fruit-tree branch and marked with signs are scattered on a white cloth; the priest or the father then picks up three, one at a time, and reads them by their signs ("ter singulos tollit ... secundum impressam ante notam interpretatur"). Nothing is read from how they land, and free Germania has only three dodecahedron fragments.
- Idea 1, DROPPED "not Mediterranean dice culture". The north-west had full dice culture (the Vettweiss-Froitzheim dice tower: found 1985, 4th century, LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn). The Mediterranean had lot divination: the oak lots with old letters at Praeneste (Cicero, De Divinatione 2.85-86, checked) and the 2nd-century knucklebone oracles of south-west Asia Minor (Nollé 2007).
- Idea 1, ADDED the Geneva dodecahedron. It was found in 1982 in the Saint-Pierre cathedral excavations; it is lead coated with silver, carries the twelve zodiac names in Latin on its faces, is dated to the 4th century by context and letter forms, and is interpreted as a divination die (ge.ch; Grand Curtius, Liège). It is one solid, inscribed object, outside the hollow corpus.
- Idea 1, CORRECTED the test. The old clause that throwing onto cloth "wears little" contradicted it. The repaired version commits to rounding on all 20 knob tips and measures shape, which survives under a stable patina, rather than shine.
- Idea 2, the reviewers' map objection STANDS. A bronze incense cup from Trier (Faust, Trierer Zeitschrift) and a clay incense cup with a dedication from Coventina's Well in Britain show the north-west had its own incense vessels. No soot or heat tint is reported on any dodecahedron, and the wax specimen is dropped (see FACT 21). Map, rarity, the ring pattern and the dates are CONCEDED.
- Idea 3, CORRECTED. Spanish olive-oil imports to Britain did fall: Dressel 20 amphorae peak in the Antonine period and their production ended c. 260-270. But clay lamps were already scarce in Britain and confined mainly to forts and large towns (Eckardt 2002, Britannia), so candles were normal before c. 200, and the dating mechanism fails. The reviewer's "rare after the 1st century" timing is not independently confirmed. All defects CONCEDED.
- Idea 4, KEPT: the gutuatri priests, on two Autun altars to Anvallus (CIL XIII 11225-6; EDH HD032388); the Wanborough regalia (17 sceptre handles and 4 chain head-dresses, in a layer of the mid-1st to mid-2nd century with a coin hoard of c. AD 55), which differ in form and are older; and the Theodosian bans of 391-392.
- Idea 4, CORRECTED the Gellep "rod". The grave number 3040 is unverified. The grave is a rich 4th-century woman's grave, and the bone object was too decayed to lift, so it cannot be re-examined.
- Idea 4, DROPPED the priest, the sprig crowns and the anti-roll knobs. The repaired, household-emblem version answers the reviewers' context objection.
- User note, REJECTED "the technique never reached the Mediterranean". Hollow lost-wax casting was standard Mediterranean practice. The dodecahedron was known there too: Iamblichus has Hippasus drown for revealing the "sphere of twelve pentagons", and a stone dodecahedron from Monte Loffa near Verona was read as a die (date uncertain).

SINGLE-OBJECT WEIGHING
- Established across the corpus: the template; hole variation within every object; no shared scheme; sizes on a continuum; type 2a commonest; about 130 pieces; north-west only; 2nd-4th century; mixed contexts, mostly civil; few stratified finds; no text, image or inscription. Low wear is only a reported impression.
- Jublains: its ten ringed holes fall into three tight classes (about 11, 16.5 and 21.5 mm, separated by 4 mm gaps), and the five faces around each pole hold the same set of 1 small, 2 medium, 2 large (check.py). Uniform random sizes give this about 0.06% of the time.
- What Jublains shows: matched sizes are fixed when the holes are cut in the wax model, so they are not a casting defect, but they could be a code or simply a kit of three cutters. Because it is one object, it only supports and does not decide.
- Other single objects, none of them deciding:
  - Gellep: the bone object was not lifted.
  - Kortessem: "deliberate" breakage is an interpretation.
  - Norton Disney: unworn, but it may have been new.
  - Arloff icosahedron: left unexplained by Idea 1.
  - Geneva: a different object type.
  - Deonica: one alloy analysis.
  - South Shields rings: a forum source only.

MAP AND DATES
- The Gallic Empire as cause is REJECTED:
  - pieces were lost before 260 at the Feldberg and Zugmantel forts and at Jublains, and others after 274 in the 4th century;
  - Carnuntum, Brigetio and Deonica were never in the Gallic Empire;
  - Spain belonged to it in 260-c.269 and has no finds.
  The 90% figure just restates "north-western provinces".
- A missing technique is REJECTED (see above).
- A Druid or Pythagorean regional cult is WEAK: the Druids were suppressed 150-300 years earlier, Platonism was empire-wide, and the case rests on texts.
- A military spread is SECONDARY: under a fifth of finds are military, though it explains the Danube tail.
- SUPPORTED, only in general form: this is a regional object type used across the social range of Gaul, the Rhineland and Britain, spilling as fragments into the Germanic settlements opposite Brigetio. Lot divination itself was empire-wide; the device was regional. Divination using a twelve-faced object in the region is attested only by Geneva. (Soft: the Historia Augusta places a Gaulish druidess prophesying to Diocletian at Tongeren; the source is late and unreliable.)
- Workshop network UNTESTED: no moulds are known, and the two analysed alloys differ, which suggests several workshops. The Hitchens results from Bonn could show regional alloy groups.
- Start c. 150-200: unexplained.
- End in the 4th century: an association with the anti-divination and anti-pagan laws only, not proven.
