=== REVIEW ===
IDEA 1: Tallow-candle draught shade
DEFECT | MAJOR | The idea says its map follows lighting economics, but the map does not follow the olive line: free Germania had no olive oil at all and used tallow lighting, yet has almost no dodecahedra, and oil-poor Pannonia and Raetia have only a few. | FACT 11, 12, 36
DEFECT | MAJOR | The dating driver does not hold: Baetican Dressel 20 oil amphorae were made until c. 260-270 and Baetican oil kept arriving after that, and ceramic lamps in Britain were always largely limited to forts and large towns, so there was no oil-to-candle switch c. 200 that the type could follow; it was also already in use by c. 200 (Jublains) and before c. 260 (Feldberg, Zugmantel). | FACT 42; Dressel 20 chronology (ihtc.orex.es/6972.pdf; diposit.ub.edu/dspace/handle/2445/169119); Eckardt, Britannia (Colchester lamp production)
DEFECT | MAJOR | A smoky tallow flame inside a closed bronze shell should leave soot, heat tint and fat on interiors and rims, yet no piece is reported with any, including the complete, unworn Norton Disney find, so the idea has to assume all such traces were lost or missed. | FACT 20, 24
DEFECT | MAJOR | An opaque shell around the flame works against giving light: on Jublains the holes open only about 23% of the surface (my calculation from the per-hole table, 59 mm piece), and one hole is plugged by the candle, while the Romans already had draught-proof lanterns with horn or bladder panes. | FACT 31; Martial 14.61-62
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Irregular candles" predicts random hole sizes, not three tight size classes repeated as the same set around both poles at Jublains, and "rings mark designed holes" does not explain why exactly the ten non-axial holes carry triple rings. | FACT 3, 31, 44
DEFECT | MINOR | The smallest pieces (40 mm, wall about 0.6 mm) leave room only for a candle stub under about 35 mm, and the shell would heat the tallow (melting at about 45 °C) right at the socket. | FACT 5, 7
DEFECT | MINOR | The lipid and soot test can only refute the idea on recently excavated, uncleaned pieces (Norton Disney, Kortessem, Jublains), because most pieces are antiquarian finds that were probably cleaned, so absence of residue on them proves nothing. | FACT 16
FIT | 5 of 15 | Explains why holes vary within each piece, the knobs as feet, the civil contexts, the lack of inscriptions and the absence from the Mediterranean, but not the edges of the map, the start date, the low wear or the pattern of rings and size classes.
SURVIVES | Candle lighting did spread in mid-to-late Roman Britain (pottery candlesticks at York), and the GC-MS-plus-soot test is a clean, feasible way to refute the idea.

IDEA 2: Touch-coded casting lot
DEFECT | MAJOR | The cultural anchor is contradicted: the Germanic lots in Tacitus were wooden slips marked with signs and read by picking up three of them, not by which face lands up, and dodecahedra are almost absent from free Germania, where that practice existed. | FACT 36, 12; Tacitus, Germania 10
DEFECT | MAJOR | It contrasts the objects with "Mediterranean dice culture", but the north-western provinces had full Roman dice culture themselves, so its explanation of the distribution (no Mediterranean finds, 90% in the Gallic-Empire area) loses its cause. | FACT 10, 11, 35
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Each owner knew their own piece" makes the code private and untestable, and it does not explain why a strict template recurs across about 130 pieces, or why Jublains has three tight size classes repeated as the same set around both poles. | FACT 1, 31, 44
DEFECT | MAJOR | The knobs, the hollow shell and the regular twelve-faced form get no function beyond "tumbles on its knobs"; a marked cube or slip would do the same job, so the distinctive features are not derived. | FACT 1, 34
DEFECT | MINOR | Pieces of up to 580 g (one over 1 kg) and 100 mm, with walls of 0.6-1.6 mm, make poor objects to toss. | FACT 5, 6, 7
DEFECT | MINOR | The wear test sets no threshold and does not require pieces with original surface; burial corrosion alters knob tips, and the idea already says cloth landings wear little, so slight polish can be read either way. | FACT 20
FIT | 6 of 15 | Fits holes that vary within a piece with no shared scheme between pieces, the ringed/unringed distinction, the lack of inscriptions, civil and grave contexts and low visible wear; the map, the dating, the Jublains pattern and the knobs are unexplained or contradicted.
SURVIVES | A lot read from the face that comes up is consistent with the hole evidence and with the lack of inscriptions, and comparing knob tips with protected recesses under a microscope is feasible.

IDEA 3: Fumigation cage for aromatics
DEFECT | MAJOR | One of its claimed advantages ("the wax specimen is residue") rests on evidence the ledger has dropped: the wax report comes only from popular sources and the piece cannot be located. | FACT 40 (replaces 21)
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Knobs give cooler grips" is physically wrong: the knobs are cast in one piece with a thin copper-alloy shell, and heat crosses a 5 mm bronze knob in about 2 s (L²/α with k ≈ 50 W/mK, my calculation), so the whole piece becomes too hot to hold. | FACT 1, 7, 8
DEFECT | MAJOR | Incense burning with censers (thymiateria) was common across the Mediterranean, so "a regional form of a common rite" gives no reason for the absence from Italy, Spain and Africa, the Gallic-Empire concentration or the near-absence in free Germania. | FACT 10, 11, 12, 36
DEFECT | MAJOR | An ember inside a bronze shell would leave soot, charred resin and heat tint, yet no piece is reported with any, including the complete, unworn Norton Disney find, so the idea has to assume every trace was lost or cleaned off. | FACT 20, 24
DEFECT | MAJOR | Controlling the air intake only needs holes of different sizes; it does not explain the three tight size classes repeated around both poles at Jublains, the triple rings on exactly ten holes (written off as "ornament") or the fixed regular twelve-face template. | FACT 1, 3, 31, 44
DEFECT | MINOR | The residue test is decisive and feasible, but it must use recently excavated, uncleaned pieces, because antiquarian finds were probably cleaned. | FACT 16
FIT | 6 of 14 | Gives a working role to the hollow shell, the holes and the knobs, fits holes that vary within a piece, the mixed civil contexts and no inscriptions; it leaves the distribution, the dating, the ring and size-class pattern and the missing heat traces unexplained.
SURVIVES | Knobs as stand-offs that let air in, holes for intake and venting, and a test using soot, heat tint and GC-MS resin markers make this a real mechanism that can be refuted.

IDEA 4: Knob-footed twelve-face divination lot
DEFECT | MAJOR | The claim of twelve outcomes for the twelve parts of the heavens is contradicted by the commonest type itself: the two unringed pole faces are almost identical (Jublains 26x21.5 vs 25.5x21.5 mm; Newhall both 21x26 mm), and Jublains repeats ringed sizes (17/17, 22/22, 21/21.5) with the same set around both poles, so on the one measured piece well under twelve faces can be told apart by eye or finger. | FACT 3, 31, 44
DEFECT | MAJOR | A bronze lot thrown again and again onto cloth or a table, landing only on its knob tips, should show worn tips, but the pieces are reported as rarely worn and Norton Disney as complete and unworn, so the idea needs an unsupported assumption of rare use or of wear too slight to see. | FACT 20, 24
DEFECT | MAJOR | It restates the distribution rather than explaining it: "a regional device" gives no cause for the Mediterranean absence or the 90% Gallic-Empire concentration, and no workshop, mould or core debris is known to anchor a "Gaulish, Rhenish and British bronze trade". | FACT 10, 11; GAPS (no workshop)
DEFECT | MINOR | Its main parallel partly cuts against it: the Geneva die is solid, silver-coated lead and is labelled with the zodiac names, so the one known divination dodecahedron needed lettering; it is also a single late (4th-century) piece outside the hollow corpus. | FACT 43
DEFECT | MINOR | The start date and the end date's tie to the laws of 356-357 rest on context dates, which date deposition, not use (the Gellep woman died c. AD 350); and knobs as feet are not needed, since any polyhedron settles on a face. | FACT 13, 16, 34; Cambridge 2025
DEFECT | MINOR | The Arloff icosahedron, a related hollow knobbed object, has holes on only two faces, so it lacks the hole code this mechanism needs (the idea acknowledges this). | FACT 32
FIT | 12 of 19 | Fits holes that vary within a piece with no shared scheme, no inscriptions, a level top face when resting on five knobs, valued contexts (graves, hoard, shrine), the Kortessem repair, the Danube and Slovak finds and the start c. 150-200; it fails on twelve readable outcomes, on wear and on the cause of the distribution.
SURVIVES | Reading the hole variation as a face code fits the hard evidence best of the six ideas, and its proposed test (all 20 knob tips against their bases, on pieces with original surface) is feasible and genuinely able to refute it.

IDEA 5: Cosmic emblem, sometimes on a staff
DEFECT | MAJOR | It is a symbol reading with no mechanism for the distinctive features: the holes, their varying sizes, the triple rings and the corner knobs are dismissed as decoration, which would fit any provincial bronze. | FACT 1, 2, 3; task criterion 2
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Hole sizes vary because nothing depended on them" is contradicted by Jublains: its ringed holes fall into three tight classes repeated as the same set around both poles (about 0.06% likely by chance), so the sizes were set in the wax model. | FACT 31, 44
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Face holes were optional" (argued from the Arloff icosahedron) clashes with the consistent twelve-hole template across the hollow corpus of about 130 pieces. | FACT 1, 9, 32
DEFECT | MAJOR | Its regional cause ("provincial taste", "regional workshop network") is unsupported: no workshop, mould or core debris is known, and the Mediterranean read the same Timaeus without making the object. | FACT 10, 11; GAPS
DEFECT | MINOR | The staff mount is weak: the Gellep bone object is about 15 cm long and about 3 cm thick, wider than the measured unringed openings (Jublains 21.5 mm and Newhall 21 mm across their narrow axis), and those openings are often oval. | FACT 3, 17, 31; Cambridge 2025 (core-reader excerpt)
DEFECT | MINOR | Its sanctuary examples are thin: Lydney is a fragment whose exact context in the Nodens temple complex is unknown, and only about 8.5% of findspots are plausibly sacred. | FACT 14; Cambridge 2025 (Lydney catalogue entry)
DEFECT | MINOR | The test is lopsided: most rival readings also predict unworn tips, and staff abrasion is expected only "sometimes", so only evenly rounded tips on all 20 knobs can refute it. | FACT 20
FIT | 6 of 15 | Fits low wear, the size spread with no standard, household and grave contexts, no letters, the Kortessem repair and its rejection of a Druid root; it leaves the hole variation, the Jublains classes, the rings, the knobs and the map unexplained.
SURVIVES | Low wear, valued and deposited contexts, and its correct rejection of a Druid-Pythagorean root (the Druids were suppressed 150-300 years earlier) hold up.

IDEA 6: Native priest's radiate sceptre
DEFECT | MAJOR | Priestly regalia should come mostly from temples, but only about 8.5% of findspots are plausibly sacred, over half are civil and under a fifth military (the forts at Feldberg, Zugmantel and Carnuntum), and the British regalia deposits (Wanborough with 17 sceptre handles and 4 headdresses, Hockwold, Farley) contain no dodecahedra. | FACT 14, 15; Esposito, Performing the Sacra; surreyarchaeology.org.uk (Wanborough)
DEFECT | MAJOR | Its test cannot refute it: plant stems are softer than bronze and do not scratch it, and plant residue rarely survives burial and old cleaning, so finding no scratches or residue is expected even if the idea is true. | FACT 16, 20
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Knobs stopped it rolling on altars" gives the knobs no real job, because a twelve-faced body already rests stably on any face, so this distinctive feature is still unexplained. | FACT 1, 34
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Native priesthoods existed only in the Celtic north-west" is unsupported: gutuatri are attested in Gaul (the Autun altars) but not in Britain or the Rhineland, Celtic peoples in Iberia and northern Italy produced no pieces, and the finds in Germanic settlements in Slovakia are left unexplained. | FACT 11, 12; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anvallus (gutuater altars)
DEFECT | MINOR | The rod needs the unringed pair of type 2a, which is often oval, and the Gellep bone object (about 3 cm thick) is wider than the measured openings (21-21.5 mm across their narrow axis); other types without such a pair are left out. | FACT 3, 17, 31; Cambridge 2025
DEFECT | MINOR | The triple engraved rings on the ten sprig holes and the 40-100 mm size spread are left unexplained. | FACT 3, 5
FIT | 7 of 14 | Fits holes that vary within a piece with no shared scheme, the axial unringed pair, the matched size sets around both poles at Jublains, low wear, the silence of art and the end of the Druids; it fails on findspot contexts, the knobs, the regional cause and the eastern finds.
SURVIVES | It gives a coherent reason for the axial unringed pair and for two five-fold rings carrying the same set of sizes (Jublains), and British regalia known only from finds, not from images, matches the silence of art.
