Propose a new architecture for an AI accelerator chip, named "Continuum", that could credibly be called the best in the world. The asker has not said which workloads count: training, inference, or both. They have not said what "best" is measured by, or which kind of target (data center, edge, or other) the chip is for. A good answer therefore has to state its own target workloads and metrics, then defend the architecture against the current leading designs. Those designs include GPUs such as the Nvidia Rubin class, Google TPUs such as Ironwood, and SRAM-centric dataflow chips such as Cerebras WSE-3 and Groq LPU. The architecture should cover compute, memory hierarchy, interconnect, numerics, programming model, packaging and power. It should cite known physical, economic and manufacturing limits, and say what would need to be true for it to work. Reported 2026 reference points are listed in CONTEXT. Many are vendor or blog figures, and the sources disagree on some numbers.
A good answer: 
- The answer states explicit target workloads and a metric set. Examples are tokens/s per user, tokens per joule, cost per token, and training throughput. It says why those metrics define "best".
- It describes a coherent architecture with named blocks and quantified estimates. Estimates cover compute, on-chip and off-chip memory capacity and bandwidth, interconnect, power and die or package size. They are sanity-checked against roofline or bandwidth arithmetic.
- It compares quantitatively against at least three current designs (GPU, TPU, SRAM/wafer-scale) and says where Continuum wins, where it loses, and by how much.
- It names the main risks and the physical, process or supply limits (HBM supply, CoWoS packaging, power density, yield). It gives a falsifiable test or simulation plan that could prove the design wrong.
- It covers the software stack and programming model, and says what happens to existing models and frameworks.
- It separates established facts from the design's own assumptions and estimates.
- "Best in the world" is not asserted without evidence. Trade-offs are stated honestly.
Constraints: 
- The name is "Continuum".
- The answer is an architecture for an AI chip.
- Nothing else was specified. The answer must be a real, physically plausible design, not marketing text.

The final candidates with their blind reviews (data):
IDEA 1
NAME: Node-scale expert-stationary SRAM stacks
MECHANISM: The anchor survives: hybrid-bonded multi-layer SRAM stacked under each compute die holds all expert weights, so HBM carries only KV. Capacity is repaired by putting the stacks on 2-die Rubin-class packages using N5 SRAM dies (est. 2.29 MB/mm²). Three layers give 790 GB per rack, a margin of 1.38 on Kimi K2 and 2.15 on V3; the original design had 0.96. But removing weight reads can save at most W/(N·BW) per step, which is 0.40–0.62 ms on an EP72 rack. Rack-scale gain is therefore only 1.19–1.33×, which fails the 1.5× bar. The repair moves the design to where that term is large: an 8-GPU node, without a 72-GPU NVLink rack (NVL72), serving MoE models of 120B parameters or less (gpt-oss-120b). Four layers give 117 GB per node, a 1.86 capacity margin. Gain is 1.39× at 500 tok/s per user and 1.86× at 800, and above 1,000 tok/s per user the stacked node keeps working where the baseline node cannot reach that speed. The SRAM adds about $24k to a ~$0.95M node.
VALUE: At 500–800 tok/s per user on 8-GPU nodes, cost falls from $0.024–0.032 to $0.017 per million tokens (1.4–1.9× cheaper). Against the same model spread across a full NVL72 rack, the gain is only about 1.06×.
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: The gain only exists against a deliberately weak baseline (an isolated 8-GPU node). A competent operator chasing 500–800+ tok/s per user would use NVL72 (their own figure: 1.06×) or an SRAM system that already exceeds 1,000 tok/s on this exact model. (Weight read per step on NVL72 is 63 GB ÷ 72 ÷ 22 TB/s = 0.04 ms. Cerebras serves gpt-oss-120b at about 1,700 (AA) to 2,700 (vendor) tok/s; the best 8×GB200 result is 650 tok/s.)
- MAJOR: The 1.39× and 1.86× gains need an effective weight-read time of about 0.57 ms per step. At Rubin's 22 TB/s with all experts touched it is 0.36 ms, and at small batch fewer experts are touched. (T/(T−W): with W = 0.36 the gain is 1.21× at 500 and 1.39× at 800; W = 0.57 is needed to get 1.40/1.84. Top-4 of 128 experts at batch 16 touches only 40% of experts.)
- MAJOR: Neither the risk nor the NRE of this design is counted. It puts four hybrid-bonded SRAM layers under a roughly 900 W Rubin-class compute die, so all power and HBM I/O must pass through four TSV layers. It also needs a custom Rubin-class die that only Nvidia can build. (Closest precedent is AMD's single 64 MB layer under a 120 W CCD (9800X3D). The $24k adder covers only about 51,000 mm² of N5 (about 1 wafer at 100% yield, before stack yield and bonding).)
- SURVIVES: 790 GB per rack and 117 GB per node reproduce at about 800 mm² × 2.29 MB/mm². 790/377 = 2.10 (claimed 2.15). Cost ratios of 1.41–1.88 follow from the stated $/M figures.
- RATIO: 1.06 | Against NVL72, the best GPU practice for a 120B MoE at high tok/s per user; below 1 against Cerebras above 1,000 tok/s per user.
- KNOWN: partly | AMD 3D V-Cache (SRAM hybrid-bonded under logic); Cerebras/Groq LPX for SRAM-resident weights; HGX Rubin NVL8 exists as the node baseline.

IDEA 2
NAME: Continuum-D: SRAM experts, LPDDR sparse-KV
MECHANISM: The anchor survives: a 671B MoE (DeepSeek-V3.2 class) sits fully resident in SRAM across standard-packaged dies with no HBM and no CoWoS, and the KV cache goes to commodity LPDDR. The repair changes three things. First, SRAM area is recomputed at a realistic 55% of N3E bitcell density, so 1 GB takes 436 mm² rather than 258 mm². That gives 36 experts per die and one 8+1-die stage per MoE layer, so all-to-all traffic stays inside a stage. The system grows to 527 dies costing $1.44M, under the $1.5M kill line. Second, the target changes from dense-MLA attention to sparse-attention (DSA) MoE models at 32K–128K context. The binding quantity is "drain time": how long a memory takes to be read end to end. Sparse KV at 128K needs about 55 ms at 100 tok/s per user, and LPDDR drains in about 62 ms. HBM4 drains in 13 ms, so it strands bandwidth or runs out of capacity. Dense MLA needs 10 ms, and there the design only ties GPUs ($0.138 vs $0.135–0.157 per million tokens). Third, the system is decode-only: prefill runs on separate GPUs and arrives as about 1.5 GB/s per die of KV writes.
VALUE: At 128K context, sparse attention and 200–400 tok/s per user, it costs about $0.095 per million tokens. The best Vera Rubin NVL72 setup (HBM only, or with CPU-memory KV offload) costs $0.127–0.136, so Continuum-D is 1.34–1.44× cheaper. Against LPX+Rubin it is about 2.9× cheaper, but that figure depends on an estimated LPX price. It is one of the few options above 1,000 tok/s per user.
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: The "decode-only" $/token leaves out the prefill GPUs. By the design's own figure of 1.5 GB/s of KV writes per die, those GPUs cost far more than the Continuum-D system they feed, so the 1.34–1.44× decode saving shrinks to almost nothing on the whole chain. (527 dies × 1.5 GB/s = 790 GB/s of KV ÷ 48 KB/token ((132+656) B × 61 layers) = 16.4M prefill tok/s. At 76.5k tok/s per Rubin (Idea 4) that is about 215 Rubins × ~$108k ($7.8M per rack ÷ 72) ≈ $23M, against $1.44M of decode hardware. Whole-chain ratio is (23.2+2.0)/(23.2+1.44) = 1.02, or 1.15 with a 90% prefix-cache hit rate.)
- MAJOR: The drain-time argument for choosing LPDDR over HBM only holds at 100 tok/s per user. At the 200–400 tok/s where the value is claimed, the required drain time drops to the point where HBM4 is the matched memory and LPDDR is 2.2–4.5× short on bandwidth. (Required drain = 6.30 GB per user ÷ 1.137 GB per step ÷ tok/s. That gives 55.4 ms at 100, 27.7 ms at 200, 13.8 ms at 400 (HBM4 is 13.1 ms) and 5.5 ms at 1,000, against LPDDR's 62 ms (python check).)
- MAJOR: Every die needs a wide LPDDR bus, on a die that is already about 80% SRAM. This is not costed or floor-planned. If KV sits only on the "+1" die of each stage, the bandwidth cannot be built at all. ($0.095/M tokens with a 4-year life, 100–160 kW and $0.10/kWh implies about 150–170k tok/s × 1.137 GB = 170–190 TB/s. That is about 330–360 GB/s per die over 527 dies (a 512-bit LPDDR5X-class bus, like M4 Max), or about 3 TB/s per die if only 61 dies hold KV. The SRAM alone takes 691 mm² per die (36 × 44.0 MB × 436 mm²/GB), leaving under 170 mm² of an 858 mm² reticle for compute, PHYs and SerDes.)
- SURVIVES: Die count of 527 (58 × 9 + 5) and 36 experts × 44.0 MB = 1.59 GB per die reproduce. KV of 6.30 GB per user and 1.137 GB per step at 128K reproduce. Rubin drain of 13.1 ms reproduces.
- RATIO: 1.05 | Whole-chain cost (prefill GPUs plus decode) with the decode gain kept at 1.39×; range 1.02–1.15 depending on prefix-cache hit rate.
- KNOWN: partly | Nvidia Groq 3 LPX (256 SRAM LPUs, 128 GB per rack, decode-only next to Vera Rubin, which does prefill), in full production in 2026 (theregister.com/2026/03/16/nvidia_lpx_groq_3/); Cerebras WSE-3.

IDEA 3
NAME: Touch-rate-split DSA KV tier
MECHANISM: The anchor is kept. The model's own lightning indexer picks the top-2,048 entries, and a hardware engine gathers exactly those 656-B MLA latents from on-package LPDDR6. The 132-B FP8 indexer keys stay in HBM and are scanned in full every step. The repair drops the bandwidth and energy claim, which was false: against FlashMLA-sparse, HBM bytes per step fall only 1.137→1.055 GB (1.08×), and memory energy is 31.3 vs 31.7 mJ per token, so no gain. The design is restated as a capacity split by touch rate. The indexer needs about 100 reads per second of its own capacity, which nearly matches HBM4's 76 per second, while the latent is touched at only 2,048/L. Each byte class therefore goes to the memory whose bandwidth-to-capacity ratio fits it. The decode die (Continuum-D) has 4 HBM4 stacks, 4 LPDDR6 modules (512 GB, ~0.7 TB/s), a hardware per-layer top-k unit and an LRU hot-buffer tag directory in HBM. At 128K context and 100 tok/s it serves 74 users, or 18.4 per HBM stack, against 5.0 per stack for an all-HBM Rubin. That is 3.9×, and the limit is HBM bandwidth spent on the indexer scan. LPDDR miss traffic (79 GB/s) and gather latency (37 of 164 µs per layer) both have headroom down to a hot-buffer hit rate of about 0.4.
VALUE: Cost per 128K user slot is 1.0× (2026 prices) to 1.0× (2027) that of the best practice, Rubin+Vera with HiSparse offload ($86–100 vs $102 per user slot). The win is 1.9× users per HBM stack when top-k locality is low (hit ≤0.4), and 1.8–3.4× users per chip on accelerators that lack a large coherent host memory, such as Ironwood-class chips (47.7–89.5 vs 26.6 users).
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: The baseline already has the same tier. Vera gives each Rubin about 750 GB of LPDDR5X at about 0.6 TB/s, over a 0.9 TB/s C2C share, and HiSparse already keeps indexer and hot data on the GPU with latents in host memory. So the claimed "1.9× users per HBM stack when hit ≤0.4" has no mechanism behind it. (At hit 0.4, miss traffic for 74 users is 364 GB/s, which is below Vera's per-GPU share of about 0.6 TB/s. Users per stack limited by the indexer scan are the same for both: 2.75 TB/s ÷ 105.5 GB/s ≈ 26.)
- MAJOR: The cost per slot cannot cover the die's own memory at 2026 prices. (74 × $86–100 = $6.4–7.4k per die. Memory alone is 512 GB LPDDR × ~$12/GB = $6.1k plus 144 GB HBM4 × ~$17/GB = $2.5k, so at least $116 per slot before any silicon (against $102 for the baseline).)
- MAJOR: It uses the name "Continuum-D" for a die with 4 HBM4 stacks, while Idea 1's Continuum-D is defined as having no HBM. The two tiers cannot both be the architecture. (Idea 1 says "no HBM and no CoWoS"; Idea 2 says "4 HBM4 stacks".)
- SURVIVES: 1.055 and 1.137 GB per step, 82 MB of latent per step, 164 µs × 61 = 10.0 ms, and 74 users × 105.5 GB/s = 1.95 TB/s per stack all reproduce. The "no bandwidth or energy gain" concession is correct.
- RATIO: 1.0 | Same mechanism and roughly the same LPDDR bandwidth and capacity as Rubin+Vera with HiSparse; at 2026 memory prices it is ≤1.0.
- KNOWN: yes | SGLang HiSparse, April 2026 (lmsys.org/blog/2026-04-10-sglang-hisparse); ESS latent-cache offload for V3.2-Exp (arxiv.org/abs/2512.10576).

IDEA 4
NAME: SRAM-tiled LPDDR prefill die
MECHANISM: The anchor is kept: a prefill die with no HBM, sharing an ISA with the decode tier and passing it KV (132 B to decode-tier HBM, 656 B to its LPDDR, 1.8 GB/s per die). The original threshold was wrong. The die's ridge is 12,500 FLOP/B (17.5 PF ÷ 1.4 TB/s), not 300. MoE expert-stationary streaming is also capped at about 2·F_expert = 4,096 FLOP/B whatever the batch, so an LPDDR-only die with 256 MB of SRAM reaches only 34% of its compute at 128K. Two changes fix this. First, each package holds a full NVFP4 replica of the model (377 GB in 512 GB LPDDR6), so there is no expert-parallel all-to-all. Second, a hybrid-bonded SRAM token-tile buffer (≥0.75 GB per die at 128K, ≥1.0 GB at 32K; 1.45 GB planned) keeps tiles of 31–65K tokens resident while weights stream past. The package then becomes compute-bound at 76.5k tok/s at 128K, the same as Rubin. A CPX-like part that needs EP3 over a 200 GB/s NIC is network-bound at 30k tok/s. The price is at least 64K tokens per pass, which means about 1–2 s of batching latency.
VALUE: Prefill throughput per FLOP matches Rubin with zero HBM stacks. BOM per ktok/s is 1.0× Rubin at 2026 HBM prices ($115 vs $114) and 1.6× cheaper at 2027 prices ($115 vs $187). Energy per token is the same (23.8 vs 24.2 mJ).
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: The CPX comparison is a straw man. Prefill across Rubin CPX can use pipeline parallelism instead of expert parallelism, and then the NIC is nowhere near the limit, so CPX is compute-bound rather than "network-bound at 30k tok/s". (PP activation traffic at 30k tok/s is 7,168 × 2 B × 30k = 0.43 GB/s, against a 200 GB/s NIC. At 457 GF/token, CPX (30 PF) is compute-bound at 65.6k tok/s, which is 0.86× this package with one die and no HBM.)
- MAJOR: The BOM leaves out the 2026 LPDDR price rise. The 512 GB of LPDDR alone costs more than Rubin's HBM, and the 2027 "1.6×" assumes HBM rises 121% while LPDDR stays flat. (512 GB × ~$12/GB = $6.1k, which is 70% of the claimed $8.8k package BOM, against $4.9k of HBM4 in Rubin (UBS). That makes the BOM about $10.5k, or $137 per ktok/s (0.83× in 2026). In 2027 it is 1.39× with LPDDR flat and 1.07× with LPDDR +50%.)
- MAJOR: The memory bandwidth contradicts Idea 2. A die needs 1.4 TB/s from LPDDR6 (about a 1,050-bit bus per die, about 2,100 bits per package), while Idea 2 says the same 512 GB in 4 modules gives 0.7 TB/s, which is 4× less per package. (1.4e12 × 8 ÷ 10.667 Gb/s ≈ 1,050 bits. At 0.7 TB/s per package the ridge is 50,000 FLOP/B, and a 128K DSA pass needs about 108K tokens.)
- SURVIVES: Ridge of 12,500, the 34% figure (4,096/12,500), NVFP4 size of 377 GB, KV rate of 1.84 GB/s per die, 1.82 kW power (23.8 mJ × 76.5k), and the $187/$115 = 1.63 arithmetic all reproduce.
- RATIO: 0.9 | Corrected 2026 BOM (0.83) up to a 2027 asymmetric-HBM case (1.07–1.39), against CPX with pipeline parallelism or Rubin.
- KNOWN: yes | Nvidia Rubin CPX: a prefill part with no HBM, 128 GB GDDR7 and 30 PF NVFP4 in the NVL144 CPX rack, shipping late 2026 (guru3d.com/story/nvidia-rubin-cpx-gpu-announced-at-ai-infra-summit-2025/).
