Propose a new architecture for an AI accelerator chip, named "Continuum", that could credibly be called the best in the world. The asker has not said which workloads count: training, inference, or both. They have not said what "best" is measured by, or which kind of target (data center, edge, or other) the chip is for. A good answer therefore has to state its own target workloads and metrics, then defend the architecture against the current leading designs. Those designs include GPUs such as the Nvidia Rubin class, Google TPUs such as Ironwood, and SRAM-centric dataflow chips such as Cerebras WSE-3 and Groq LPU. The architecture should cover compute, memory hierarchy, interconnect, numerics, programming model, packaging and power. It should cite known physical, economic and manufacturing limits, and say what would need to be true for it to work. Reported 2026 reference points are listed in CONTEXT. Many are vendor or blog figures, and the sources disagree on some numbers.
A good answer: 
- The answer states explicit target workloads and a metric set. Examples are tokens/s per user, tokens per joule, cost per token, and training throughput. It says why those metrics define "best".
- It describes a coherent architecture with named blocks and quantified estimates. Estimates cover compute, on-chip and off-chip memory capacity and bandwidth, interconnect, power and die or package size. They are sanity-checked against roofline or bandwidth arithmetic.
- It compares quantitatively against at least three current designs (GPU, TPU, SRAM/wafer-scale) and says where Continuum wins, where it loses, and by how much.
- It names the main risks and the physical, process or supply limits (HBM supply, CoWoS packaging, power density, yield). It gives a falsifiable test or simulation plan that could prove the design wrong.
- It covers the software stack and programming model, and says what happens to existing models and frameworks.
- It separates established facts from the design's own assumptions and estimates.
- "Best in the world" is not asserted without evidence. Trade-offs are stated honestly.
Constraints: 
- The name is "Continuum".
- The answer is an architecture for an AI chip.
- Nothing else was specified. The answer must be a real, physically plausible design, not marketing text.

Candidate answers (data):
IDEA 1
NAME: Expert-stationary stacked SRAM
MECHANISM: Four-high hybrid-bonded SRAM layers (V-Cache-style; est. ~7 GB per chip) hold all MoE expert weights resident across a 64-chip rack. Each step therefore reads weights at about 0.2 pJ/bit instead of streaming them from HBM, and HBM is left serving only KV. The closest attempts, Groq (230 MB) and Cerebras (44 GB), failed on capacity because they tried to fit KV too. Here 3D stacking gives about 30× Groq's per-chip capacity, and KV stays off-SRAM. The deciding quantity is SRAM $/GB: about $170/GB (est.) × 178 GB ≈ $30k per rack, negligible next to rack cost.
VALUE: Gives about 1.4–1.6× lower J/token and about 1.6× more users per rack at 50 tok/s/user, because all HBM bandwidth goes to KV.

IDEA 2
NAME: Continuum air-cooled FP4 trainer
MECHANISM: Digital FP4 matrix units with per-16-element scales and FP8 fallback for sensitive layers run in 40 kW air-coolable racks on organic-substrate packaging, avoiding CoWoS. The closest earlier attempt, NVFP4 training, stayed within 1–1.5% of FP8 only up to 12B parameters. Deciding quantity: the FP4/FP8 loss gap at 70B or more over 10T tokens.
VALUE: About 1.3–1.5x cheaper pretraining in existing data halls, a gain capped by staff cost.

IDEA 3
NAME: Continuum-T FP4 training mode
MECHANISM: The tensor cores run NVFP4 with per-block FP8 fallback, chosen per layer by a hardware overflow and stochastic-rounding monitor that promotes unstable layers and the final phase of training. This doubles throughput per mm² over FP8. The closest earlier attempt is NVFP4, which is proven only to 12B and lost on code benchmarks; the automatic promotion bounds that loss. The deciding quantity is tokens-to-target-loss at ≥70B within 10% of FP8. Hardware is 44% of training cost, so the gain is ≈1.3× cost, not 3×.
VALUE: Frontier training about 1.3× cheaper, and the design is falsifiable on a single 70B run.

IDEA 4
NAME: Continuum-D: SRAM weights, LPDDR KV
MECHANISM: A FP4 671B-parameter MoE (about 335 GB) sits resident in SRAM across about 340 standard-packaged, CoWoS-free dies of about 800 mm² each, with roughly 1 GB per die. Weight reads cost no HBM and about 40x less energy. The KV cache, which is about 2.2 GB per 32K-token MLA user, lives in commodity LPDDR6 at about 1 TB/s per die. The closest earlier attempt is Groq, which needed 576 chips for one 70B model because it put everything in SRAM. Continuum-D avoids this because the weights are shared across a large MoE batch, and the per-user KV goes to cheap, abundant DRAM instead of SRAM. The deciding quantity is SRAM cost per GB of resident weights versus HBM-rack cost per GB/s of weight traffic. It fails if 340 dies cost more than about $1.5M.
VALUE: It delivers high tok/s per user on frontier MoE models with zero HBM stacks and zero CoWoS wafers, which bypasses the 2026–28 supply limits that cap every GPU and TPU.

IDEA 5
NAME: Continuum-D 3D-DRAM decode
MECHANISM: A logic die is hybrid-bonded under 4 DRAM stacks (est. 64 GB, 16 TB/s, ~0.5 pJ/bit, versus ~4 pJ/bit for HBM3E). Each chip holds its expert shard plus the KV of its own users, so no byte crosses a PHY. Roofline: at 4 pJ/bit, reading 8.6 GB of KV per token costs ~275 mJ; at 0.5 pJ/bit it costs ~34 mJ. If HBM streaming is ~50% of decode energy (est.), total energy/token falls ~1.8×. The closest earlier attempt is HBM-PIM, which stalled on programmability; here the DRAM is plain addressable memory with no in-DRAM logic. The deciding quantity is delivered pJ/bit at ≥60% of peak bandwidth (H100 reached only 27%).
VALUE: About 1.8× more tokens per joule at long context, with no HBM supply dependence.

IDEA 6
NAME: HBM-free air-cooled decode rack
MECHANISM: Organic-substrate accelerators (no CoWoS) are each paired with 16 GDDR7 devices, giving about 2 TB/s and 32 GB per chip. That holds about 7 users at 128K and 230 GB/s each, so capacity and bandwidth are balanced. Racks are capped at 40 kW so they fit existing air-cooled halls. The closest attempt, CXL memory pooling, offered capacity without bandwidth. Here every KV byte is local at full rate. The deciding quantity is decode bandwidth per dollar against GB300, which must beat it about 2×; this holds only if GDDR7 supply and its ~5 pJ/bit energy do not erase the saving.
VALUE: Lowers $/token by removing HBM and packaging (about 62% of BOM) and the 120 kW facility requirement, though it loses roughly 1.3× on J/token.

IDEA 7
NAME: Hybrid-bonded DRAM-over-MAC decode die
MECHANISM: DRAM layers are hybrid-bonded directly over a logic die, so weights and KV stay stationary within micrometres of the GEMV units, and the compiler sees the bank-edge matmul as an ordinary tensor op. The closest earlier attempt is HBM-PIM, which stalled on programmability and DRAM-process logic. Here the logic sits on a logic-process die and is exposed only through a standard compiler path. The deciding quantity is delivered pJ/bit at the junction temperature, including refresh.
VALUE: About 1.6x lower decode energy per token without any HBM supply.

IDEA 8
NAME: Attention in HBM4 base die
MECHANISM: Each HBM4 stack's custom logic base die runs GQA decode attention (QKᵀ, softmax, AV at FP8) on KV read through the TSVs, skipping the PHY and interposer, and sends only the partial outputs to the main die. The closest attempt, HBM-PIM, stalled on general programmability. Here a single fixed compiler op with a small vector unit avoids that. The deciding quantity is attention intensity × in-stack bandwidth against the base-die thermal budget: about 16 FLOP/B × 2 TB/s ≈ 32 TFLOPS ≈ 16 W per stack, which I estimate is tolerable. MLA, at about 480 FLOP/B, falls outside it.
VALUE: Cuts KV-movement energy about 2× (est. ~1.5 vs ~3.9 pJ/bit) for long-context GQA decode, the largest term in decode energy.

IDEA 9
NAME: Commodity-DRAM air-cooled MoE decoder
MECHANISM: Each chip carries about 512 GB of LPDDR-class DRAM (A) on an organic substrate, holds fixed experts locally, and moves activations instead of weights. The rack stays at or below 40 kW, which allows air cooling. The closest earlier attempts are Groq and Cerebras, whose SRAM capacity forced hundreds of chips or off-wafer streaming; capacity here comes from commodity DRAM. The deciding quantity is all-to-all link time per token relative to DRAM read time.
VALUE: MoE decode on standard packaging in existing air-cooled halls, at an estimated 2x lower cost per token.

IDEA 10
NAME: Continuum stacked-DRAM decode die
MECHANISM: Four DRAM layers are hybrid-bonded directly onto an 800 mm² logic die, holding about 120 GB of expert weights and KV at about 40 TB/s and about 0.5 pJ/bit (assumed), with no HBM or CoWoS interposer. Logic sits next to the cold plate, and refresh rate tracks measured die temperature. The closest earlier attempt, HBM-PIM, stalled on programmability; here the logic is an ordinary compiler target and the DRAM is plain memory. Deciding quantity: delivered pJ/bit including refresh at 95 °C.
VALUE: About 1.6–1.9x tokens per joule on MoE decode versus Rubin-class HBM, without consuming HBM supply.

IDEA 11
NAME: Memory-light prefill compute die
MECHANISM: A dense FP4/FP8 compute die with only LPDDR runs the compute-bound prefill phase. It hands KV to the decode tier over a fabric and shares an ISA and compiler with that tier. The closest earlier attempt is Nvidia's Rubin CPX, which uses a separate GDDR7 stack. This design avoids GDDR shortages and keeps a single toolchain across both phases. The deciding quantity is prefill FLOP/byte, which must stay above about 300.
VALUE: Takes prefill off scarce HBM and CoWoS, roughly halving HBM demand per served token (A).

IDEA 12
NAME: Continuum-T: HBM-lean training die
MECHANISM: A training die carries 4 HBM4 stacks instead of 8 and adds 3D-stacked SRAM (SoIC) for GEMM tiles. Optimizer state and activations are sharded over the fabric in ZeRO style. The bet is that large training GEMMs exceed about 560 FLOP/B, so they do not need B200-class HBM bandwidth. The closest earlier attempt is TPU Ironwood, which still provisions 8 stacks. The deciding quantity is achieved MFU when HBM bandwidth per FLOP is halved. This can be tested today by down-clocking HBM on B200s during a 10B+ parameter run.
VALUE: It gives about 1.6–2x the training throughput per HBM stack, which makes it the best FLOP-per-scarce-input design if MFU falls by less than 15%.

IDEA 13
NAME: Continuum-P LPDDR prefill die
MECHANISM: A reticle-sized FP4/FP8 compute die (est. 10 PFLOPS) sits with 256 GB of LPDDR6 (~1 TB/s) on an organic substrate, with no CoWoS and no HBM. An 8K-token prefill chunk does ~16K FLOP per weight byte, which is above the 10K FLOP/B ridge point, so cheap memory does not throttle it. Estimated BOM is ~$2.5k against $6.4k. The closest earlier attempt is Rubin CPX on GDDR7; Continuum is open-licensed rather than tied to one vendor's margin. The deciding quantity is sustained prefill MFU ≥50%, given the LPDDR bandwidth.
VALUE: About 2.5× lower BOM per prefill FLOP, built from uncontended supply chains.

IDEA 14
NAME: Continuum-K: attention on HBM base-die
MECHANISM: Fixed-function attention units on the custom HBM4 base die read the KV cache over TSVs. Only the query vectors in and the attention outputs back cross the PHY, which is kilobytes instead of gigabytes per step. The closest earlier attempts are Samsung HBM-PIM and UPMEM, which died on general programmability. Continuum-K exposes exactly one kernel that the compiler emits, so programmability is not required. The deciding quantity is the PHY's share of HBM read energy. If it is about 50%, decode energy falls only about 1.3x.
VALUE: It cuts long-context decode joules per token and lifts the KV-limited batch per facility kW, but the gain is likely about 1.2–1.5x, not several-fold.

IDEA 15
NAME: Continuum split attention-expert fabric
MECHANISM: Attention dies keep each request's KV in near-stacked DRAM. Separate expert dies hold FFN weights in cheap LPDDR and gather tokens from about 2,000 concurrent requests, so each expert becomes compute-bound. Routing runs over an optical scale-up fabric. The closest earlier attempt, attention-FFN disaggregation such as MegaScale-Infer, still used HBM on both sides. Deciding quantity: tokens batched per expert per step versus the fabric round-trip latency within a 10 ms token budget.
VALUE: Moves weight capacity onto commodity DRAM, cutting accelerator BOM by roughly 30–40%.

LESSONS:
- **Capacity versus bandwidth.** SRAM gives bandwidth but cannot hold frontier weights plus KV. Examples are 230 MB per Groq chip and 44 GB per wafer, and they force hundreds of chips or off-wafer streaming. HBM gives capacity but is supply-limited and still bandwidth-bound at decode. A new design must say where the weights live, where the KV lives, and the byte/s each tier costs.
- **Supply and facility limits.** Any plan that needs scarce HBM, scarce CoWoS or 120 kW racks inherits those limits. Lower-density, standard-packaging options are a real advantage.
- **Software killed more challengers than silicon.** Gaudi, Graphcore and PIM all stalled on it. Unmodified PyTorch, or a compiler path that does not lose to CUDA, is required from the start.
- **Exotic compute stays inference-only.** Analog and photonic compute give about 8-bit precision, so they cannot train. A claim to beat GPUs needs digital low-precision numerics, with FP4-class training evidence beyond 12B.
- **Fixed-function bets are risky.** Transformer-only silicon fails if the model architecture shifts. Wafer-scale yield and memory limits were cited in the Dojo end.
- **Search gaps.** I used 14 of 15 searches. Rubin per-chip power, Ironwood specs and SambaNova/d-Matrix status were not found, so do not treat them as established.
