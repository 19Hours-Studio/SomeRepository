Russia holds vast natural resources (oil, gas, metals, diamonds, coal, rare earths) in Siberia, Yakutia and the Arctic, mostly under permafrost and far from roads, railways and ports. Propose a fundamentally new way to reach, extract, process or bring these resources to market that is extremely cheap (several times cheaper than current practice), has never been tried or seriously costed by anyone, can be deployed within a few years, and works with known physics. Not incremental improvements of existing mining, drilling, pipelines or winter-road logistics.

The final candidates with their blind reviews (data):
IDEA 1
NAME: Pre-staged flood-crest barges
MECHANISM: Anchor: a loaded barge waits out the ice drift in a sheltered backwater and leaves on the flood crest. Repair: load it in autumn while it is still afloat at a deep berth, so nothing is loaded through ice. It winters in a backwater at least 2.5 m deep, and it is used on a tributary that is navigable only on the spring flood, where there is just one trip a year anyway. With a modelled 12-day recession, a convoy arriving 5–10 days late loses 35–62% of its load. A barge pre-staged at the river mouth goes in full.
VALUE: About 3,100 rub/t against 4,700–11,300 rub/t on that leg today (1.5–3.6x). On the Kolyma main stem the gain is only about 14%.
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: 2.5 m of backwater is not enough to winter a loaded barge. (Mean ice is 1.42–1.67 m, plus about 1.3–2 m of loaded draft, which needs 2.7–3.7 m. The barge freezes to the bed or sits on it, and the winter drop in stage makes it worse.)
- MAJOR: A frozen-in loaded barge in a backwater at break-up is exposed to ice jams. The loss risk and insurance, plus the cost of capital in barge and cargo idle for 7–8 months, aren't costed. (No loss probability or capital charge is given.)
- SURVIVES: The 1.5–3.6x arithmetic reproduces, and the idea concedes a 14% gain on the Kolyma main stem.
- RATIO: 1.3 | After lost hulls, capital tied up and cutting the barge out of the ice.
- KNOWN: partly | Wintering loaded vessels in backwaters for a spring high-water run is, as far as I know, a long-standing practice on Yakutia's small rivers. My search didn't confirm it.

IDEA 2
NAME: Fuel on empty coal-barge legs
MECHANISM: Anchor: one Kolyma flow rides on the other flow's empty leg. The data reverse the direction. Only about 3 kt of dry cargo goes upstream against 165 kt of coal coming down, so at most 1.8% of the coal could ride on returning barges. The ~165 coal-barge legs going back upstream empty are the real spare capacity. About 17 kt/yr of Northern Delivery fuel would ride those legs in 42 t collapsible fuel bladders, with the coal hold acting as spill containment. That needs 17 of the 165 legs, about 100 bladders and 24 per shoal-limited 1,000 t leg.
VALUE: About 2,100 rub/t for the river leg against 2,700–12,000 rub/t for a dedicated 38–54-year-old tanker (1.3–5.7x). That is under 10% of the delivered diesel price. The real value is not having to replace the tanker fleet.
BLIND REVIEW:
- MAJOR: Sea tankers reach the Kolyma mouth only after the ice clears, in July–August, so the upstream fuel window is about 50–60 days of falling water. Each bladder needs about 4 round trips of 12–15 days. (4 × 12–15 = 48–60 days. Zelyony Mys navigation averages 85 days ([Ruwiki](https://ru.ruwiki.ru/wiki/Зелёный_Мыс_(порт))).)
- MAJOR: Under the River Register, carrying oil in flexible tanks in a dry-cargo coal hold needs a fire and oil-cargo class. Coal residue can puncture the bladders, and partly filled bladders have a free-surface effect. None of this is costed. (No compliance cost is given.)
- SURVIVES: 3/165 = 1.8%, 17,000/42 = 405 bladder-trips = 17 legs × 24, and 24 × 42 = 1,008 t.
- RATIO: 1.2 | Against the existing tanker, after bladder handling.
- KNOWN: partly | Flexible fuel tanks rated to –60 °C are sold for northern supply ([bizon](https://bizon.ru/news/print/id/398842/format/smoothbox)).

IDEA 3
NAME: Self-heated barge boilerhouse
MECHANISM: Anchor: the coal barge is itself the village boilerhouse and coal yard, frozen in at the berth. Repair: drop the bubbler. A covered 1 m moat is kept open with the boiler's own spare low-grade heat. It needs about 8 kW against roughly 480 kW of flue loss from a 3 MW boiler (0.3%), or 36 kW if the moat is left uncovered. The hull should sit in a backwater, not in the main channel. It pays best where the shore boilerhouse has to be replaced anyway.
VALUE: A net saving of 0.8–3.4k rub/t out of 15–23k delivered, about 1.04–1.3x cheaper.
BLIND REVIEW:
- FATAL: The gain is 1.04–1.3x, nowhere near the "several times cheaper" the brief requires. (15/(15–0.8) = 1.06, and 15/(15–3.4) = 1.29.)
- MAJOR: The uncovered-moat heat is understated by 1.3–3x. (Open water in winter loses 250–575 W/m² ([Makinson Inlet](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/view/65922), Mertz). On about 190 m² of moat that is 48–110 kW, not 36 kW.)
- MAJOR: A backwater berth has to stay deeper than ice plus draft, and the heat-network link has to ride the spring stage rise and ice jams. If the barge fails, the village loses its only heat source in mid-winter. (Mean end-of-April ice is 1.42 m on the Kolyma, 1.52 m on the Yana and 1.67 m on the Indigirka ([AARI](https://old.aari.ru/resources/f0021/2016_spring.pdf)). With a 2 m draft that needs 3.4–3.7 m of water.)
- SURVIVES: 8 kW / 3 MW = 0.27%, and 480 kW is 16% flue loss, which is plausible.
- RATIO: 1.05 | The idea's own savings, less the heat-network link.
- KNOWN: partly | Floating power and heat plants exist, e.g. Akademik Lomonosov at Pevek, 70 MWe.

IDEA 4
NAME: Scrape-thawed river-bluff lignite
MECHANISM: Anchor: the river has already exposed the seam, so it is mined by natural thaw only, with no drilling, blasting or overburden stripping. Repair: scrape a 0.1 m thawed layer off the bluff face every 1–3 days. Each scrape restarts the thaw, so the face thaws 3–7 cm/day, or 2.7–5.6 m per 85-day season, against 0.9–1.35 m when the face is left alone (a 1951 placer-mining paper reports about 10 cm/day on a freshly exposed surface). The best regime is one bluff with a seam at least 6 m thick, supplying 20 kt/yr to several villages from about 850 m of face. It works only above high water, behind a berm and settling sump, and the whole face has to retreat together so that no frozen overhang forms.
VALUE: About 3,200 rub/t of lignite, or 1,390 rub per useful Gcal. That is 2.8–4.3x cheaper than imported hard coal at 3,850–5,900 rub/Gcal, but only 1.0–1.5x cheaper than a conventional micro-pit on the same deposit, and 1.3–2.0x if one village is served alone.
BLIND REVIEW:
- FATAL: "The whole face retreats together" means the overburden above the seam has to be thawed and removed as well, so there is stripping after all. (A 20–30 m bluff over a 6 m seam has 14–24 m of overburden, which is 2.3–4 m³ per m³ of coal, or 46–80 k m³ a year.)
- MAJOR: At the low end of the thaw range the face doesn't deliver 20 kt. (850 × 6 × 2.7 × 1.25 = 17.2 kt. It needs ≥3.14 m of thaw a season.)
- MAJOR: Thawed overburden slumping down the face mixes waste into the coal, and coal exposed at the face is weathered, so its heating value drops. (Not in the cost.)
- SURVIVES: 3,200/1,390 = 2.30 useful Gcal/t, and 2.8–4.2x against imported coal.
- RATIO: 0.8 | Against a micro-pit on the same deposit, once overburden retreat is counted.
- KNOWN: partly | Thawing and scraping off thawed layers in successive passes is standard in Kolyma placer stripping, which is where the 1951 paper comes from.
