=== CHECKED D01 ===
ANCHOR: Paper-machine laminate wound into oversized spiral cartridges
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: A 50 µm porous cellulose sheet lets 1 M of small redox ions through (D_eff ≈ 1.5e-10 m²/s after porosity and tortuosity) at F·D·c/L ≈ 290 A/m². That is about three times the ~100 A/m² an oversized cell carries, so most of the charge would cross the separator. The scaling law is that the crossover fraction F·D·c/(L·i) times the ohmic fraction i·L/(σ·V) is fixed at F·D·c/(σ·V) ≈ 0.0024 (σ ≈ 20 S/m, V ≈ 1 V) whatever the thickness or current, so oversizing (lower i) makes crossover worse and only the diffusivity ratio of active species to salt ions can move it. A 30 µm regenerated-cellulose (cellophane) film with redox polymers of tens of kDa (D_eff ≈ 1e-13 m²/s, assumed) gives ~0.3 A/m² of crossover, ~1% of a 30 A/m² current, with under 1 mV of separator drop.
NAME: Paper-machine cellophane spiral cartridges
MECHANISM: A paper machine lays carbon-paper electrodes on both faces of a ~30 µm regenerated-cellulose film in one pass. The laminate is wound with mesh spacers into short spiral rolls stacked like coins in a mass-produced pressure tube. Each roll is ~6 cm tall and collected along both end faces, because 0.3 Ω/sq carbon paper at 30 A/m² already loses 20 mV over 6.7 cm. The electrolytes carry fast outer-sphere redox polymers (for example TEMPO and viologen polymers) far above the film's size cutoff, so the film holds them back about a thousandfold while small salt ions pass freely. As a result, crossover is ~0.3 A/m² against a 30 A/m² operating current (~1%) without any ion-exchange membrane or catalyst. Each m² of laminate gives ~30 W, and spent rolls are re-pulped to recover the carbon.
VALUE: Flow-battery builders get a converter that costs about as much as paper and cellophane (~$70/kW if the laminate costs ~$2/m², assumed) and separates the electrolytes as well as a membrane, which removes the stack cost that dominates long-duration storage.

=== CHECKED D02 ===
ANCHOR: Charge formed in place in conductive sand, behind a plain-sand diffusion barrier
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: With 1 M active species, D_eff ≈ 2e-10 m²/s in sand and a 0.1 m barrier, the two species leak toward each other at 2F·D_eff·c/L ≈ 0.39 A/m². That is ~2.7%/day of the 1.3e6 C/m² stored for a 5-day discharge at 3 A/m², so about half the charge is gone in a month instead of holding for months (~5x off). The scaling law is that daily loss × ohmic fraction ≈ 2F·D_eff·c·(1 day)·(L+h)/(σ_eff·V·L·t_d), so barrier thickness L only trades one loss for the other. Only lowering c while keeping σ with supporting salt, or lengthening the discharge, closes the gap. With 0.2 M active species in ~4 M NaCl (σ_eff ≈ 5 S/m), L = 0.13 m, 0.15 m conductive zones and a 1.7 A/m² one-week discharge, the leak is ~0.06 A/m² (~0.5%/day) and the ohmic loss ~8%.
NAME: Dilute sand-pit bipolar stack
MECHANISM: A lined pit holds stacked layers between bipolar carbon sheets. Each layer has ~0.15 m of carbon-coated conductive sand against each sheet, where the soluble charged species form and stay, and a ~0.13 m plain-sand barrier in between. The brine carries only 0.2 M active species in ~4 M NaCl, so conductivity stays ≈5 S/m in the sand while the force driving crossover drops five-fold. That gives ~0.06 A/m² of leak against a 1.7 A/m² one-week discharge, about 0.5% of stored charge per day, with ~8% ohmic loss each way. Each 0.43 m layer stores ~0.34 kWh per m² of sheet (~0.8 kWh per m³ of pit), and ~20 layers in a 10 m pit make a ~25 V series stack.
VALUE: Grid operators get membrane-free storage that holds charge through multi-week lulls (~15% loss per month), with both the energy and power parts built from earthworks, sand, salt brine and carbon sheet.

=== CHECKED D03 ===
ANCHOR: Single electrolyte that sorts itself by density, with dipped electrodes
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: A 5 m pit of 1 M electrolyte stores F·c·H/2 ≈ 2.4e8 C per m² of footprint, so a one-week discharge needs ~400 A/m² through 5 m of brine (σ ≈ 20 S/m). That is ~100 V of ohmic drop against a ~1.2 V cell, roughly 1000x too much. The scaling law is that the drop, F·c·H²/(2σ·t_d), grows with depth squared, while leak across a middle buffer of thickness L is 2F·D·c/L (D ≈ 1e-9 m²/s). With H ≈ 0.4 m, c = 0.3 M, a permanent 0.25 m spent middle layer and 3 A/m², the ohmic loss is ~5% and the leak (0.23 A/m²) is ~8% of the current.
NAME: Shallow self-sorting electrolyte pond
MECHANISM: A shallow lined pond about 0.4 m deep holds one membraneless electrolyte with 0.3 M of the reactant pair in a strong supporting salt, with a carbon mesh on the floor and one at the surface. Charging makes a dense oxidized form that pools on the floor and a light reduced form that floats. Discharged fluid rises or sinks by its own buoyancy into a permanent 0.25 m spent middle layer that keeps the two apart. Because ohmic drop grows with depth squared, the discharge time fixes the depth: at 3 A/m² for one week, ohmic loss is ~5%, leak ~8% and idle loss ~1%/day, so charging is still timed by the weather forecast. The pond is split into ~3 m cells wired in series so the sideways drop in each mesh stays near 30 mV, giving ~0.6 kWh and ~3.6 W per m² of pond.
VALUE: Grid operators can cover week-long lulls with a shallow lined pond holding one liquid and two meshes per cell, with no membranes, pumps or tanks.

=== CHECKED D04 ===
ANCHOR: Buried magnetite ore used in place as solid electrodes
VERDICT: FAIL
ESTIMATE: Formation free energies put the hematite/magnetite and magnetite/ferrous-hydroxide couples only ~0.37 V apart, so 100 MW needs ~2.7e8 A. Holding ohmic loss to 10% then needs under ~1.4e-10 Ω, which through 30 m of brine-filled rock (σ ≈ 0.1 S/m) means ~2e12 m² of facing lens area. A real 1 km² lens pair carries ~1e-4 A/m², about 50 W in total, so the design falls short by ~10^6. The length of the ion path between the lenses controls the power, not the mineral surface area. That path cannot be shortened in place, because two electrodes inside one conductive ore body short-circuit.
NAME: Layered magnetite tailings in mine voids
MECHANISM: The solid-state iron-oxide chemistry stays the same: in carbonate-buffered alkaline brine, magnetite oxidizes to maghemite/hematite on one side and reduces to ferrous hydroxide on the other, and nothing dissolves. The lens-to-lens ion path is replaced with fine magnetite tailings laid in a flooded mine void as 2 cm beds, separated by 1 cm sand layers and stacked between bipolar graphite sheets. With a ~3 cm ion path at ~5 S/m, a 10% loss of the 0.37 V allows ~5 A/m² (~2 W/m²). At an assumed 20% use of the 28 Wh/kg theoretical capacity, the ~120 kg/m² of ore per cell stores ~0.7 kWh/m²: about two weeks of discharge for ~$7/kWh of graphite sheet (assumed $5/m²). The charged ferrous side sits ~0.16 V below the hydrogen line, so its slow hydrogen-releasing decay sets the true holding time and must be measured first.
VALUE: Owners of flooded iron mines and tailings dumps gain a non-polluting store with two-week discharge, built from waste ore, sand and carbon sheet.

=== CHECKED D05 ===
ANCHOR: Stepped-potential cascade that identifies couples in waste brine by charge counting
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: An all-iron flow battery (~1.2 V) needs ~3.5 kg of iron per kWh (31 mol of electrons, 47 mol Fe at ~70% use), i.e. ~3500 t per GWh. A large acid-mine discharge (50 L/s at 200 mg/L Fe) carries only ~300 t/yr, which means ~11 years and ~1.7e7 m³ of water per GWh. Electrolyte supply, not the stack, therefore limits the claim. The controlling ratio is the iron concentration of the feed: spent steel pickle liquor (~120 g/L Fe, assumed, about 600x richer) needs only ~30,000 m³ per GWh, so one pickling line making ~100 m³/day supplies ~1 GWh of electrolyte per year.
NAME: Pickle-liquor electrolyte refining cascade
MECHANISM: Iron-rich industrial waste brine, mainly spent steel pickle liquor (~120 g/L iron as FeCl2), flows through a cascade of cheap cells held at stepped potentials. At each stage, the charge passed minus the measured conversion shows which couple the stage is working on. Stages that find parasitic metals such as zinc, nickel or copper plate or precipitate them out. The iron leaves as purified flow-battery electrolyte at ~30,000 m³ per GWh, so one pickling line (~100 m³/day) yields ~1 GWh of storage electrolyte a year. Liquor-disposal fees pay for the cascade, dilute mine drainage (~0.2 g/L, 600x leaner) is limited to remediation work, and new stack area is added only where measured charge return justifies it.
VALUE: Steelmakers turn a hazardous waste they pay to dispose of into ~1 GWh/yr of purified iron electrolyte per pickling line, and nearby grids get storage electrolyte whose purification is paid for by waste treatment.

=== CHECKED D06 ===
ANCHOR: Cavern brine/organic store stratified by charge, feeding membraneless trays
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: During discharge both tray layers arrive fully charged, so the two charged species meet at the liquid–liquid interface. If electron transfer there is fast (as for ferrocene/ferricyanide), the leak is limited only by diffusion, at ≈2F·c·√(D/πt) ≈ 260 A/m² over a 3-min stay (c = 1 M, D = 1e-9 m²/s). That is several times the ≤50 A/m² that the ohmic drop of the 1 mm organic layer (σ ≈ 0.4 S/m) allows. The controlling length is a spent buffer s at the interface compared with √(D·t) ≈ 0.35 mm, since leak falls as exp(−s²/4Dt). A 1.5 mm spent-brine lamina with 2-min residence cuts the leak to ~0.0007 mol/m² per pass, ~1.3% of the 0.05 mol/m² moved at 40 A/m².
NAME: Cavern-fed buffered biphasic trays
MECHANISM: A salt cavern stores a dense brine carrying one cheap redox couple beneath an immiscible organic liquid carrying the other. It is stratified by state of charge, so metres of spent liquid at the interface hold the bulk leak to ~0.04 A/m² (5 m buffer, 1 M). At the surface the liquids run through stacked shallow trays between plain carbon plates. The brine enters as two side-by-side flowing laminae: charged brine against the bottom plate, and a ≥1.5 mm lamina of spent brine from the cavern interface zone against the 1 mm organic layer. With 2-min residence, charged brine molecules cannot cross the spent lamina (√(Dt) ≈ 0.35 mm), so interfacial leak is ~1% of the current at 40 A/m². The highly conductive brine buffer adds only ~3 mV, while the organic layer costs ~0.1 V.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-week storage at the gigawatt-hour scale with no membrane or tank, where a spent-liquid buffer in both the cavern and each tray keeps self-discharge near one percent.

=== CHECKED D07 ===
ANCHOR: Solar-pond heated electrolytes separated by a salt step
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: At ~70 °C (D ≈ 2e-9 m²/s, σ ≈ 30 S/m), a 0.1 m step leaks 2F·D·c/L ≈ 3.9 A/m² at 1 M. Running at ~38 A/m² therefore balances ~10% leak against ~10% ohmic loss, with ~0.7 m layers for a three-week discharge, so the electrochemistry passes. But a pond-wide mesh must carry that current sideways: the drop is ≈ i·R_s·W²/8 ≈ 475 V across a 100 m pond with a 0.01 Ω/sq graphite mesh, against ~0.03 V allowed. Pond width, not depth or step thickness, controls the limit, and W must be ≤ ~0.8 m.
NAME: Honeycomb solar-pond electrochemical cells
MECHANISM: A lined solar pond keeps a metre-thick salt gradient on top as a heat trap, so the bottom zone runs at ~70 °C. Inside it, a dense 1 M charged electrolyte ~0.7 m deep lies beneath a lighter one, separated by a 0.1 m salt step with carbon meshes just above and below it, and convection in each warm layer carries reactants to the meshes. Thin plastic honeycomb walls through the full depth divide the pond into ~0.8 m cells, and each cell's upper mesh is wired in series to the next cell's lower mesh. Each mesh then carries only ~24 A and loses ~30 mV, and the walls block shunt currents. At ~38 A/m² this gives ~10% leak, ~10% ohmic loss, ~0.5% of charge lost per idle day, and ~23 kWh and ~45 W per m² of pond. Heat raises leak and conductivity about equally, so its real gain is faster electrode kinetics.
VALUE: Grid operators get three-week electrochemical storage built from a salt pond, carbon mesh and a plastic honeycomb, with free solar heat speeding the electrodes and no tanks, membranes or pumps.
