Z01
MECHANISM: A water-saturated bed of waste lignin is charged in place through sparse buried carbon electrodes, against a cheap counter-electrode layer laid over the bed. Kept mildly alkaline, the bed dissolves its own small lignin fragments, which carry charge between the electrodes and the solid lignin, so the bed mediates itself and its insulating bulk becomes active with no added chemical. Occasional heavy-water charges tag the cycling quinones with deuterium, NMR on cores measures how far this native shuttle really carries charge, and electrode spacing is set to that measured reach, with electrodes added to dead zones and removed from saturated ones.
VALUE: Pulp and biorefinery sites turn a nearly free byproduct into storage that lasts for weeks, in which most of the bed's mass stores charge and electrodes sit only as close together as the bed's own measured shuttle range requires.

Z02
MECHANISM: The long-duration tier's converter is rated at about 1/250 of its stored energy per hour. That is the average deficit of the design lull rather than the hourly peak, so the converter alone covers a 1-2 week lull. Each forecast cycle, ensemble forecasts reset one flat rate: slow charging through weeks of surplus, then flat discharge sized to the high-percentile forecast deficit over the lull's length. Daily batteries absorb hour-to-hour swings and pre-fill only for the hours just before the lull starts. Total charge and discharge throughput is about 17 times the stored energy per year, so the converter runs about half of all hours at around C/250, where catalyst-free electrodes and slow, cheap chemistries stay efficient.
VALUE: Grid planners get a multi-week tier whose converter costs only about ($300/kW)/250 h ≈ $1/kWh and stays busy half the year, removing both the idle-converter cost and most of the rate-driven round-trip loss.

Z03
MECHANISM: A retired steel or cast-iron gas main is relined with a porous separator sleeve. Its wall becomes the iron-plating negative electrode and its own current collector, and a carbon-fibre cable down the bore is the positive. A cheap complexing agent holds the iron-salt electrolyte near neutral pH, so the plated iron neither gives off hydrogen nor eats away the wall during weeks at rest. Each segment connects at the street transformer it passes through an inverter that also does daily peak-shaving and voltage support for that feeder, so the converter earns its keep every day, while night-time open-circuit checks valve out segments that leak or drift.
VALUE: Distribution utilities get weeks of storage at every street transformer with no new land, tank or stack, using an inverter that its everyday work on the feeder already pays for.

Z04
MECHANISM: Excavated iron-rich smectite (layer iron switching between Fe(III) and Fe(II), about 1 mmol e⁻/g) and crushed manganese-oxide ore are laid as 3 cm layers, each mixed with about 10 vol% cheap char so every platelet is within about 2 mm of carbon. A dissolved mediator in salty pore water ferries electrons over that last distance. The layers are stacked as a bipolar earth pile: Fe-clay, a 5 mm compacted bentonite liner, MnO2 ore, then a graphite-foil sheet into the next cell. Each cell stores about 0.7 kWh per m² of sheet with about 0.07 V of ohmic loss at two-week discharge. The only fabricated parts are the about $5/m² sheets (about $7/kWh) and an inverter; everything else is earth at tens of dollars per tonne.
VALUE: Utilities and landowners get a weeks-long store built with earthmoving and layering equipment from local clay and ore, where the electrode itself is the converter, so no stack capex sits idle.

Z05
MECHANISM: The pond's positive electrode is a 6 cm bed of 3-5 mm coke grains on a carbon-felt floor. A charged redox molecule binds a polyelectrolyte and condenses as a dense phase on the coke, always within about 3 mm of carbon. A cheap metal plates onto a floating carbon mat a few centimetres above, and gravity plus the phase boundary replace the separator. PV modules on part of the mat feed DC straight into series-wired cell strings. Z06
MECHANISM: Charged redox molecules are dissolved in a water-free trehalose-based eutectic that is liquid just above room temperature at the stack and is quenched into a glass for storage, so no water ever has to be evaporated. In the glass even highly reactive, high-voltage, fast-kinetics molecules such as organic radicals barely move, so a small plain carbon stack can run at low overpotential with chemistry chosen for speed, not shelf life. Granules are remelted only as discharge needs them, with a counterflow exchanger passing heat from the outgoing melt to the incoming glass and the stack's own loss heat covering the rest.
VALUE: Grid operators get weeks of charge stored like grain in silos, feeding a small, fast and efficient stack, without spending stored energy on drying or redissolving.

Z07

Z08

Z09
MECHANISM: Surplus power drives a reversible hydraulic pump-motor that presses water out of the interlayers of thin swelling-clay beds, so energy sits as unmet hydration energy in dry clay rather than as stress, with no pressure vessel. Z11
MECHANISM: A paper machine lays carbon-paper electrodes on both faces of a ~30 µm regenerated-cellulose film in one pass. The laminate is wound with mesh spacers into short spiral rolls stacked like coins in a mass-produced pressure tube. Each roll is ~6 cm tall and collected along both end faces, because 0.3 Ω/sq carbon paper at 30 A/m² already loses 20 mV over 6.7 cm. The electrolytes carry fast outer-sphere redox polymers (for example TEMPO and viologen polymers) far above the film's size cutoff, so the film holds them back about a thousandfold while small salt ions pass freely. As a result, crossover is ~0.3 A/m² against a 30 A/m² operating current (~1%) without any ion-exchange membrane or catalyst. Each m² of laminate gives ~30 W, and spent rolls are re-pulped to recover the carbon.
VALUE: Flow-battery builders get a converter that costs about as much as paper and cellophane (~$70/kW if the laminate costs ~$2/m², assumed) and separates the electrolytes as well as a membrane, which removes the stack cost that dominates long-duration storage.

Z15
MECHANISM: A salt cavern stores a dense brine carrying one cheap redox couple beneath an immiscible organic liquid carrying the other. VALUE: Grid operators get multi-week storage at the gigawatt-hour scale with no membrane or tank, where a spent-liquid buffer in both the cavern and each tray keeps self-discharge near one percent.

Z20
MECHANISM: Raw mine or industrial waste brine flows through a cascade of cheap cells, each held at a stepped electrode potential. The charge each stage passes, minus the conversion measured there, tells it which couple it is working on. Stages whose residual shows a parasitic couple precipitate it out. When it is not storing power, the cascade keeps treating brine for remediation fees, so stack area earns money year-round. New stack area is added only where measured charge return justifies it.
VALUE: Mine operators and nearby grids gain storage electrolyte that is effectively free, made inside a water-treatment plant whose remediation revenue carries the converter capex.

A complexing agent keeps the iron-salt electrolyte near neutral pH, so the roughly 0.1 mm of iron plated per full charge neither gives off hydrogen nor eats the wall. Each metre stores about 0.4 kWh, about 120 kWh per 300 m segment, through the transformer's inverter, which earns its keep daily with peak-shaving (about 30 kW for 4 h) and voltage support. In a calm, dark spell the segment can carry only critical feeder loads (about 1.5 kW for three days: streetlights, telecom, pumps), and night-time open-circuit checks valve out segments that leak or drift.
VALUE: Distribution utilities get daily storage plus a multi-day critical-load reserve at every street transformer with no new land or tanks, paid for by everyday feeder work, but not weeks of supply for a whole feeder.

MECHANISM: A trench of biochar soaked in neutral brine is both store and converter. Impedance tomography maps pockets that will not charge, and these are dried into insulating walls that split the bed into series cells at inverter voltage. The char's own pyrolysis liquor (about 0.1 kg of phenolics per kg of char) is oxidised and split: catechol-type quinones are fixed in the positive half of each cell and anthraquinone-type ones in the negative half. That gives about 0.5 V of redox voltage and about 8 Wh/kg of char, eight times bare double-layer storage. Discharge over days keeps current density tiny, so even a crude bed has small resistive loss.
VALUE: Farms and grid operators get weeks of storage at about $12-25/kWh, dug with earthmoving equipment, with an inverter as the only factory-made part.

MECHANISM: A lined pit holds one membraneless electrolyte with both reactants, and plain dipped electrodes each react with only one of them, so the electrode array is the whole converter. The reactant pair is chosen so the charged oxidized form is dense and sinks while the charged reduced form is light and floats, with the charging electrodes placed at the bottom and at the top. The pit stratifies on its own and the two charged forms meet only across a thin mixed layer in the middle, which stretches holding time from days to weeks. Charging is still timed by the weather forecast so that the remaining self-discharge costs only a few percent, and the electrodes are sized to average lull demand.
VALUE: Grid operators can ride through lulls longer than any forecast horizon with a hole in the ground, liquid and dipped electrodes, and nothing else.

MECHANISM: The road's base course is built as sealed slabs wrapped in geomembrane. Each slab holds alternating layers of iron slag and manganese slag, about 7 cm each, separated by sand, with steel-mesh collectors and 10-20% coke breeze for electronic conduction, and is flooded with 6 M KOH. About 7 cm is the thickest layer that keeps ohmic loss under 0.1 V at 10% utilisation over 3 days. Each slab is one ~1 V cell, and about a thousand slabs in series reach the kilovolt input of one inverter per substation. A storage course 30 m wide and 1 m deep holds about 0.5 GWh per km. KOH (about $5/kWh) is the largest cost after the road-funded aggregate, and load-bearing monitoring sets how deep each segment is cycled.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-day storage at about $10/kWh along new road corridors, where the road budget pays for the bulk mass, the housing and the land.

MECHANISM: A bank of static thick-electrode cells sits on a wind or solar plant's DC link, so the plant's own inverter, transformer and grid line, which sit idle in a calm, dark spell, are its only power electronics. Contactors switch how many cells are in series, as a transformer tap changer does, so the bank follows the voltage the plant's power tracking asks for instead of clamping it, and the taps change rarely because state of charge drifts over days. Sized for weeks at a few percent of plant rating, the cells run at very low current density with small losses and charge only from output that would otherwise be curtailed.
VALUE: Plant owners add week-long storage that costs little more than cell material and contactors, because the conversion hardware, the grid connection and the charging energy are already paid for or would otherwise be wasted.

MECHANISM: A lined solar pond keeps a standard metre-thick salt gradient near its top as an insulating heat trap, so the whole bottom zone warms by tens of degrees. Inside that hot zone, a dense charged electrolyte lies beneath a lighter one, separated only by a thin salt step about a decimetre thick: enough to stop mixing for weeks, yet short enough to keep the ion-path resistance low, with pond-wide carbon meshes just above and below it. Both electrolytes therefore run hot, which raises conductivity and electrode kinetics on both sides, and capacity is set by pond area and by a layer depth chosen so that the pond's power matches a discharge lasting several weeks.
VALUE: Grid operators get weeks of electrochemical storage with no tanks, membranes or stacks, where the pond's own salt layering both separates the electrolytes and supplies the free heat that keeps round-trip losses down.

Z43
MECHANISM: A lined solar pond keeps a metre-thick salt gradient on top as a heat trap, so the bottom zone runs at ~70 °C. Inside it, a dense 1 M charged electrolyte ~0.7 m deep lies beneath a lighter one, separated by a 0.1 m salt step with carbon meshes just above and below it, and convection in each warm layer carries reactants to the meshes. Thin plastic honeycomb walls through the full depth divide the pond into ~0.8 m cells, and each cell's upper mesh is wired in series to the next cell's lower mesh. Each mesh then carries only ~24 A and loses ~30 mV, and the walls block shunt currents. At ~38 A/m² this gives ~10% leak, ~10% ohmic loss, ~0.5% of charge lost per idle day, and ~23 kWh and ~45 W per m² of pond. Heat raises leak and conductivity about equally, so its real gain is faster electrode kinetics.
VALUE: Grid operators get three-week electrochemical storage built from a salt pond, carbon mesh and a plastic honeycomb, with free solar heat speeding the electrodes and no tanks, membranes or pumps.

Z44
MECHANISM: Two buried magnetite lenses with barren rock between them become the two electrodes. A borehole ring injects a carbonate-buffered alkaline brine so that both charged states stay in place as insoluble solids: magnetite oxidizes to maghemite/hematite on one side and reduces to ferrous hydroxide/green rust on the other. Nothing dissolves, migrates or becomes acid drainage. The same perimeter boreholes pump a slight inward hydraulic gradient, which keeps oxygen-bearing outside water from discharging the store. Self-potential mapping shows where the charged front lags, and current is moved between boreholes to keep the front even. Because each lens spreads the reaction over millions of square metres of mineral surface, current density stays tiny and voltage losses stay small. The only fabricated converter is the inverter.
VALUE: Owners of magnetite deposits and flooded iron-mine voids gain a reversible, non-polluting seasonal store whose cost per kWh is drilling plus an inverter, aimed at utilities that need to cover multi-week lulls.

Z45
MECHANISM: A neat strained liquid of the norbornadiene-quadricyclane class (about 90 kJ/mol, about 0.27 kWh of heat per kg) is charged electrochemically through a radical-anion channel where the strained form is the downhill product, and it sits inert in plain steel tanks. To discharge, it flows over a carbon-felt anode that injects a few holes, and each relaxed cation oxidizes the next strained molecule in a chain of thousands of turnovers per electron. The felt thus acts as a switchable catalyst that releases the strain as heat at about 150-200°C into an organic Rankine cycle at about 20-25%, about 60 Wh of electricity per kg. Round-trip is only about 15-20%, so this pays only if the liquid costs under about $0.5/kg (about $8/kWh) and the charging power is nearly free.
VALUE: Grid operators get a pumpable, inert liquid whose extra days cost only tank volume, returned as dispatchable heat-to-power. It suits absorbing nearly free surplus, not efficient arbitrage.

Z46
MECHANISM: A lined pit holds stacked brine-soaked sand layers between cheap bipolar carbon sheets, and each layer has three zones: carbon-coated conductive sand against each sheet, where the soluble charged species form throughout the zone, and a plain-sand barrier zone between them. The species are therefore made where they are stored instead of having to diffuse in, while crossover must still cross the barrier, whose diffusion time grows with its thickness squared but whose resistance grows only linearly. A decimetre barrier holds charge for months, and the conductive sand grains give enough surface for days-long discharge at a few amps per square metre, with series stacking raising the voltage.
VALUE: Grid operators get membrane-free storage for weeks in which the whole layer holds charge and both energy and power sides are earthworks, sand, brine and carbon sheet.
