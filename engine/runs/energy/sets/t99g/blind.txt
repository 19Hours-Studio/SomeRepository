Y01
MECHANISM: The road's base course is built as sealed slabs wrapped in geomembrane. Each slab holds alternating layers of iron slag and manganese slag, about 7 cm each, separated by sand, with steel-mesh collectors and 10-20% coke breeze for electronic conduction, and is flooded with 6 M KOH. About 7 cm is the thickest layer that keeps ohmic loss under 0.1 V at 10% utilisation over 3 days. Each slab is one ~1 V cell, and about a thousand slabs in series reach the kilovolt input of one inverter per substation. A storage course 30 m wide and 1 m deep holds about 0.5 GWh per km. KOH (about $5/kWh) is the largest cost after the road-funded aggregate, and load-bearing monitoring sets how deep each segment is cycled.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-day storage at about $10/kWh along new road corridors, where the road budget pays for the bulk mass, the housing and the land.

Y02
MECHANISM: A retired steel or cast-iron gas main is relined with a porous separator sleeve. Its wall is the iron-plating negative electrode and current collector, and a carbon-fibre cable down the bore is the positive. A complexing agent keeps the iron-salt electrolyte near neutral pH, so the roughly 0.1 mm of iron plated per full charge neither gives off hydrogen nor eats the wall. Each metre stores about 0.4 kWh, about 120 kWh per 300 m segment, through the transformer's inverter, which earns its keep daily with peak-shaving (about 30 kW for 4 h) and voltage support. In a calm, dark spell the segment can carry only critical feeder loads (about 1.5 kW for three days: streetlights, telecom, pumps), and night-time open-circuit checks valve out segments that leak or drift.
VALUE: Distribution utilities get daily storage plus a multi-day critical-load reserve at every street transformer with no new land or tanks, paid for by everyday feeder work, but not weeks of supply for a whole feeder.

Y03
MECHANISM: Lignin-derived quinone electrolytes in 4 M NaCl flow on both sides of a 0.2-mm layer of 1-µm silica flour on a coarse-sand backing, with no membrane. Leak through a porous bed is set by grain size, not thickness. At a permeability of about 1e-15 m², a 10 Pa imbalance between the sides leaks under 1% of the current, and mirrored low-pressure-drop flow-by channels keep the imbalance that small; diffusion adds about 1.7%. The thin layer carries about 280 A/m² at 2% resistive loss (about 170 W/m²), and capacity scales with tank volume.
VALUE: Grid operators get flow storage with no ion-exchange membrane: cents of ground quartz per m² replace the costliest stack part, and crossover stays at a few percent.

Y04
MECHANISM: A lined solar pond keeps a metre-thick salt gradient on top as a heat trap, so the bottom zone runs at ~70 °C. Inside it, a dense 1 M charged electrolyte ~0.7 m deep lies beneath a lighter one, separated by a 0.1 m salt step with carbon meshes just above and below it, and convection in each warm layer carries reactants to the meshes. Thin plastic honeycomb walls through the full depth divide the pond into ~0.8 m cells, and each cell's upper mesh is wired in series to the next cell's lower mesh. Each mesh then carries only ~24 A and loses ~30 mV, and the walls block shunt currents. At ~38 A/m² this gives ~10% leak, ~10% ohmic loss, ~0.5% of charge lost per idle day, and ~23 kWh and ~45 W per m² of pond. Heat raises leak and conductivity about equally, so its real gain is faster electrode kinetics.
VALUE: Grid operators get three-week electrochemical storage built from a salt pond, carbon mesh and a plastic honeycomb, with free solar heat speeding the electrodes and no tanks, membranes or pumps.

Y05
MECHANISM: A shallow lined pond about 0.4 m deep holds one membraneless electrolyte with 0.3 M of the reactant pair in a strong supporting salt, with a carbon mesh on the floor and one at the surface. Charging makes a dense oxidized form that pools on the floor and a light reduced form that floats. Discharged fluid rises or sinks by its own buoyancy into a permanent 0.25 m spent middle layer that keeps the two apart. Because ohmic drop grows with depth squared, the discharge time fixes the depth: at 3 A/m² for one week, ohmic loss is ~5%, leak ~8% and idle loss ~1%/day, so charging is still timed by the weather forecast. The pond is split into ~3 m cells wired in series so the sideways drop in each mesh stays near 30 mV, giving ~0.6 kWh and ~3.6 W per m² of pond.
VALUE: Grid operators can cover week-long lulls with a shallow lined pond holding one liquid and two meshes per cell, with no membranes, pumps or tanks.

Y06
MECHANISM: An alkaline iron anode slab (about 1.5 cm) and a coke-filled MnO₂ cathode slab (about 3 cm) sit in KOH (12 S/m effective in the pores) across one separator. Ohmic drop grows as L²/t, so a 250-hour discharge lets the cathode be 3 cm thick with only about 0.09 V of drop at about 74 A/m². Each m² of separator and collector then stores about 18 kWh, using roughly 1 kg Fe and 3 kg MnO₂ per kWh at about 1 V.
VALUE: Grids riding through calm, dark spells get ten-day storage with about 50x less collector and separator per kWh and materials near $8-15/kWh.

Y07
MECHANISM: Alkaline iron anode slabs and MnO₂ cathode slabs, each about 3 cm thick and filled with coke, sit in 12 S/m KOH across a single separator. The slab thickness is chosen from the 250-hour discharge time, since the ion-conduction length grows with the square root of time. The result stores about 1 V per electron pair, using roughly 1 kg Fe and 3 kg MnO₂ per kWh.
VALUE: About 50× less collector and separator per kWh, with materials near $8/kWh, makes ten-day storage cheap.

Y08
MECHANISM: A trench of biochar soaked in neutral brine is both store and converter. Impedance tomography maps pockets that will not charge, and these are dried into insulating walls that split the bed into series cells at inverter voltage. The char's own pyrolysis liquor (about 0.1 kg of phenolics per kg of char) is oxidised and split: catechol-type quinones are fixed in the positive half of each cell and anthraquinone-type ones in the negative half. That gives about 0.5 V of redox voltage and about 8 Wh/kg of char, eight times bare double-layer storage. Discharge over days keeps current density tiny, so even a crude bed has small resistive loss.
VALUE: Farms and grid operators get weeks of storage at about $12-25/kWh, dug with earthmoving equipment, with an inverter as the only factory-made part.

Y09
MECHANISM: In a closed coal-ash pond, only the top ~5 cm of ash under the closure cap works as a thin iron-air cell. A steel rod grid laid just below the cap reduces the ash's own iron oxide in its lime-alkaline pore water, and the cap's drainage gravel carries a steel mesh as the air electrode. Ions can only travel about 3 cm through the pore water at 10% utilisation over a week, so the store holds about 0.2 kWh per m² of cap: about 100 MWh at about 0.6 MW for a 50 ha pond. It is metered zone by zone and runs through the retired plant's switchyard, at $25/kWh or more in steel alone and well under 50% efficiency.
VALUE: Retired coal plants get a modest week-long pilot store of about 100 MWh from a closure cap they must build anyway, not a multi-GWh grid resource.

Y10
MECHANISM: The pond's positive electrode is a 6 cm bed of 3-5 mm coke grains on a carbon-felt floor. A charged redox molecule binds a polyelectrolyte and condenses as a dense phase on the coke, always within about 3 mm of carbon. A cheap metal plates onto a floating carbon mat a few centimetres above, and gravity plus the phase boundary replace the separator. PV modules on part of the mat feed DC straight into series-wired cell strings. The mat is about five times the panel area because a 6 cm bed holds about 1.9 kWh/m² at under 0.1 V ohmic loss over a 10-day discharge (about 7 A/m²). Coke adds about $4/kWh, and at about 8 kg of molecule per kWh the molecule must cost $2/kg or less to keep the total near $20/kWh.
VALUE: Floating-solar operators gain 10-day storage in the pond under and around their arrays, with no charging inverter and a cell structure costing a few dollars per kWh.

Y11
MECHANISM: Excavated iron-rich smectite (layer iron switching between Fe(III) and Fe(II), about 1 mmol e⁻/g) and crushed manganese-oxide ore are laid as 3 cm layers, each mixed with about 10 vol% cheap char so every platelet is within about 2 mm of carbon. A dissolved mediator in salty pore water ferries electrons over that last distance. The layers are stacked as a bipolar earth pile: Fe-clay, a 5 mm compacted bentonite liner, MnO2 ore, then a graphite-foil sheet into the next cell. Each cell stores about 0.7 kWh per m² of sheet with about 0.07 V of ohmic loss at two-week discharge. The only fabricated parts are the about $5/m² sheets (about $7/kWh) and an inverter; everything else is earth at tens of dollars per tonne.
VALUE: Utilities and landowners get a weeks-long store built with earthmoving and layering equipment from local clay and ore, where the electrode itself is the converter, so no stack capex sits idle.

Y12
MECHANISM: A bank of LFP-graphite cells sits on a wind or solar plant's DC link. Their electrodes are about 4 mm thick, the most that L ≈ √(D_eff·t) allows for a multi-day discharge. Contactors change how many cells are in series so the bank follows the plant's power-tracking voltage. In calm, dark spells the bank uses the plant's idle inverter and grid connection, and it charges only from output that would otherwise be curtailed. Collectors and separators shrink about 50x per kWh, so the installed cost is about $20/kWh, mostly active material. The bank is sized at about 20-30% of plant rating for a week (about 40-50 rated-hours) so it can actually carry the plant's average output through a lull.
VALUE: Plant owners add week-long storage at about $20/kWh with no new power electronics or grid connection, charged from power they would otherwise curtail.

Y13
MECHANISM: Thin swelling-clay beds sit fixed in earth-covered, vacuum-tight enclosures that only have to resist atmospheric pressure (0.1 MPa instead of about 260 MPa). Energy is stored as unmet hydration energy at about 7 Wh/kg, about 150 kg of clay per kWh, roughly $7/kWh at $50/t. To charge, one shared vapour compressor draws water vapour off one bed at a time at about 0.5 kPa (RH about 0.15) and condenses it into a pond. To discharge, pond water evaporates and expands through a low-pressure vapour turbine into the dry clay, valved from bed to bed. The adsorption heat (about 10x the work) must flow back to the pond across 3 K or less, so beds and pond are interleaved through thin walls. The machine handles about 1 m³/s of vapour per kW, so it is costly per kW and pays only at multi-week durations.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-week storage whose capacity costs about as much as clay earthworks and a vacuum liner, with one vapour machine, sized only for power, as the converter.

Y14
MECHANISM: A neat strained liquid of the norbornadiene-quadricyclane class (about 90 kJ/mol, about 0.27 kWh of heat per kg) is charged electrochemically through a radical-anion channel where the strained form is the downhill product, and it sits inert in plain steel tanks. To discharge, it flows over a carbon-felt anode that injects a few holes, and each relaxed cation oxidizes the next strained molecule in a chain of thousands of turnovers per electron. The felt thus acts as a switchable catalyst that releases the strain as heat at about 150-200°C into an organic Rankine cycle at about 20-25%, about 60 Wh of electricity per kg. Round-trip is only about 15-20%, so this pays only if the liquid costs under about $0.5/kg (about $8/kWh) and the charging power is nearly free.
VALUE: Grid operators get a pumpable, inert liquid whose extra days cost only tank volume, returned as dispatchable heat-to-power. It suits absorbing nearly free surplus, not efficient arbitrage.

Y15
MECHANISM: Lignin-derived quinone macromolecules (D ≈ 1e-13) in 4 M NaCl flow on both sides of a centimetre-wide bed of packed sand, with no membrane. Crossover fraction times ohmic fraction is fixed at about 5e-7, so 0.5% resistive loss comes with only 0.01% crossover. Capacity scales with tank volume.
VALUE: It removes the costliest flow-battery part, the ion-exchange membrane, while keeping crossover negligible.

Y16
MECHANISM: Charged ferric chloride, a 1.5-mm layer of spent ferrous chloride and an iron-plating wall share one membraneless 3-mm channel tilted only about 0.25°. The liquids creep at about 2 cm/s (Reynolds number about 40) instead of falling turbulently. The layers are stacked by density: the salted, densest ferric layer sits on a carbon floor and the plating wall forms the ceiling, where hydrogen vents. Fe³⁺ spreads only about 0.17 mm in each 50-s pass, a splitter at the outlet returns each layer to its tank, and at about 150 A/m² the cell gives about 150 W/m².
VALUE: Utilities that need multi-day backup get membrane-free flow storage whose only active medium is iron salt.

Y17
MECHANISM: Bands cut from natural-rubber-rich scrap truck tyres, screened for strain fixity at room temperature, are pulled in bundles of about 100 by a shared motor-generator winch to about 5x their length while warm. They are spray-cooled in the grip for about 30 s (a 3 mm band cools in about L²/α ≈ 20 s), so strain-induced crystallites lock the stretch. The now force-free bundles are baled densely (about 600 kg/m³) in shaded, covered yards at about $10/m³, about $5/kWh at about 3 Wh/kg. To discharge, a bundle is gripped again and warmed with about 45°C waste heat at a rate set by the grid frequency signal; the crystallites melt and the bundle contracts against the generator, drawing part of its work from that heat. Each stroke of about 1.5 kWh on a 30 s cycle gives about 180 kW per winch head, and about 0.3 t of rubber is handled per kWh.
VALUE: Industrial sites with waste heat get leak-free, non-hazardous multi-week storage whose medium comes with a disposal fee and whose housing is a covered bale yard instead of steel racks.

Y18
MECHANISM: The charged Fe³⁺ stream and the charged Cr²⁺ stream run side by side down a cheap plastic channel. A 1.5 mm sheath of their own discharged mixed electrolyte separates them, and they stay in contact for about two minutes. Diffusive leak through the sheath falls as exp(−s²/4Dt), to about 1%, while ions conduct across it with only about 10 mV of loss.
VALUE: An iron-chromium flow battery with no membrane and chemicals near $10/kWh.

Y19
MECHANISM: The long-duration tier's converter is rated at about 1/250 of its stored energy per hour. That is the average deficit of the design lull rather than the hourly peak, so the converter alone covers a 1-2 week lull. Each forecast cycle, ensemble forecasts reset one flat rate: slow charging through weeks of surplus, then flat discharge sized to the high-percentile forecast deficit over the lull's length. Daily batteries absorb hour-to-hour swings and pre-fill only for the hours just before the lull starts. Total charge and discharge throughput is about 17 times the stored energy per year, so the converter runs about half of all hours at around C/250, where catalyst-free electrodes and slow, cheap chemistries stay efficient.
VALUE: Grid planners get a multi-week tier whose converter costs only about ($300/kW)/250 h ≈ $1/kWh and stays busy half the year, removing both the idle-converter cost and most of the rate-driven round-trip loss.

Y20
MECHANISM: Iron-rich industrial waste brine, mainly spent steel pickle liquor (~120 g/L iron as FeCl2), flows through a cascade of cheap cells held at stepped potentials. At each stage, the charge passed minus the measured conversion shows which couple the stage is working on. Stages that find parasitic metals such as zinc, nickel or copper plate or precipitate them out. The iron leaves as purified flow-battery electrolyte at ~30,000 m³ per GWh, so one pickling line (~100 m³/day) yields ~1 GWh of storage electrolyte a year. Liquor-disposal fees pay for the cascade, dilute mine drainage (~0.2 g/L, 600x leaner) is limited to remediation work, and new stack area is added only where measured charge return justifies it.
VALUE: Steelmakers turn a hazardous waste they pay to dispose of into ~1 GWh/yr of purified iron electrolyte per pickling line, and nearby grids get storage electrolyte whose purification is paid for by waste treatment.

Y21
MECHANISM: An iron-ore heap and a manganese-ore heap, threaded with steel mesh and separated by sand, form a sealed cell that a shared alkaline reservoir floods only to charge or discharge. The first slow charge turns the hematite into a conductive sponge-iron network, so the whole heap works and not just the ore touching the mesh. Draining leaves the pores inside each grain wet, so self-discharge is suppressed chemically instead: a few percent sulfide ore plus about 1 wt% bismuth sulfide holds hydrogen evolution to about 0.1-0.2%/day (3-5% over 4 weeks). Draining still stops shunt currents and frees the electrolyte for other heaps. Hectare-wide beds run at very low current density, so each heap is its own converter.
VALUE: Grid operators get weeks of storage built from mined ore and shared electrolyte that keeps about 95% of its charge through a month-long wait.

Y22
MECHANISM: Two parallel carbon-lined ditches, separated by a porous divider about 2.5 mm thick, each carry one dissolved organic molecule. Its charged form is a strained isomer that crystallises only after a delay, so it nucleates on seed crystals in that side's own downstream settling basin rather than on the electrode. Each side stays saturated at about 20 mM, which keeps the voltage flat and holds crossover to about 1.5% of a 10 A/m² current at 0.05 V ohmic loss. A potential-gated catalyst on each floor speeds snap-back only at discharge potential, and discharge redissolves slurry fed back upstream. The two powders sit on separate covered heaps for months. The converter is two lined ditches at about $30/m², or about $3/W at 10 W/m² (about $15/kWh over a 200 h discharge).
VALUE: Grid operators get months-long storage whose energy sits in heaps of cheap dry powder, provided molecules with about 20 mM charged-state solubility and delayed crystallisation can be found.

Y23
MECHANISM: Dilute iron and chromium chlorides (0.2 M) ride in concentrated sodium chloride brine, which carries most of the current. Because the salt is much more mobile than the actives, a plain sand layer keeps crossover and resistance both near 2%. The same mixed electrolyte is stored in lined ponds on both sides.
VALUE: Flow storage at about $12/kWh using only commodity salt, iron, chromium and sand.

Y24
MECHANISM: Iron-oxide paste and manganese-ore paste, each made conductive with coke, are cast in centimetre-thick layers with a KOH gel between them, in cheap trays. A 100-hour discharge lets ions cross 1.5 cm of electrode, so foil, separator and housing per kWh drop about 50x. Bi₂S₃ added to the iron layer suppresses hydrogen evolution during weeks of holding.
VALUE: Batteries stamped out like concrete paving, at under $10/kWh, sized for week-long lulls.

Y25
MECHANISM: A lined pit holds stacked layers between bipolar carbon sheets. Each layer has ~0.15 m of carbon-coated conductive sand against each sheet, where the soluble charged species form and stay, and a ~0.13 m plain-sand barrier in between. The brine carries only 0.2 M active species in ~4 M NaCl, so conductivity stays ≈5 S/m in the sand while the force driving crossover drops five-fold. That gives ~0.06 A/m² of leak against a 1.7 A/m² one-week discharge, about 0.5% of stored charge per day, with ~8% ohmic loss each way. Each 0.43 m layer stores ~0.34 kWh per m² of sheet (~0.8 kWh per m³ of pit), and ~20 layers in a 10 m pit make a ~25 V series stack.
VALUE: Grid operators get membrane-free storage that holds charge through multi-week lulls (~15% loss per month), with both the energy and power parts built from earthworks, sand, salt brine and carbon sheet.

Y26
MECHANISM: A salt cavern stores a dense brine carrying one cheap redox couple beneath an immiscible organic liquid carrying the other. It is stratified by state of charge, so metres of spent liquid at the interface hold the bulk leak to ~0.04 A/m² (5 m buffer, 1 M). At the surface the liquids run through stacked shallow trays between plain carbon plates. The brine enters as two side-by-side flowing laminae: charged brine against the bottom plate, and a ≥1.5 mm lamina of spent brine from the cavern interface zone against the 1 mm organic layer. With 2-min residence, charged brine molecules cannot cross the spent lamina (√(Dt) ≈ 0.35 mm), so interfacial leak is ~1% of the current at 40 A/m². The highly conductive brine buffer adds only ~3 mV, while the organic layer costs ~0.1 V.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-week storage at the gigawatt-hour scale with no membrane or tank, where a spent-liquid buffer in both the cavern and each tray keeps self-discharge near one percent.

Y27
MECHANISM: A paper machine lays carbon-paper electrodes on both faces of a ~30 µm regenerated-cellulose film in one pass. The laminate is wound with mesh spacers into short spiral rolls stacked like coins in a mass-produced pressure tube. Each roll is ~6 cm tall and collected along both end faces, because 0.3 Ω/sq carbon paper at 30 A/m² already loses 20 mV over 6.7 cm. The electrolytes carry fast outer-sphere redox polymers (for example TEMPO and viologen polymers) far above the film's size cutoff, so the film holds them back about a thousandfold while small salt ions pass freely. As a result, crossover is ~0.3 A/m² against a 30 A/m² operating current (~1%) without any ion-exchange membrane or catalyst. Each m² of laminate gives ~30 W, and spent rolls are re-pulped to recover the carbon.
VALUE: Flow-battery builders get a converter that costs about as much as paper and cellophane (~$70/kW if the laminate costs ~$2/m², assumed) and separates the electrolytes as well as a membrane, which removes the stack cost that dominates long-duration storage.

Y28
MECHANISM: A water-saturated bed of waste lignin is held at pH about 11, so it keeps about 0.1 M of its own quinone-bearing fragments dissolved. This pore water is slowly flushed (about 3 m/day) in a loop between the bed and a compact carbon stack with a cheap counter electrode. The fragments are charged in the stack and hand their charge to the solid lignin as they percolate, so the insulating bulk becomes active with no added chemical and no electrodes inside the bed. At about 1 mmol e⁻/g and about 0.7 V the bed holds about 9 kWh/m³, and flow paths of about 2.5 m sustain a 100-hour discharge. Occasional heavy-water charges tag the cycling quinones, and NMR on cores shows how deep each pass reaches into the grains, which sets the flow rate and bed length.
VALUE: Pulp and biorefinery sites turn a nearly free byproduct into weeks-long storage: the bed is plain earthworks and only a small stack, sized for power, is fabricated.

Y29
MECHANISM: Iron and chromium chlorides at 0.2 M are dissolved in about 4.5 M sodium chloride brine, and the brine carries the current. The silica's permeability of about 1e-15 m² means a 30 Pa pressure mismatch leaks only about 0.4%. The same mixed electrolyte, about 0.37 m³ per kWh, is stored in covered, lined ponds on both sides.
VALUE: Grids needing week-scale reserves get flow storage at about $12-20/kWh, made only from commodity salt, iron, chromium and ground quartz.

Y30
MECHANISM: A membraneless carbon cell array reduces air oxygen to peroxide in soda-ash solution and crystallizes sodium percarbonate. It runs as a merchant plant on cheap surplus power, selling to bleaching, water-treatment and detergent buyers at a high capacity factor. About one month of output, about 25 kt of percarbonate per 100 kt/yr plant (about 3 GWh at 0.13 kWh/kg), is held back in silos. VALUE: Industrial parks hosting peroxide production get a roughly 10 MW, two-week local reserve whose converter is paid for by chemical sales: a resilience asset, not whole-grid storage.

Y31
MECHANISM: Charged ferric chloride, spent ferrous chloride and an iron-plating wall share one membraneless gap that the liquids fall through. Y32

The lens-to-lens ion path is replaced with fine magnetite tailings laid in a flooded mine void as 2 cm beds, separated by 1 cm sand layers and stacked between bipolar graphite sheets. Y33

Y34
MECHANISM: The charged Fe³⁺ stream flows along a carbon floor, a 1.5-mm sheath of discharged mixed electrolyte flows above it, and the charged Cr²⁺ stream runs along a carbon ceiling, where hydrogen vents. All three move through a flat plastic channel about 1.2 m long at about 1 cm/s, giving about 2 minutes of contact. Stacking them in density order, with the bottom stream salted at least 1% denser, stops buoyant overturning. That leaves only a diffusive leak of about e^-7.8 ≈ 0.04%, while ions cross the sheath with about 5 mV of loss at about 100 A/m².
VALUE: Multi-day grid storage from an iron-chromium flow battery with no membrane and chemicals near $10/kWh.

Y35
MECHANISM: Charged posolyte and negolyte radicals are dissolved in a water-free sugar eutectic with Tg about 50°C and quenched into glass granules, which sit in ambient silos with their mobility frozen (about 40 Wh/kg, 25 kg per kWh, so the sugar must cost under about $0.4/kg to keep the medium near $10/kWh). For discharge, granules pass through a counterflow exchanger that recovers at least 90% of the heat. They are melted to about 170°C (about 0.5 Pa·s, about 0.01 S/m) for a residence of only minutes, and the melt flows once through a plain carbon cell with a 30 µm gap at 3 mA/cm², losing about 0.1 V to resistance. That needs about 20 m² of cell per kW, which for a 100-hour store is only about 0.2 m² per kWh. The stack's loss heat (about 15% of output) covers the exchanger's leftover heat demand (about 13%), and the melt is re-quenched as spent glass.
VALUE: Grid operators get weeks of charge stored like grain in silos, with no drying or redissolving, discharged through a cheap, large-area, slow carbon cell sized for days-long output.
