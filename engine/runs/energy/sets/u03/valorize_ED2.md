=== CHECKED D01 ===
ANCHOR: radicals frozen in sugar-glass granules, melted briefly to discharge
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: In a 0.5 Pa·s melt at 443 K, D = kT/(6πηr) ≈ 1.3e-12 m²/s (r = 0.5 nm). With about 2 M of radical in each melt, the diffusion-limited current across a 30 µm film is nFDc/h ≈ 96485×1.3e-12×2000/3e-5 ≈ 8 A/m², about 4x below the 30 A/m² claimed. Without a separator, posolyte and negolyte also mix across the same 30 µm in the same h²/D ≈ 700 s, so crossover is about as large as the useful current. Diffusion is the binding limit, not conductivity (σΔV/h ≈ 33 A/m²). Both limits scale as 1/h, but thinning the film does not help, because the pressure needed to push the melt through it for its h²/D residence grows as Δp ≈ 12ηL²D/h⁴ (≈1 bar at 30 µm and 10 cm, ≈16 bar at 15 µm). The way out is lower current over more cheap area: at ~5 A/m² and 200 h the cell needs ~0.7 m²/kWh, about $4-7/kWh at $5-10/m².
NAME: Melt-on-demand sugar-glass radicals
MECHANISM: Charged posolyte and charged negolyte (as small dimers) are each dissolved at ~2 M in a water-free sugar eutectic (Tg ≈ 50 °C) and quenched into separate glass granules, which sit in ambient silos at ~40 Wh/kg overall (~25 kg/kWh). To discharge, each granule stream passes a counterflow exchanger that recovers ≥90% of the heat and melts at ~170 °C (0.5 Pa·s). It then flows for ~10 min as a 30 µm film along 10 cm of carbon paper. The two films are separated by a thin size-exclusion film that blocks the dimers but lets small ions through. Diffusion in the viscous melt caps current near nFDc/h ≈ 6-8 A/m², so the cell runs at ~5 A/m² (~7 W/m²), which is ~0.7 m² of cheap carbon per kWh for a 200 h discharge; its ~15% loss heat covers the exchanger's leftover heat demand before the melt is re-quenched as spent glass.
VALUE: Grid operators get weeks of charge stored as inert glass granules in grain-type silos, discharged through a slow, large, cheap carbon cell sized for multi-day output.

=== CHECKED D02 ===
ANCHOR: stretch-locked crystallised rubber released by waste heat
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: Stored work is W ≈ (G/2)(λ²+2/λ−3) times fixity. Stretching a filled tyre compound (G ≈ 0.8 MPa) to λ = 5 takes ≈ 9 MJ/m³ ≈ 2.2 Wh/kg. Strain-crystallisation hysteresis (~40% of the loading work lost on retraction) and incomplete fixity of a densely crosslinked network leave ~1 Wh/kg recoverable, about 3x below the 3 Wh/kg claimed. W scales as Gλ², but fixity falls as crosslink density (G) rises, so λ is the only real lever, and tread compounds break near λ ≈ 6. Rubber mass is therefore ~1 t/kWh, baled at 600 kg/m³ into ~1.7 m³/kWh, which is ~$17/kWh at $10/m³ and still ~10x below today's batteries because the rubber is free.
NAME: Heat-released stretch-locked rubber bales
MECHANISM: Bands with no steel, rich in natural rubber, are cut from scrap truck tyres and screened for room-temperature strain fixity. A shared motor-generator winch pulls them in bundles of ~100 to ~5x their length while warm (~1.5 MN pull over a ~12 m stroke), then they are spray-cooled in the grip for ~30 s so strain-induced crystallites lock the stretch. The now force-free bundles are baled at ~600 kg/m³ in shaded, covered yards. To discharge, a bundle is gripped again and warmed with ~45 °C waste heat at a rate set by the grid signal, so the crystallites melt and it contracts against the generator. With a realistic recoverable ~1 Wh/kg, each 500 kg bundle stroke gives ~0.5 kWh, or ~60 kW per winch head on a 30 s cycle, using ~1 t of rubber and ~1.7 m³ of yard (~$17/kWh) per kWh.
VALUE: Industrial sites with low-grade waste heat get leak-free, non-hazardous multi-week storage whose medium is a waste that comes with a disposal fee and whose housing is a covered bale yard.

=== CHECKED D03 ===
ANCHOR: charged isomers crystallise into separate dry powder heaps
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: At 10 A/m² with only ~20 mM dissolved (assuming n = 2), the floor electrodes need a mass-transfer coefficient of k = i/(nFc) ≈ 10/(2×96485×20) ≈ 2.6e-6 m/s. A slow laminar ditch (1 cm/s over 10 m, D = 5e-10 m²/s, Sc ≈ 2000) gives Sh = 0.664Re^½Sc^⅓ ≈ 2600 and k ≈ 1.3e-7 m/s, a limiting current of ~0.5 A/m², which is 20x short. In turbulent flow k scales as u^0.8 d^-0.2, so flow speed is the lever, not ditch size. A 5 cm-deep channel at 0.3 m/s (Re ≈ 1.5e4, Sh ≈ 0.023Re^0.8Sc^⅓ ≈ 640) gives k ≈ 6e-6 m/s and a limit of ≈ 24 A/m². Wall friction then costs only ~0.2 W/m² of pumping (~2% of output), and the 5 cm ion path matches the stated 0.05 V ohmic loss in ~10 S/m electrolyte; crossover through the 2.5 mm divider stays at ≈ 0.2 A/m² ≈ 2%.
NAME: Fast-channel crystallising redox heaps
MECHANISM: Two shallow (~5 cm) carbon-floored channels sit side by side, separated by a ~2.5 mm porous divider, and each circulates one dissolved organic at ~0.3 m/s. The charged form is a strained isomer that crystallises only after a delay, so it nucleates on seed crystals in that side's own downstream settling basin rather than on the electrode, and each side stays saturated at ~20 mM. The turbulent flow sets a mass-transfer limit of ~24 A/m², so the floors run at 10 A/m² (~10 W/m²) with ~2% crossover, ~0.05 V ohmic loss and ~2% pumping. A potential-gated floor catalyst speeds snap-back only at discharge potential, discharge redissolves powder fed back upstream, the two powders sit on separate covered heaps for months, and the channels cost ~$30/m², about $3/W or ~$15/kWh over a 200 h discharge.
VALUE: Grid operators get months-long storage whose energy sits in heaps of cheap dry powder, provided molecules with ~20 mM charged-state solubility and delayed crystallisation can be found.

=== CHECKED D04 ===
ANCHOR: vacuum vapour machine driving swelling-clay hydration
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: Each kg of water adsorbed releases ~2.7 MJ of heat for ~0.2 MJ of work (~13x), so clay storing 7 Wh/kg at ~1000 kg/m³ must shed ~3e8 J/m³ through clay with k ≈ 0.2 W/m·K. With 1.5 K of the 3 K budget spent inside the bed, a slab cooled on both faces carries q ≈ 8kΔT/d², so a bed of thickness d needs t ≈ 3e8·d²/2.4 s to discharge: ~14 h at 2 cm and ~2 weeks at 10 cm. Valving one of ~50-100 beds at a time over a two-week discharge gives each bed only 3-7 h. That forces ~1 cm beds and ~10-15 m² of interleaved bed-to-pond wall per kWh, at least $20-30/kWh even at $2/m², about 3-4x the $7/kWh capacity claim. Allowed thickness scales as √(time each bed works), so how long each bed works is the lever, not how many beds there are. Running all beds in parallel for the whole spell allows ~10 cm beds and ~1.5 m² of wall per kWh (~$3/kWh).
NAME: Parallel vacuum clay-hydration store
MECHANISM: Swelling-clay beds ~10 cm thick are interleaved with shallow water layers through thin polymer walls, which carry no pressure difference inside the vacuum. They sit in earth-covered, vacuum-tight enclosures that resist only atmospheric pressure (0.1 MPa), and they store energy as unmet hydration at ~7 Wh/kg, about 150 kg of clay (~$7/kWh at $50/t) per kWh. To charge, one shared vapour compressor draws vapour from all beds in parallel through a common header down to ~0.5 kPa and condenses it into the water layers. To discharge, the water evaporates and expands into the dry clay through a low-pressure vapour turbine that handles ~1 m³/s of vapour per kW. Because every bed works through the whole multi-week spell, each sheds its adsorption heat (~13x the work) to the adjacent water across ≤3 K, needing only ~1.5 m² of wall per kWh.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-week storage whose capacity costs about as much as clay earthworks, a vacuum liner and plastic film, with one vapour machine sized only for power.

=== CHECKED D05 ===
ANCHOR: salt-step-stratified membraneless electrolytes inside a solar pond
VERDICT: PASS
ESTIMATE: 0.7 m of 1 M electrolyte holds 700 mol/m², or 6.8e7 C/m², which is ≈ 23 kWh/m² at ~1.2 V. At 70 °C (D ≈ 1.5e-9 m²/s), molecular crossover across the 0.1 m step is FDc/L ≈ 1.4 A/m², about 4% of 38 A/m² and ~0.2%/day when idle. Ohmic loss is iL/σ ≈ 38×0.1/30 ≈ 0.13 V (~10%). The product of crossover fraction and ohmic fraction is FDc/(σV) ≈ 0.4%, independent of step thickness, current and temperature (D and σ rise together with heat). The design's 10% × 10% = 1% sits 2.5x above that floor, which leaves room for interface erosion. One number was hidden: to carry 24 A across a 0.8 m cell for 30 mV, the mesh needs a sheet resistance of ≤0.02 Ω/sq, i.e. ~0.5 mm graphite-equivalent, costing ~$1-2/kWh.
NAME: Honeycomb salt-pond flow battery
MECHANISM: A lined solar pond keeps a metre-thick salt gradient on top as a heat trap, so its bottom zone runs at ~70 °C. Inside it, a dense 1 M charged electrolyte ~0.7 m deep lies beneath a lighter one, separated by a 0.1 m salt step with carbon meshes just above and below it, and convection in each warm layer carries reactants to the meshes. Thin plastic honeycomb walls through the full electrolyte depth divide the pond into ~0.8 m cells, and each cell's upper mesh is wired to the next cell's lower mesh, so each mesh carries ~24 A and, as ~0.5 mm graphite-equivalent sheet (≤0.02 Ω/sq), loses ~30 mV, while the walls block shunt currents. At ~38 A/m² this gives ≤10% crossover (≈4% from molecular diffusion), ~10% ohmic loss, ≤0.5% of charge lost per idle day, and ~23 kWh and ~45 W per m² of pond.
VALUE: Grid operators get three-week electrochemical storage built from a salt pond, carbon mesh and a plastic honeycomb, with free solar heat speeding the electrodes and no tanks, membranes or pumps.

=== CHECKED D06 ===
ANCHOR: road base course doubles as slag-electrode battery
VERDICT: FIX
ESTIMATE: Slag Fe and Mn sit in dense, poorly conducting oxide and silicate grains. Only a rim δ of a few µm, reached by both coke contacts and electrolyte, can react, so the usable fraction is ≈ 3δ/r. For road aggregate (r ≈ 5 mm, δ ≈ 5 µm) that is ≈ 0.3%, about 30x below the 10% utilisation that 0.5 GWh/km needs (≈ 840 kg of iron slag per m² × 20% Fe × 2F × 10% ≈ 16 kWh per m² of road). Grain radius controls the limit, not layer thickness. Crushing the active layers to slag sand (r ≈ 0.1 mm) gives 3δ/r ≈ 15%, enough for 10%, for ~$3/t of crushing, about $0.3/kWh.
NAME: Slag-sand road-base battery
MECHANISM: The road's base course is built as sealed, geomembrane-wrapped slabs. Each holds alternating ~7 cm layers of crushed, wüstite-rich steel-slag sand and manganese-oxide-rich slag sand, ground to ≤0.2 mm so ~15% of the metal is within reach, separated by sand, with steel-mesh collectors and 10-20% coke breeze, flooded with 6 M KOH and vented for the hydrogen the iron side evolves. Crystalline slags are chosen because glassy slag fines set like cement in strong alkali and use it up. Each slab is one ~1 V cell, ~1000 slabs in series feed one inverter per substation, and a course 30 m wide and 1 m deep holds ~0.5 GWh per km at 10% utilisation over 3 days, with KOH (~6 kg, ~$5/kWh) the largest cost after the road-funded slag.
VALUE: Grid operators get multi-day storage at about $10/kWh along new road corridors, where the road budget pays for the bulk slag, the housing and the land.
