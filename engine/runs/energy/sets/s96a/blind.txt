C01
MECHANISM: Road bases and embankments are built from redox-active iron or manganese slag aggregate in a damp, ion-conducting binder, with current collectors buried in them, so the construction budget pays for the storage mass. Spread over kilometres, multi-day discharge runs at very low current density, which keeps conversion loss low without an engineered stack. The structure feeds DC directly to the roadside loads and chargers it already powers, so it reuses their existing power electronics. Continuous load-bearing measurements set how far charge may be drawn down before strength suffers.
VALUE: Massive storage mass and much of its power electronics are already paid for by roads and their roadside equipment, so cycling only a few times a year still pays off.

C02
MECHANISM: Surplus power drives a reversible hydraulic pump-motor and rams that squeeze thin, drained beds of swelling clay, pressing the water out from between the mineral sheets. The water drains away, and the dry clay then sits unloaded in a sealed pit. Its energy is held as unmet hydration energy, not as stress, so it needs no pressure vessel. To discharge, water is let back into one section at a time under the same rams, and the very high swelling pressure of the first water layers drives the pump-motor in reverse as a generator. One cheap, mature hydraulic machine handles both directions. The controller works only in the high-force early stage of wetting, where the compression and swelling curves lie close together, so most of the energy comes back.
VALUE: Storage capacity costs about as much as moving earth, and the only converter is standard hydraulic machinery.

C03
MECHANISM: A lined pit of mixed electrolyte, with no membrane, holds both reactants, and simple electrodes dipped into it each react with only one reactant, so the dipped electrode array is the whole converter, with no stack, membrane or pumps. Charging starts only when a weather forecast shows a lull a few days ahead. Because charge is held just for the forecast gap plus the lull, the self-discharge that crossover causes in such a crude design costs only a few percent. The same electrodes are used for charging and discharging and are sized to average lull demand, not peak surplus.
VALUE: Whole-lull storage in a hole in the ground, where forecast timing turns cheap, leaky containment and electrodes into an acceptable round-trip loss.

C04
MECHANISM: A trench of biochar soaked in neutral brine is both the energy store and its own power converter, because charge sits on the char's internal surface right where the current flows, so no separate stack or membrane has to be built. Impedance tomography maps pockets that will not charge, and those pockets are dried and left as insulating walls. This splits the bed into many series cells, so its voltage climbs to the DC input level of a standard grid inverter, and the inverter is the only power hardware left. Discharging over days keeps current density in the bulk tiny, so resistive loss stays small even in a thick, crude bed, and the low voltage per cell in neutral brine limits self-discharge.
VALUE: Multi-day storage whose cell, separators and voltage stacking are all dug with earthmoving equipment, leaving only an off-the-shelf inverter as factory-made converter cost.

C05
MECHANISM: The strained isomer oxidizes at a lower potential than its relaxed partner, and once it is oxidized it snaps open to the relaxed radical cation. That radical cation is then reduced at a second electrode at a higher potential, so relaxing the molecule yields a cell voltage of up to about the strain energy per electron. Charging runs through the radical-anion route instead, where the strained form is the downhill product, so charge and discharge each follow their own one-way path. Because each electrode selects its reactant by potential, both electrodes sit in the same liquid in a short, membraneless channel fed from steel tanks. Radical ions exist only in that channel, so the tank cannot self-discharge through a chain reaction.
VALUE: The whole system is one cheap liquid in plain tanks plus a membraneless two-electrode channel, so adding days of storage costs only tank volume and the converter has no membrane or second electrolyte.

C06
MECHANISM: A lined pit holds horizontal layers of brine-soaked sand about a decimetre thick, separated by cheap bipolar carbon sheets. Charging makes a soluble oxidized species on one face of each sheet and a reduced species on the other, and the sand stops convection, so the two meet only by diffusion. Diffusion time grows with the square of the layer thickness, while resistance grows only with the thickness. At decimetre spacing the charge therefore holds for months, and a discharge spread over days loses only a few percent to resistance. Stacking the layers in series raises the voltage. The carbon sheets are the whole converter, and because they run at a few amps per square metre they can be the cheapest grade.
VALUE: Membrane-free electrochemical storage for weeks, where both the energy and the power side are earthworks, sand, brine and carbon sheet.

C07
MECHANISM: Two cheap ore heaps, one iron oxide and one manganese oxide, each threaded with plain steel mesh and separated by a layer of sand, form the cell. The ore heaps themselves are the electrodes. A shared alkaline reservoir floods a bed pair only when it is about to charge or discharge, and drains it afterwards. Draining stops iron's hydrogen-evolving self-discharge, so there is no need for a costly, lossy air electrode or membrane. Because the electrode area is just more ore, the beds can be made hectares wide and run at very low current density, which keeps voltage losses small and round-trip efficiency high.
VALUE: The power converter turns into a cheap heap of ore that sits dry and idle at no cost, giving weeks of storage that is cheap and efficient on both the energy side and the power side.

C08
MECHANISM: Charged electrolyte is dried with trehalose into a glass, using waste heat from the charging stack. In the glass, even highly reactive molecules barely move, so the store can use high-voltage, fast-kinetics redox molecules such as organic radicals that would normally decompose within days. Their fast electron transfer and high voltage per electron let a small plain carbon stack with no catalyst deliver high power at low overpotential. The glass is stored in bulk like grain and redissolved only as discharge needs it.
VALUE: Vitrification lets the chemistry be chosen for converter speed and efficiency rather than shelf life, which shrinks the stack and its losses while weeks of charge sit in a grain silo.
