 Parent A: MECHANISM: Electrodes are drilled into a conductive sulfide or magnetite ore body that sits across a natural redox gradient. PAIR 2
 Parent A: MECHANISM: Surplus current at an electrode rearranges a cheap, small organic liquid into a cramped, high-strain shape made of the same atoms. Nothing at room temperature can push it back over the barrier to relaxing, so it stays charged for as long as it is stored. The strained liquid sits in plain steel tanks. When power is needed, it flows past a catalytic electrode surface that lowers the barrier, and as each molecule snaps back to its relaxed shape it gives up electrons to the external circuit. The liquid changes colour as it relaxes, so the charged fraction can be read optically. Only the tank volume grows with extra days of backup.
   VALUE: Weeks of reserve come down to tank space for a liquid that is reused forever and uses up no air, ore or carbon.
   nearest published work: unknown
 Parent B: MECHANISM: Cheap but unstable redox molecules (organic radicals or mixed-valence metal salts) are charged in water mixed with trehalose. Spare heat dries the mix into a sugar glass in which those molecules can barely move, so self-discharge and breakdown almost stop. The glass is stored in bulk like grain and redissolved for discharge. Water content and glass-transition temperature are the control settings.
   VALUE: The cheapest redox chemistries, which normally leak charge, can hold it for weeks without tanks.
   nearest published work: unknown
