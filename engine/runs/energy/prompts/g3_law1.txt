Counting many earlier attempts at this question revealed these quantitative laws (each holds for any design of the kind it names):
- Transport-length law for any bulk or in-situ electrode bed, pit, pond, layer or current collector discharged over time t: the usable thickness is L = √(2κΔV·t/q_vol) for ion conduction, L = √(D·t) or √(nFDc/j) for diffusion, and the collector width is W = √(8ΔV/(i·R_s)). Areal energy is therefore capped at E_area ≈ √(2κ·ΔV·V·t·e_vol). Slab, pond, ore-body or bed size cancels out, and conductivity × time controls instead (E05, E07, E08, E13, E14, E17, E18, E20, E21). (rules out: metre-thick beds, deep pits, ore lenses or waste ponds wired with sparse electrodes. It also rules out pond-wide planar collectors and anything relying on low-conductivity pore water (about 0.01-0.1 S/m).; opens: thin cm-scale layers with high-κ electrolyte (KOH at about 12 S/m) or a conductive filler (coke). Long discharge helps because L grows as √t, which allows multi-day electrodes about 50x thicker and so about 50x less foil, separator and can per kWh. Advection escapes the law entirely: a Darcy flow delivers nFcu/2 per area whatever the bed length, at about 0.1% pumping power.)
- Crossover-ohmic invariant for any two-species cell kept apart by a diffusive barrier (separator, sand, spent buffer layer or porous divider): (crossover fraction) × (ohmic fraction) = nF·D_active·c/(σ·V). Barrier thickness, current density and even temperature cancel, since by Walden's rule D and σ both scale as 1/viscosity. Lowering the current (oversizing) makes crossover worse (E12, E19, E20, E22). (rules out: small, concentrated (about 1 M) redox ions behind thin porous separators. It also rules out fixing leakage with thicker barriers, heating, or slower, oversized cells.; opens: a large ratio of salt mobility to active-species mobility. That means macromolecular or redox-polymer actives (D about 1e-13), or dilute actives (about 0.2 M) in strong supporting salt (about 4 M NaCl). Longer discharge also helps, because it lowers the leak per stored charge.)
- Homogeneous co-mixing law for any membrane-free or single-phase design where the charged species meet in the bulk: the loss rate k·c² (diffusion-limited, k about 1e8-1e10 M⁻¹s⁻¹) beats the useful rate j/(nF·h) by about 10^10-10^11. A surface catalyst cannot gate a bulk reaction, and metastable charged states that live nanoseconds cannot reach electrodes seconds away (E10, E22, E23). (rules out: mixed membrane-free channels, strain-energy or metastable-molecule cells, and designs that rely on a slow bulk reaction.; opens: spatial separation with a spent-electrolyte buffer of thickness s, where the leak falls as exp(−s²/4Dt). Short contact times (minutes) with s ≫ √(Dt), for example a 1.5 mm spent lamina, cut the leak to about 1%.)
- Converter-hours law for any long-duration store covering a lull of length T: it delivers min(P·T, E), so it needs E/P ≤ T. Power-side cost per kWh is c_P/(E/P), only about $0.5-1/kWh at E/P of 170-340 h. Forecast lead time and shared inverters barely matter, so the storage medium sets the cost (E04, E17). (rules out: very cheap undersized converters slowly trickling a giant store (E/P ≫ T). It also rules out expecting savings from early discharge, forecasting or shared grid ties.; opens: E/P ≈ T/2 (about 250 h) with the converter running about 50% of hours. All of the 10x cost cut must then come from $/kWh of the medium (about ≤$20/kWh).)
- Host-flow law for any design that piggybacks on existing infrastructure or by-product streams (pipes, a chemical market, mine drainage, waste liquor): energy or power = host volume or throughput × concentration of the active species. It scales with the host, not with the grid (E01, E02, E06). (rules out: dilute or small hosts, such as water mains (about 100x short), the world H2O2 market (about 0.45 GW against 10 GW per grid) and acid-mine drainage (about 11 years per GWh).; opens: concentrated streams. Pickle liquor at about 120 g/L Fe is 600x richer, about 1 GWh/yr per line. Use the host only for cheap medium and size the power stage to the grid.)
- Stress-holding bound for any store that keeps energy as load in a structure (pressed clay, hydraulic, elastic or overburden): energy per mass of structure ≤ f·σ/(1.5ρ), about 1-3 Wh/kg for steel, whatever the pressure, plate or column size. Cost is therefore (structure $/kg)/(Wh/kg) (E09, E16). (rules out: any design whose stored state needs continuous load-bearing containment (hundreds of kg of steel per kWh). Earth overburden cannot carry pressures above about 10 km of rock either.; opens: latched or force-free stored states, such as strain-crystallised locked rubber that carries no force. Housing then drops to bale-yard cost, and cost is set only by the medium's $/kg ÷ Wh/kg.)
- Frozen-mobility law for any design that locks charge by freezing or vitrifying the electrolyte: by Walden's rule, conductivity ∝ 1/viscosity, so the same immobility that stops self-discharge also cuts conductance by about 10^6. Thawing costs c_p·ΔT, about 1.6x the stored energy density (E11). (rules out: glass-locked or solid-locked electrolytes discharged without heat recovery, or at normal current densities and gaps.; opens: ≥90% heat recuperation, with large, thin-gap, low-current stacks that are affordable only because discharge lasts about 100 h or more.)
- Wetted-surface self-discharge law for any porous metal or iron electrode held for weeks: pores with capillary entry pressure 2γ/r above the drainage head (r of about µm, about 1.4 bar against about 0.1 bar) stay flooded. Self-discharge is therefore set by the hydrogen overpotential on the wetted surface, not by liquid level (E03). (rules out: drain-to-hold or dry-storage schemes for porous electrodes, which still lose about 1%/day.; opens: cheap kinetic poisons for hydrogen evolution, such as about 1 wt% Bi₂S₃ at about $0.3/kWh, giving 0.1-0.2%/day loss.)
