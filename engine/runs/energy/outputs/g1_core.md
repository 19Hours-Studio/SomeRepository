**1. Goals from the center**
- G1: Make energy-capacity cost (dollars per kWh) close to zero. The stored medium has to cost almost nothing and need almost no containment.
- G2: Keep idle losses close to zero for weeks, including self-discharge and the medium breaking down.
- G3: Pay for capital that cycles only a few times a year. The asset has to earn its cost some other way while it sits idle.

**2. Paradigm negation**
Mainstream approaches build a dedicated, contained store (a cell, tank, cavern or pile) that is always ready and paid for by energy throughput alone. That is banned here as a mechanism.

**3. Known families avoided:** no mediator shuttle, no gas, heat or pressure store, no fuel loop, no load shifting, no grid smoothing.

**4. Mechanism from the periphery**
- G1, from ore geophysics: Sulfide ore bodies are natural "geobatteries" (Sato–Mooney). The conductive mineral connects a reduced deep zone to an oxidized shallow zone, and groundwater acts as the electrolyte. Ported: drive current into an ore body where it lies, so the rock in place is the active mass.
- G2, from anhydrobiosis: Tardigrades and seeds survive by drying into a trehalose glass, which stops reaction speed almost completely. Water content is the switch. Ported: charge cheap redox molecules in water, then dry them into glass. They rehydrate only when it's time to discharge.
- G3, from bone remodeling: Bone holds up the body and is also the body's calcium store. When the hormone PTH rises, osteoclasts withdraw calcium within what the structure can tolerate, and it is redeposited later. Ported: civil structures whose aggregate is redox-active, so construction budgets pay for the mass.

**5. Honesty check**
- Ore body: Closest work is electrokinetic remediation and in-situ leaching. Neither stores and returns charge, so the idea is kept.
- Glass: Closest work is precipitation flow batteries and freeze-dried enzymes. Nobody uses vitrification to block self-discharge, so it is kept.
- Structures: Closest work is MIT's carbon-cement supercapacitor and Chalmers' structural batteries. Those are capacitive or fabricated cells. This is redox storage in bulk aggregate under a depletion budget, so it is kept.

=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: In-place ore body geobattery
MECHANISM: Electrodes are drilled into a conductive sulfide or magnetite ore body that sits across a natural redox gradient. Grid current partly reduces the ore near one end and oxidizes it near the other, and groundwater carries the ions. Discharge reverses this, using the same conduction the ore already shows naturally. Electrode spacing and current are tuned from measurements of the rock's own voltage (self-potential) so the charged front spreads evenly.
VALUE: Millions of tonnes of active mass that is never mined, moved or contained, so the capacity cost is mostly drilling.

IDEA 2
NAME: Vitrified charged electrolyte stockpile
MECHANISM: Cheap but unstable redox molecules (organic radicals or mixed-valence metal salts) are charged in water mixed with trehalose. Spare heat dries the mix into a sugar glass in which those molecules can barely move, so self-discharge and breakdown almost stop. The glass is stored in bulk like grain and redissolved for discharge. Water content and glass-transition temperature are the control settings.
VALUE: The cheapest redox chemistries, which normally leak charge, can hold it for weeks without tanks.

IDEA 3
NAME: Remodeling structural energy reserve
MECHANISM: Road bases, embankments and foundations are built with redox-active aggregate (iron or manganese oxide slag) held in a damp, ion-conducting binder, with buried current collectors. During long lulls a grid-scarcity price signal draws charge down to a set structural depletion limit, as bone gives up calcium under PTH, and surplus power replaces the charge later. Load-bearing capacity is checked continuously and sets that limit.
VALUE: Massive storage mass whose cost is already paid by construction, so equipment that cycles only a few times a year still pays off.
