E01 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: A 150 mm main holds about 17.7 L per metre. At about 1.5 M Fe²⁺ in an all-iron couple (1.2 V, 3 Fe²⁺ per 2 e⁻) that is about 0.57 kWh/m in theory and about 0.4 kWh/m usable, so a roughly 300 m segment per street transformer stores about 120 kWh. A 100-home transformer averaging about 100 kW needs about 10-17 MWh for 4-7 days, about 100x more. Energy scales with bore area × iron solubility, so even a 600 mm main (16x the bore area) or 2 M electrolyte cannot close the gap.

E02 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: Discharging percarbonate (2H2O2→2H2O+O2: 1.07 V ideal, about 0.5 V in practice, 1 e⁻ per H2O2) gives about 0.13 kWh/kg, so the stockpile costs only about $3/kWh at $0.4/kg. The converter's power, however, is fixed by its merchant production current. A 100 kt/yr H2O2 plant runs about 1.8e7 A, which at 0.5 V delivers only about 9 MW, and the world's entire output of about 5 Mt/yr corresponds to about 0.45 GW. That is about 20x less than one 10 GW grid needs. Discharge power scales with the size of the chemical market, not the size of the grid. Round-trip is about 10% (about 2.2 V to make, about 0.5 V back).

E03 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: The formation charge turns dense hematite into sponge iron with about 50% porosity and ~1 µm pores. Their capillary entry pressure (2γ/r ≈ 1.4 bar) far exceeds a ~0.1 bar drainage head, so draining empties only the gaps between grains. The iron surfaces stay wet, and the trapped pore water is enough to oxidise about half the charge. Loss therefore continues at the flooded rate of about 1%/day, about 25% over a 4-week hold, against the roughly 5% or less needed. What controls self-discharge is the hydrogen overpotential on the wetted iron, not the liquid level between grains. About 1 wt% bismuth sulfide, known to cut hydrogen evolution 5-10x, brings the loss to about 0.1-0.2%/day for about $0.3/kWh.

E04 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: If the store holds a design lull's deficit D for T = 170-340 h, a converter of E/500 h delivers only D·T/500 ≈ 0.35-0.7 D during the lull. Discharging days early cannot close the gap: that energy can only be carried by daily batteries holding about 8 h of load, which covers 8/T ≈ 2-5% of the deficit. So the converter is 1.5-3x too small. What matters is converter hours (E/P) against lull length T, not forecast lead time. Setting P ≈ E/250 h still keeps the converter running about 4,400 h a year (about 50% of hours) at the same yearly throughput of about 17 E.

E05 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: Formation free energies put the hematite/magnetite and magnetite/ferrous-hydroxide couples only ~0.37 V apart, so 100 MW needs ~2.7e8 A. Holding ohmic loss to 10% then needs under ~1.4e-10 Ω, which through 30 m of brine-filled rock (σ ≈ 0.1 S/m) means ~2e12 m² of facing lens area. A real 1 km² lens pair carries ~1e-4 A/m², about 50 W in total, so the design falls short by ~10^6. The length of the ion path between the lenses controls the power, not the mineral surface area. That path cannot be shortened in place, because two electrodes inside one conductive ore body short-circuit.

E06 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: An all-iron flow battery (~1.2 V) needs ~3.5 kg of iron per kWh (31 mol of electrons, 47 mol Fe at ~70% use), i.e. ~3500 t per GWh. A large acid-mine discharge (50 L/s at 200 mg/L Fe) carries only ~300 t/yr, which means ~11 years and ~1.7e7 m³ of water per GWh. Electrolyte supply, not the stack, therefore limits the claim. The controlling ratio is the iron concentration of the feed: spent steel pickle liquor (~120 g/L Fe, assumed, about 600x richer) needs only ~30,000 m³ per GWh, so one pickling line making ~100 m³/day supplies ~1 GWh of electrolyte per year.

E07 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: Fly ash with about 7% iron at 1.2 t/m³ holds about 80 kWh/m³ in theory. Even an optimistic 10% use over a week needs about 48 A/m³, and with ash pore water at κ_eff ≈ 0.1 S/m the ion path to the cap can be only L = √(2κΔV/j) ≈ √(2·0.1·0.2/48) ≈ 3 cm. So only the top few centimetres work: about 0.2 kWh per m² of cap, about 100 MWh for a 50 ha pond, 20-50x short of multi-GWh. Raising conductivity with caustic electrolyte is ruled out in an unlined waste pond.

E08 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Steel slag with about 25% FeO (0.75 Ah/g FeO) holds about 370 kWh/m³ in theory at about 1 V. Delivering 10% of that over 3 days needs about 520 A/m³. In a damp cement-like binder (κ ≈ 0.01 S/m), keeping the ohmic drop ΔV = jL²/2κ at or below 0.1 V limits electrode layers to L = √(2κΔV/j) ≈ 2 mm, about 100x thinner than a slab. The scaling law L ∝ √(κ·t/u) shows electrolyte conductivity is what controls, while slab size only appears to. Flooding open-graded slag (35% voids) with 6 M KOH (κ_eff ≈ 12 S/m) gives L ≈ 7 cm.

E09 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: The first interlayer water is held at Δμ = RT ln(RH about 0.15) ≈ 4.7 kJ/mol ≈ 0.26 MJ per kg of water. At about 0.1 kg water per kg clay that is about 7 Wh per kg of clay, equivalent to a press pressure of Δμ/V_w ≈ 260 MPa. Any structure holding a pressure p over a volume that changes by a fraction f stores at most fσ/(1.5ρ) ≈ 0.2 × 300 MPa / (1.5 × 7850 kg/m³) ≈ 1.4 Wh per kg of steel, whatever the pressure, plate or column size. That is about 700 kg (about $700) of steel per kWh, roughly 50x the goal. 260 MPa is also the weight of about 10 km of overburden, so earth cannot carry it either.

E10 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: The hole-transfer step (relaxed radical cation + strained neutral → relaxed neutral + strained radical cation) is downhill by the same roughly 0.9 eV (about 90 kJ/mol ÷ F) that would give the cell voltage, so it runs near diffusion control (k about 1e9-1e10 M⁻¹s⁻¹). Its rate depends on the abundant neutral strained molecules (about 1-10 M), not on the dilute cations, so low current density does not help. Each relaxed cation lives about 0.1-1 ns and moves about 1 nm, while reaching a cathode 100 µm downstream takes seconds: off by about 10^10. On top of that, the anode itself, held below the relaxed couple's potential, re-reduces any relaxed cation that touches it.

E11 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Walden's rule (conductivity scales as 1/viscosity) ties the stack's conductance to the same mobility the glass freezes. A sugar melt at Tg+40 K has a viscosity of about 10^3-10^4 Pa·s (WLF), so its conductivity is about 10^-6 S/m, against about 5 S/m needed for 100 mA/cm² at 0.1 V over a 0.5 mm gap: roughly 10^6 short. Getting to about 0.5 Pa·s (about 0.01 S/m) needs about Tg+120 K. With Tg at 50°C that means about 170°C, which costs 1.6 kJ/kg·K × 145 K ≈ 64 Wh/kg of sensible heat against about 40 Wh/kg stored (1.5 mol/kg radicals per side, 2 V cell). So the heat exchanger must recover at least 90%, and the stack must be large, thin-gapped and slow, which is affordable only because discharge lasts about 100 h.

E12 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: With 1 M active species, D_eff ≈ 2e-10 m²/s in sand and a 0.1 m barrier, the two species leak toward each other at 2F·D_eff·c/L ≈ 0.39 A/m². That is ~2.7%/day of the 1.3e6 C/m² stored for a 5-day discharge at 3 A/m², so about half the charge is gone in a month instead of holding for months (~5x off). The scaling law is that daily loss × ohmic fraction ≈ 2F·D_eff·c·(1 day)·(L+h)/(σ_eff·V·L·t_d), so barrier thickness L only trades one loss for the other. Only lowering c while keeping σ with supporting salt, or lengthening the discharge, closes the gap. With 0.2 M active species in ~4 M NaCl (σ_eff ≈ 5 S/m), L = 0.13 m, 0.15 m conductive zones and a 1.7 A/m² one-week discharge, the leak is ~0.06 A/m² (~0.5%/day) and the ohmic loss ~8%.

E13 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Storing 10 days of a panel's average ~40 W/m² is about 9.6 kWh/m². At about 1 M one-electron dense phase and 1.2 V (about 32 kWh/m³), that is a 30 cm layer. But molecules in a coacervate diffuse at D ≈ 1e-11 to 1e-10 m²/s and reach only √(D·t) ≈ 3-9 mm from the felt in 10 days, so only 1-3% of the layer is usable, about 50x short. Filling the zone with conductive coke fixes that reach. Ionic conduction then caps the bed: E_area = √(2κ·ΔV·V·t·e_vol) ≈ √(2·2·0.1·1.2·8.6e5·1.15e8) J/m² ≈ 1.9 kWh/m² for a 6 cm bed, so the mat must be about 5x the panel area.

E14 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Assumptions: about 1 mmol e⁻/g of reversible layer iron at 1500 kg/m³, giving about 1.45e8 C/m³ (about 24 kWh/m³ at 0.6 V), discharged over two weeks, so about 120 A/m³. Ionic drop through a bed of thickness H is j·H²/(2σ). With pore brine at an effective 2 S/m, keeping the drop under 0.05 V needs H ≤ √(2σΔV/j) ≈ 4 cm. Mediator reach is √(FDc/j) ≈ 2 mm (50 mM, effective D 1e-10 m²/s). So beds a metre thick fail by about 30-100x in thickness, and rods metres apart fail by far more. Thickness, not bed volume, is the controlling length.

E15 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Plain biochar (about 30 F/g) in neutral brine at 1 V stores ½·(30/4 F/g)·(1 V)² ≈ 1 Wh/kg of cell, which at about $200/t of char is about $200/kWh. "Several-fold" loading still leaves about $50/kWh against the roughly $20/kWh needed. Also, one redox couple on both sides of a symmetric bed adds no voltage, so the gain only comes from two different quinone families about 0.5 V apart. Slow-pyrolysis liquor yields about 0.1 kg of phenolics per kg of char; oxidised to quinones and split between the two halves, this gives about 13 mAh/g × 0.5 V plus the double layer ≈ 8 Wh/kg, or about $12-25/kWh at char prices of $100-200/t.

E16 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Strain-crystallizing tyre rubber stretched to about 4-5x its length stores about 10-15 kJ/kg, and after about 25% hysteresis about 3 Wh/kg comes back. So each kWh needs about 300 kg of bands, and every other cost must stay under about $0.03 per kg of rubber to reach about $10/kWh. Racks that hold the stretch need about 2 kg of steel per kg of rubber (10 MPa band stress over a 5x length, vs 200 MPa steel), about $600/kWh. A shed at about $100/m³ with racks packed at 300 kg/m³ adds about $100/kWh, so the housing is 10-60x too expensive. Cost scales as (housing $/kg)/(Wh/kg). Because locked bands carry no force, housing can drop to the price of a bale yard.

E17 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Sharing the plant's inverter and grid tie (~$150/kW) over a 1-2 week store (E/P = 170-340 h) saves only about $0.5-1/kWh, so the cells set the cost. Today's LFP cells cost about $50-60/kWh, but a 10x cut needs about $20/kWh or less, so the design is roughly 3x short. The scaling law: at multi-day rates the usable electrode thickness is L ≈ √(D_eff·t) ≈ √(3e-11 m²/s × 6e5 s) ≈ 4 mm, 50x today's 80 µm. That cuts foils, separators and cans per kWh about 50x, leaving active materials (LFP ~$10 + graphite ~$4 + electrolyte ~$3) at about $17-20/kWh.

E18 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: At ~70 °C (D ≈ 2e-9 m²/s, σ ≈ 30 S/m), a 0.1 m step leaks 2F·D·c/L ≈ 3.9 A/m² at 1 M. Running at ~38 A/m² therefore balances ~10% leak against ~10% ohmic loss, with ~0.7 m layers for a three-week discharge, so the electrochemistry passes. But a pond-wide mesh must carry that current sideways: the drop is ≈ i·R_s·W²/8 ≈ 475 V across a 100 m pond with a 0.01 Ω/sq graphite mesh, against ~0.03 V allowed. Pond width, not depth or step thickness, controls the limit, and W must be ≤ ~0.8 m.

E19 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: A 50 µm porous cellulose sheet lets 1 M of small redox ions through (D_eff ≈ 1.5e-10 m²/s after porosity and tortuosity) at F·D·c/L ≈ 290 A/m². That is about three times the ~100 A/m² an oversized cell carries, so most of the charge would cross the separator. The scaling law is that the crossover fraction F·D·c/(L·i) times the ohmic fraction i·L/(σ·V) is fixed at F·D·c/(σ·V) ≈ 0.0024 (σ ≈ 20 S/m, V ≈ 1 V) whatever the thickness or current, so oversizing (lower i) makes crossover worse and only the diffusivity ratio of active species to salt ions can move it. A 30 µm regenerated-cellulose (cellophane) film with redox polymers of tens of kDa (D_eff ≈ 1e-13 m²/s, assumed) gives ~0.3 A/m² of crossover, ~1% of a 30 A/m² current, with under 1 mV of separator drop.

E20 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: A 5 m pit of 1 M electrolyte stores F·c·H/2 ≈ 2.4e8 C per m² of footprint, so a one-week discharge needs ~400 A/m² through 5 m of brine (σ ≈ 20 S/m). That is ~100 V of ohmic drop against a ~1.2 V cell, roughly 1000x too much. The scaling law is that the drop, F·c·H²/(2σ·t_d), grows with depth squared, while leak across a middle buffer of thickness L is 2F·D·c/L (D ≈ 1e-9 m²/s). With H ≈ 0.4 m, c = 0.3 M, a permanent 0.25 m spent middle layer and 3 A/m², the ohmic loss is ~5% and the leak (0.23 A/m²) is ~8% of the current.

E21 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: Assumptions: about 1 mmol e⁻/g of native quinone sites, 500 kg lignin per m³ and a 100 h discharge. The bed must then deliver about 130 A/m³. Diffusing fragments (10 mM, effective D about 1e-10 m²/s, 2 e⁻) can supply only nFDc/L per unit area, so every grain must lie within L ≈ √(nFDc/j) ≈ 1 mm of an electrode. Electrode area scales as 1/L², so this needs about 10^6 times more electrode than "sparse buried electrodes" spaced a metre apart. Advection breaks this scaling: pore water carrying 0.1 M fragments at a Darcy flow of about 3 m/day delivers nFcu·½ ≈ 340 A/m², enough to drain about 2.5 m of bed, at about 0.1% pumping power (permeability about 1e-11 m²).

E22 (FAIL)
ESTIMATE: In one membrane-free channel kept saturated with both charged species (say 20 mM each), they react on contact at the outer-sphere electron-transfer rate k ≈ 1e8 M⁻¹s⁻¹. That is a loss of k·c² ≈ 4e4 M/s, while a 0.5 m deep ditch at 10 A/m² does useful work at only j/(nFh) ≈ 1e-7 M/s, about 11 orders of magnitude less. A catalyst on the floor cannot gate a reaction happening in the bulk liquid. With a thin porous divider instead, crossover fraction × ohmic drop = nF·D·c_sat/κ ≈ 2·96485·1e-10·20/0.5 ≈ 8e-4 V, so a 0.05 V drop allows about 1.5% crossover through a ~2.5 mm divider at 10 A/m².

E23 (FIX)
ESTIMATE: During discharge both tray layers arrive fully charged, so the two charged species meet at the liquid–liquid interface. If electron transfer there is fast (as for ferrocene/ferricyanide), the leak is limited only by diffusion, at ≈2F·c·√(D/πt) ≈ 260 A/m² over a 3-min stay (c = 1 M, D = 1e-9 m²/s). That is several times the ≤50 A/m² that the ohmic drop of the 1 mm organic layer (σ ≈ 0.4 S/m) allows. The controlling length is a spent buffer s at the interface compared with √(D·t) ≈ 0.35 mm, since leak falls as exp(−s²/4Dt). A 1.5 mm spent-brine lamina with 2-min residence cuts the leak to ~0.0007 mol/m² per pass, ~1.3% of the 0.05 mol/m² moved at 40 A/m².
