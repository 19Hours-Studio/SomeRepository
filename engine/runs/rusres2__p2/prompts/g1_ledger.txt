LESSONS:
- No ground-cooling or pile fix has a measured lifetime against thaw. Cooling weakens as winters warm, and design norms lag the ground.
- The unit cost of a remote site is dominated by external infrastructure and power. Power and the transport window repeatedly slipped or went over budget, by 38% to 60% or more.
- Every domestic substitute for imported plant is at prototype or unit scale. Spares for the Western base remain the schedule risk.
- Seasonal windows and ice-class tonnage cap deliveries, and ice-class tonnage is the scarcest item (only 2 Arc7 carriers).
- Many failures were organisational (poor maintenance, immature design, ignored warnings), not physics.
- The evidence base is thin: many figures are press, vendor, secondary or conflicting, and few site-level permafrost data exist. This applies to the cost-per-unit comparisons too.
Lessons from earlier runs on other questions (data):
- Most answers that failed after review failed on one number nobody had computed: a rate times the working time against the window it needs, or a strength against months of exposure. Compute the deciding number before claiming.
- The most common overstatement was a weak baseline. Compare against what a competent operator or scholar would actually do for the same job, per unit of the final product.
- Decisive inputs that were assumed rather than sourced (efficiencies, rates, losses, costs, dates) moved 3-10x when checked. Give a source for every number the answer depends on.
- Statistics and simulations gave confident wrong answers until they were checked on a case with a known answer; one null model rejected far more often than its alpha.
- A method validated on synthetic data still gave a wrong result when its input data had unverified labels: a hobby-page list read as "which hole is opposite which" gave p = 0.0005 twice, in two separate runs. Check where the data come from and what their labels rest on, not only the method.
- State the scope of every claimed gain (which steps of the chain, which speed or load tier): the same chip design was audited at 1.5x for decode alone and 1.05x once the prefill hardware it needs was counted.
- A fix that works in one state of use only (fully flat, peak load, cold start) left the defect in the other states the user sees: a crease flattened by tension only when open is raw at partial angles. Check the claim in every state of use.
- The search for earlier attempts looked at the problem and missed patents on the winning mechanism itself (flat-state display tensioning: Apple EP 4738056, Huawei US 20240069604). Search for the mechanism of each surviving idea, not only for the problem.
- Raw data (3D models, catalogues, measurement tables) settled in one step what texts, judges and reviewers could not. Look for data before arguing from opinions.
- Claims of "never tried" were usually wrong. Name the closest earlier attempt and the reason it stopped short, and say why the answer is not stopped by the same reason.
- What the asker says may be wrong or incomplete; treat it as data to check, like any other source.
- Several partial answers that each fit some facts were often parts of one answer; check whether candidates combine before choosing one.
- "Several times cheaper" is arithmetically possible only where the cost terms an answer can touch hold most of the delivered cost; taxes, royalties and fees cap the gain at 1 / (their share).
- A single clever link rarely moved the whole chain more than 1.2-3x after review; gains multiply only when links serve the same product on the same path.
- The simplest competent alternative that uses the same insight often beat the clever design. Check it before claiming a gain.
- Seasonal and climatic windows, load-bearing limits and the time a structure must last killed more designs than missing technology.
