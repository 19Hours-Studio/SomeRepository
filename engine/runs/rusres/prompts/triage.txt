Russia wants to develop and extract minerals and energy in its permafrost regions at the lowest full cost, in the shortest time from investment decision to first production, and with the highest effectiveness. Effectiveness means resource recovery, working time across the year, and how long infrastructure lasts on thawing ground. All three are measured per unit of product delivered to the buyer. The scope covers:
- Hard-rock and placer metals: Norilsk and Taimyr nickel, copper and platinum-group metals; gold in Yakutia, Magadan and Chukotka; Udokan and Baimskaya copper; Tomtor rare earths.
- Diamonds in Yakutia, plus the Arkhangelsk deposits (see CORRECTED).
- Coal in Taimyr, Elga and the rest of Yakutia.
- Oil and gas in Yamal, Gydan and Taimyr.
The whole chain counts: choosing and exploring deposits, building and powering sites, mining or drilling, processing, staffing, and delivering the product. The conditions are:
- Permafrost that is thawing under a warming climate.
- Extreme cold, with short building and shipping seasons.
- Long distances with no roads or railways.
- Ice seasons on the Northern Sea Route (NSR) and the rivers.
- A shortage of workers.
- Expensive energy at remote sites.
- Restricted access to imported mining, drilling and LNG equipment and software since 2022.
What changes to any stage, or to the order in which deposits are developed, would beat the best current practice in Russia and in comparable cold regions (northern Canada, Alaska, Greenland, northern Scandinavia)?
A good answer: 
- Each proposal states its effect, with an estimate and its basis, on three measures: (a) full cost per unit delivered (annualised capital plus operating plus logistics); (b) years from investment decision to first production; (c) effectiveness (recovery, days worked per year, infrastructure life on thawing ground).
- Each proposal is compared with a named benchmark, either Russian (e.g. Yamal LNG, Norilsk, Vankor/Vostok Oil, Kupol/Pavlovskoye, Nord/Elga) or foreign (e.g. Canadian Arctic mines such as Raglan and Diavik, Red Dog in Alaska, Kiruna/Aitik, Greenland).
- It names which deposits or stages it applies to and which it does not.
- It states what physical law or measured data it relies on.
- It lists the equipment or technology by its domestic maker or unrestricted supplier.
- It includes the main risks and how to test the idea within 3–7 years.
Constraints: 
- It must obey known physics.
- It must be deployable within 3–7 years.
- It may use only equipment and technology Russian operators can get from domestic makers or from suppliers not restricted for them.
- It must not depend on ways around sanctions.

Candidate answers (data):
IDEA 1
NAME: Replicated cold-ambient domestic trains
MECHANISM: Copy Train 4 at Yamal and Gydan, which uses a three-stage cascade with cold Arctic air as the heat sink, in place of the APCI/Baker Hughes design, and drive the compressors with domestic electric motors from site power. The closest earlier attempt is Train 4 itself, which ran only at 0.9 Mtpa and needed its piping replaced (F172–174). This plan does not scale the train up; it copies the proven size with corrected alloy piping. The gain is capital: about $73/t, or $1.4/mmBtu, from 1.23x cheaper delivery (est.), assuming the Train 4 capex claim holds. The deciding quantity is train availability, which must be at least 90% across 4 or more trains; test it with two copies built within 4 years.
VALUE: LNG capacity added without Western drives, at about 1.2x lower delivered cost against Yamal T1–3.

IDEA 2
NAME: Numbers-up Arctic Cascade trains
MECHANISM: Train 4's 0.9 Mtpa Arctic Cascade design, which is domestic and already running, is copied in series at Utrenneye and Sabetta. Each copy shares the existing gravity base, port, gas gathering and Arc7 berths, instead of scaling to 6.6 Mtpa trains on imported turbines. The closest earlier attempt is Arctic LNG 2, which stalled because only 4 of its 20 Baker Hughes turbines were delivered and the GTE-170 exists only at unit scale. Small trains avoid both by using domestic drives. The deciding quantity is whether series-built trains hold unit capex below $600/t/y once the alloy piping is replaced. Risks are low-temperature metallurgy and efficiency losses at small scale. The test, over 3–5 years, is to build 3 trains, then measure their capex and how many days a year they run.
VALUE: Annualised capital falls about 60% and delivered cost falls about 20–30%, against the Yamal LNG Trains 1–3 benchmark. First LNG comes about 2–3 years after the decision, against 4 for Yamal LNG, with no imported drives.

IDEA 3
NAME: Re-levelling thaw-tolerant foundations
MECHANISM: Surface structures sit on spread footings or space frames with 300 mm hydraulic jacks and are re-levelled from survey data as the active layer moves. The ground is allowed to thaw instead of being kept frozen. The closest earlier attempts, fixed piles and thermosyphons, failed as winters warmed; this design carries no assumed ground-temperature margin. The deciding quantity is the differential settlement rate compared with jack stroke and re-levelling interval.
VALUE: It decouples infrastructure life from the rate of permafrost warming.

IDEA 4
NAME: Powered-first staged Baimskaya
MECHANISM: Start Baimskaya with a 5–8 Mtpa first phase, at an estimated 20–25 kWh per tonne of ore, or about 30 MW. It would run on the spare capacity of the existing Akademik Lomonosov plant and the Pevek line, and its concentrate would ship from Pevek in the NSR window. The full nuclear-powered project would follow later. The closest earlier attempt is KAZ Minerals' 300 kt/y plan, which slipped while it waited for new power and roads. This idea needs neither in its first phase. The deciding quantity is the spare MW at Chaun–Pevek after Bilibino closes.
VALUE: Brings first concentrate several years earlier and puts less capital at risk before any revenue.

IDEA 5
NAME: Parallel mid-scale domestic LNG trains
MECHANISM: Build Gydan/Yamal LNG as 3–4 parallel trains of 1.5–2.5 Mtpa each, on the Arctic Cascade process. Each train uses domestic electric or UEC-Kuznetsov drives, Arctic air cooling and gravity-base modules, and each comes online as soon as it is built. The closest earlier attempt is Train 4, which stalled at proving the process at 0.9 Mtpa; Arctic LNG 2 failed because its 6.6 Mtpa trains needed imported turbines. This idea stops short of 6.6 Mtpa, so neither the scale-up nor the import limit applies. Colder heat rejection lowers the compressor work per tonne, by the Carnot relation. The deciding quantity is the uptime of a train at 2 Mtpa against the 1,100 $/t/y benchmark of Yamal LNG Trains 1–3.
VALUE: Cuts delivered LNG cost by about 30% (est.) and brings each train into production about a year sooner.

IDEA 6
NAME: Crusher-conveyor underground ore haulage
MECHANISM: Crush ore underground at the level and carry it by fixed belt to the shaft, using Russian-made belts and Ruselprom drives, in place of long truck hauls. A belt uses an estimated 3–5 times less energy per tonne-km and needs far fewer crew and imported spares. The closest earlier attempt is the BelAZ underground truck, still at prototype stage (F184–186); this plan removes most of that fleet instead of replacing it. The deciding quantity is belt availability against a maximum lump size; pilot it on one Talnakh level within 3 years.
VALUE: Full cost at Norilsk down 3–6%, with less reliance on spares for the Western fleet.

IDEA 7
NAME: Plant-chilled foundations
MECHANISM: Run a glycol loop through the pile caps of an LNG plant, cooled by its own refrigeration and kept running in summer. Thermosyphons from Fundamentstroyarkos would still cover the winter. A summer heat flux of about 5 W/m² over 1 km² is about 5 MW of heat, or about 2.5 MW of electricity at a COP of 2. That is under 1% of the plant's drive power. The closest earlier attempts are TAPS and the Qinghai–Tibet thermosyphons, which fade as winters warm. An active loop does not depend on how cold the winter is. The deciding quantity is the summer heat flux, measured in boreholes.
VALUE: Holds pile capacity at the design γt for the life of the plant, whatever the warming.

IDEA 8
NAME: Cold-calendar maintenance shift
MECHANISM: Planned overhauls and major component changes at the Natalka, Udokan and Elga pits move into January–February at −40 to −50 °C. Fleet failure rates rise up to 6–7x in that period (F111). In the warmer months the whole fleet runs. Arctic-grade oils from Gazpromneft and Lukoil and Teplostar preheaters keep the trucks that do run in the cold working. One quantity decides it: the share of overhaul hours that can be moved compared with the hours lost to cold failures. If the 8% of fleet hours spent on planned overhaul land on cold days (est.), effective tonnes per year rise 3–6% (est.). Fixed cost is spread over more tonnes, so unit cost falls 2–4%. The closest earlier attempts, the Zyfra and BelAZ autonomous haulage pilots (F196), add technology but do not change when failures happen. Benchmarks: Kupol and Diavik. Main risks: workshop space and spares lead times (F63). Test: a 2-year A/B trial on two fleets at one site.
VALUE: The same open-pit fleets gain more days of operation per year, with almost no capital.

IDEA 9
NAME: Underground X-ray ore pre-concentration
MECHANISM: Domestic X-ray transmission sorters at the shaft bottom reject waste lumps from disseminated Ni-Cu ore, and the rejected rock becomes stope backfill. The concentrator's fixed capacity then processes more metal per hour. The closest earlier practice, Alrosa's diamond sorting, has never been applied to sulphide ore here. The deciding quantity is the waste-rejection fraction achieved while losing less than 3% of the metal.
VALUE: It raises metal output per unit of existing concentrator capital.

IDEA 10
NAME: Rock-socketed piles with slip sleeves
MECHANISM: Carry new and rebuilt plant loads on bored steel piles set into rock below the frozen ground, and coat the shaft inside the frozen layer with a bitumen slip layer, so thaw adds only small drag load instead of removing the pile's support. Use TMK pipe and Chinese piling rigs. The closest earlier attempts are pile design to SP 25.13330, which has lost 20–55% of capacity (F89), and the Ambarnaya piles set in ice-rich ground (F325). Ground cooling is not involved, so a warming climate does not erode the support. The design applies at Norilsk, Udokan and Baimskaya, but not on deep Yamal sediments. The deciding quantity is depth to competent rock, which must be 30 m or less; test it with load-cell piles monitored for 3–5 thaw seasons.
VALUE: Structure life of 50+ years, for about 1–3% more capex.

IDEA 11
NAME: Numbered-up mid-scale LNG trains
MECHANISM: Replicate the Arctic Cascade train built at 0.9 Mtpa about seven times instead of scaling up to 6.6 Mtpa. Each train is driven by 16–25 MW gas turbines already in serial production for Russian pipelines, and Arctic air does the precooling. The closest earlier attempt, Arctic LNG 2, stalled on scarce large turbines; this design avoids that by using drives already built in volume. The deciding quantity is installed capex per tonne-year together with train availability at −45 °C.
VALUE: It phases LNG capital and starts revenue sooner using only domestic drives.

IDEA 12
NAME: Grid-first phased Baimskaya
MECHANISM: Baimskaya starts at about 15 Mt/y, using its high-grade starter pit. It draws about 60–70 MW from the existing Chaun–Bilibino node and the Akademik Lomonosov plant. A concentrate stockpile holds about 9 months of output and ships in the 3–4 month Pevek window. The RITM-200S units and full 70 Mt/y scale come later, out of cash flow. The closest earlier attempt is the KAZ plan to build all power and infrastructure up front, which pushed capex to about $8bn and moved first production from 2021 to 2027 or later. The benchmark is Red Dog's single 122-day shipping season with a stockpile. The deciding quantity is the firm MW the node can supply. Risks are diseconomies at small scale and tailings in permafrost. The test is to run phase 1 within 4–6 years.
VALUE: Initial capex falls about 60% (est.) and first copper comes 2–3 years earlier, which raises the project's NPV on capital that would otherwise sit idle for years.

IDEA 13
NAME: XRT pre-sorting before milling
MECHANISM: X-ray transmission sorters from Rados (Russia) reject low-grade rock ahead of the Natalka and Olimpiada mills, so the mill and reagents treat only feed with gold in it. One quantity decides it: the share of ore mass rejected compared with the share of gold lost in that reject. If 25% of the mass is rejected and ≤5% of the gold goes with it (est., from tests on core), processing cost per ounce falls about 20%. Processing is 30% of TCC, so TCC falls about 6% (est.) and spare mill capacity can take higher-grade feed. The closest earlier attempt is Natalka's planned 2025 flotation retrofit (F66), which raises recovery but still grinds all the waste. Sorting attacks the ground tonnes directly and is domestic and modular. Benchmark: Polyus group recovery of 81.8% (F67). Not suited to Norilsk disseminated ore or Elga coal. Main risk: free gold too fine for X-ray density contrast. Test: core tests in year 1, a 200 t/h module by years 3–4.
VALUE: Gold operations get about 5–8% lower total cash cost per ounce delivered, from domestic equipment, within 4 years.

IDEA 14
NAME: Triaged thermosyphon pile retrofit
MECHANISM: Borehole temperatures plus InSAR rank plant foundations by remaining pile capacity. Retrofit GET/VET thermosyphons from Fundamentproekt then go only on the Norilsk and Talnakh structures headed toward a loss of more than 40% (F89). One quantity decides it: the air freezing index compared with the heat flowing into the pile. Norilsk's long, deep winter still gives a strong cooling margin. TAPS and Qinghai–Tibet lost cooling where winters were weak (F320–322), so the scheme is limited to the coldest sites and leaves out Salekhard and Nadym. The closest earlier attempt, post-spill monitoring (F18), detected thaw but fixed nothing. Benchmark: Ambarnaya and the 35% deformed-building share (F252). Main risk: non-condensable gas fade, so inspect every 3 years. Test: an instrumented pilot on 20 piles with 3 seasons of monitoring.
VALUE: Avoids unplanned plant outages and prolongs infrastructure life on thawing ground, at a small share of Norilsk's ~$3bn/yr capex.

IDEA 15
NAME: Murmansk winter shuttle loop
MECHANISM: In winter, Arc7 ships run only Sabetta/Utrenneye–Murmansk and back, unloading into existing floating storage there. Conventional tankers then carry the LNG onward. The closest earlier attempt is the Zeebrugge transshipment, whose roughly 5,000 km leg wastes scarce Arc7 tonnage and which the EU ban from 2027 closes. The deciding quantity is Arc7 round-trip days in the January–April ice. Risk: storage capacity at Murmansk. The test, over 3 years, is to log turnaround times for the existing ships.
VALUE: The cargo each Arc7 ship delivers per year rises about 1.5–2x (est.), which removes the shipping bottleneck that left Arctic LNG 2 Train 1 unsold. Outbound logistics, the second-largest cost share, falls roughly 25–35%.

LESSONS:
- No ground-cooling or pile fix has a measured lifetime against thaw. Cooling weakens as winters warm, and design norms lag the ground.
- The unit cost of a remote site is dominated by external infrastructure and power. Power and the transport window repeatedly slipped or went over budget, by 38% to 60% or more.
- Every domestic substitute for imported plant is at prototype or unit scale. Spares for the Western base remain the schedule risk.
- Seasonal windows and ice-class tonnage cap deliveries, and ice-class tonnage is the scarcest item (only 2 Arc7 carriers).
- Many failures were organisational (poor maintenance, immature design, ignored warnings), not physics.
- The evidence base is thin: many figures are press, vendor, secondary or conflicting, and few site-level permafrost data exist. This applies to the cost-per-unit comparisons too.

Sort these candidates before the expensive checks. Without tools, for each candidate estimate:
- I = importance 0-10: how much of the asker's aim it serves if it works - for a cost question, the share of the
  delivered cost it removes; for a defect question, the share of the defect it removes in every state of use listed;
  never more than the ceiling of its archetype allows;
- P = possibility 0-10: how likely it is to work at the scale it claims with known physics, given the earlier
  attempts that stopped short (a candidate that repeats one without beating its reason gets P <= 2);
- the one number that decides whether it works.
Being unusual earns nothing here; aiming at a term whose ceiling leaves no room earns a low I.
