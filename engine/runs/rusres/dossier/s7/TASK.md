# Task: one slice of a research dossier

QUESTION the dossier serves: Russia wants to develop and extract minerals and energy in its permafrost regions at the lowest full cost, in the shortest time from investment decision to first production, and with the highest effectiveness. Effectiveness means resource recovery, working time across the year, and how long infrastructure lasts on thawing ground. All three are measured per unit of product delivered to the buyer. The scope covers:
- Hard-rock and placer metals: Norilsk and Taimyr nickel, copper and platinum-group metals; gold in Yakutia, Magadan and Chukotka; Udokan and Baimskaya copper; Tomtor rare earths.
- Diamonds in Yakutia, plus the Arkhangelsk deposits (see CORRECTED).
- Coal in Taimyr, Elga and the rest of Yakutia.
- Oil and gas in Yamal, Gydan and Taimyr.
The whole chain counts: choosing and exploring deposits, building and powering sites, mining or drilling, processing, staffing, and delivering the product. The conditions are:
- Permafrost that is thawing under a warming climate.
- Extreme cold, with short building and shipping seasons.
- Long distances with no roads or railways.
- Ice seasons on the Northern Sea Route (NSR) and the rivers.
- A shortage of workers.
- Expensive energy at remote sites.
- Restricted access to imported mining, drilling and LNG equipment and software since 2022.
What changes to any stage, or to the order in which deposits are developed, would beat the best current practice in Russia and in comparable cold regions (northern Canada, Alaska, Greenland, northern Scandinavia)?
A good answer: 
- Each proposal states its effect, with an estimate and its basis, on three measures: (a) full cost per unit delivered (annualised capital plus operating plus logistics); (b) years from investment decision to first production; (c) effectiveness (recovery, days worked per year, infrastructure life on thawing ground).
- Each proposal is compared with a named benchmark, either Russian (e.g. Yamal LNG, Norilsk, Vankor/Vostok Oil, Kupol/Pavlovskoye, Nord/Elga) or foreign (e.g. Canadian Arctic mines such as Raglan and Diavik, Red Dog in Alaska, Kiruna/Aitik, Greenland).
- It names which deposits or stages it applies to and which it does not.
- It states what physical law or measured data it relies on.
- It lists the equipment or technology by its domestic maker or unrestricted supplier.
- It includes the main risks and how to test the idea within 3–7 years.
Constraints: 
- It must obey known physics.
- It must be deployable within 3–7 years.
- It may use only equipment and technology Russian operators can get from domestic makers or from suppliers not restricted for them.
- It must not depend on ways around sanctions.

YOUR SLICE: Earlier attempts, failures and accidents with causes and numbers | COVER: Russian and Soviet cases: the Norilsk TPP-3 diesel spill (2020, about 21,000 t), the Norilsk concentrator No. 1 roof collapse (2020), the Nadezhda tailings discharge (2020), building and pile deformation statistics in Norilsk, Yakutsk and Vorkuta; Mir mine flooding (2017); pipeline and road failures on Yamal and at Bovanenkovo; Vorkuta Severnaya mine explosion (2016) as a cold-region coal benchmark; abandoned or delayed projects (Tomtor schedule slips, Udokan's decades of delay, Baimskaya cost increases and Kaz Minerals exit, Arctic LNG 2 halted trains, Vostok Oil timeline slips, Pavlik and Natalka start-up recovery problems, Elga pre-2016 bankruptcy of the original owner, Taimyr coal and the Chaika/Sever Port projects). Foreign cases: Snap Lake closure, Jericho and Diavik dike issues, Faro and Giant mine legacy, Mary River expansion disputes, Nanisivik and Polaris closures, the Kvanefjeld and Isua permitting halts in Greenland, Red Dog water-treatment issues, Kiruna subsidence and town relocation, the Arctic LNG cost overruns at Hammerfest. For each: direct cause, root cause (thaw vs design error vs operation vs maintenance vs economics), cost and time impacts, and lessons | ANGLES: Rostekhnadzor annual accident reports and investigation summaries, plus MChS and Rosprirodnadzor records for the Norilsk spill and environmental damages; Nornickel internal-audit and independent reports on the 2020 accidents (the Norilsk Nickel Environmental Commission, Lambert and others), plus academic cause analysis distinguishing thaw from design and maintenance faults (Melnikov Institute, MGSU, Hjort et al., Streletskiy); post-mortems of delayed and cancelled projects from company reports, the Accounts Chamber of Russia, and Russian business press (Коммерсант, Ведомости, РБК, Интерфакс) with dates and cost overruns; Canadian, Alaskan and Greenland failure and closure reports (NWT and Nunavut Water Board filings, De Beers Snap Lake closure reports, Canadian government legacy-site cleanup budgets, Greenland mineral-authority decisions); Norwegian and Swedish failure and subsidence reports (Hammerfest LNG overruns from Equinor and Norwegian parliament, LKAB Kiruna subsidence data) | LANGUAGES: Russian (primary for Russian cases); English (Canada, Alaska, cross-case studies); Swedish and Norwegian (LKAB, Equinor and parliamentary records); Danish and Greenlandic (Greenland permitting decisions); French (Quebec Nunavik/Raglan records)

The other slices, which other researchers cover (do not duplicate them):
- Resource base, deposit quality and holders

- Binding natural and physical limits
- Logistics corridors and energy supply at remote sites
- Equipment, technology, spare parts and labour supply chains
- Regulation, finance, ownership, sanctions and markets

Collect the facts of your slice with sources. Do not answer the question and do not propose solutions. Tools:
WebSearch only (WebFetch does not reach hosts here; use search excerpts). About 25 searches. Prefer primary and
quantitative sources: official statistics, company annual reports and presentations, government programmes and
registers, peer-reviewed papers, standards, patents, and engineering reports; use press only where nothing better
exists, and say so. Search in the languages your slice names.

Rules:
- one fact per line, with numbers, units, year and place where they exist;
- every fact has its own source URL;
- HARD = an official figure, a measurement, or a figure documented in several independent sources; SOFT = a single
  source, an estimate, a company claim, or a disputed figure;
- prefer facts that constrain answers: costs per unit, rates, limits, failure numbers, times, capacities.
