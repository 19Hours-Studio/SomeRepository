=== EVIDENCE ===
FACT 1: Norilsk CALM site (Grebenets, Tolmanov, Streletskiy, 2005–2020): mean thaw depth in loam was 96 cm, against about 80 cm in the 1980s. The deepening trend is about 1 cm/yr, and thaw depth varies by up to 200% between microlandscapes. | HARD | https://ges.rgo.ru/jour/article/view/2179
FACT 2: Western Yamal (Marre-Sale, Earth Cryosphere Institute): mean annual ground temperature rose 1.5–2.2 °C over 43 years (1980–2021), with the most on flat and polygonal tundra and the least in flooded lake basins. The depth of zero annual amplitude is 13–16 m. | HARD | https://ges.rgo.ru/jour/article/view/3076
FACT 3: Marre-Sale is described as the fastest-warming permafrost in the western Russian Arctic since the 1970s, at about 0.06 °C/yr at the zero-amplitude depth. | HARD | https://ges.rgo.ru/jour/article/download/3076/731
FACT 4: In the 43-year Marre-Sale record, active-layer thickness rose only slowly despite warming. Moss thickening and ground subsidence as ice-rich permafrost thaws offset the deepening. Drained terrain responded most, wet tundra showed decreasing thickness, and floodplains showed no change. Thaw depth is therefore a poor proxy for ice-rich ground degradation. | HARD | https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10326636
FACT 5: Bearing capacity of pile foundations (Streletskiy, Shiklomanov et al.) has fallen since the 1970s. Nadym lost more than 40% and Salekhard about 20%. Projected loss in Norilsk is above 55% around the 2040s. A "significant (at least 25%)" Russia-wide reduction is expected by mid-century. The study's worst case is 75–95% by 2050. The modelled figures reach me only through press summaries of the study. | SOFT | https://www.thebarentsobserver.com/arctic/permafrost-thaw-will-have-devastating-impact-on-buildings/105619
FACT 6: Nadym and Salekhard: the 2012 GIS-based study (Arctic, Antarctic, and Alpine Research 44(3)) is the source of the +40%/20% decline figures. Foundation capacity is computed from modelled ground temperature, active-layer depth and soil properties. | HARD | https://bioone.org/journals/arctic-antarctic-and-alpine-research/volume-44/issue-3/1938-4246-44.3.368/Permafrost-Infrastructure-and-Climate-Change--A-GIS-Based-Landscape/10.1657/1938-4246-44.3.368.full
FACT 7: Bovanenkovo and Kharasavey (Lomonosov 2025 conference paper, 20+ years of geotechnical monitoring): rising mean annual permafrost temperature has already reduced the computed bearing capacity of the pile foundations of built facilities since construction. The excerpt omits the magnitude. | SOFT | https://lomonosov.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2025/data/36929/181707_uid455796_report.pdf
FACT 8: SP 25.13330.2020 amendment No. 3 (FAU FTsS) went to public consultation until 12 Oct 2025, covering advanced foundation technologies on permafrost. The pile temperature coefficient γt cannot be changed by a designer without a norm change or an Art. 15 of FZ-384 justification procedure. Projected warming is thus not written into the design assumptions. | SOFT | https://www.normacs.info/discussions/9666
FACT 9: A TsNIISK bulletin paper on thermometric boreholes recommends changes to SP 25.13330.2020 and GOST 25358-2020 to account for observed ground warming. | SOFT | https://vestnik.cstroy.ru/jour/article/download/584/457
FACT 10: Norilsk: reporting cites an official (Krasnoyarsk emergencies ministry) that nearly 60% of buildings are deformed, and local engineers that over 100 residential buildings (about 10% of housing) were vacated. This is secondhand. | SOFT | https://blogs.gwu.edu/arcticpire/tag/norilsk
FACT 11: Ambarnaya tank collapse, 29 May 2020, about 20,000 t of diesel spilled. Norilsk Nickel blamed permafrost thaw. Rostekhnadzor found pile foundations too weak and poor maintenance. | HARD | https://www.rferl.org/a/russian-fuel-disaster-caused-by-permafrost-melting/30655856.html
FACT 12: Panarctic hazard (Hjort et al. 2018): 70% of current infrastructure in the permafrost domain (range 48–87%) lies in zones with high potential for near-surface thaw by 2050. 45% of Russian Arctic hydrocarbon fields lie in zones where thaw instability could cause serious damage. | HARD | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6289964
FACT 13: Yamal tabular ground ice: layers of 10 m or more, at depths from near-surface to tens of metres. Exposures at the Neytinskiye lakes show about 20 m of ice at 5–26 m depth. Thickness and distribution diminish northward from Bovanenkovo to Kharasavey. Thermocirque and landslide features are tied to its degradation. | HARD | https://elib.utmn.ru/jspui/bitstream/ru-tsu/35201/1/TSUHerald_2012_7_68_76.pdf
FACT 14: Yamal thermocirques: Sentinel-2 mapping across the Yamal and Gydan peninsulas gives a median thermocirque area of 2.5 ha. | SOFT | https://doaj.org/article/0460a28a7a6d44bca5c746666f560901
FACT 15: Yamal cryopegs: a geophysical survey in northern Yamal finds permafrost laterally continuous but with taliks and lenses of saline liquid (cryopegs). | SOFT | https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-746.html
FACT 16: Saline frozen loam near a Yuribey cryopeg (central Yamal): at –3 °C cohesion is about 0.3 kg/cm² (about 30 kPa) and internal friction angle about 11°. Salinity, temperature and lithology control strength, and thawing loses the bearing capacity. | SOFT | https://aspirant.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2020_2/data/19845/107823_uid116130_report.pdf
FACT 17: InSAR near Bovanenkovo (Sentinel-1): seasonal subsidence up to 180 mm/yr in subsidence zones, and 60–120 mm/yr at three sites in 2017–18. This is seasonal active-layer movement, not net settlement. Stacked interferograms carry about 1 cm error. | HARD | https://piahs.copernicus.org/articles/382/183/2020/
FACT 18: Central Yamal subsidence: in situ and Sentinel-1 agree at 3.9 and 4.3 mm per 100 thaw degree-days. | HARD | https://doaj.org/article/c079968ddbe84c4d9dc8ab168ef5ab5d
FACT 19: Peschanka (Baimskaya): continuous permafrost 150–280 m thick, with thaw zones under rivers and streams. This is a secondary summary and gives no ground temperature. | SOFT | https://miningdataonline.com/property/498/Elmwood-Mine.aspx
FACT 20: Central Yakutia: mean annual air temperature rose from –9.6 °C (1980) to –6.7 °C (2019), about 0.07 °C/yr, and is linked to thermokarst and alas expansion. Yakutsk air temperature rose about 3 °C in 1966–2023. | HARD | https://www.mdpi.com/2073-445X/9/11/463
FACT 21: Yakutia, from the same press item: the 10 warmest years at Yakutsk are all in the 21st century, and winter temperatures below –50 °C occurred only 4 times in 25 years. | SOFT | https://news.mail.ru/society/63311771/
FACT 22: Permafrost in polar and mountain regions warms on average about 0.29 °C per decade (cited in an intro). Northeast Russia air temperature rose about 2.2 °C in 50 years. Magadan upland (Verkhnekolymsky) boreholes at zero-amplitude depth: –0.1 to –3.8 °C, seasonal thaw 0.9–2.6 m. | SOFT | https://sibran.ru/upload/iblock/f9d/f9d8c8718c655566d45afdaf2a1a161e.pdf
FACT 23: Four-borehole study (2023): permafrost warming of 0.02–0.16 K/yr against air warming of 0.09–0.11 K/yr, with a weak, inconsistent relationship. Warming rates cannot be transferred between boreholes. | HARD | https://doaj.org/article/ccaa4fdcfe464042826dc3d0f2bc81d2
FACT 24: Roshydromet: Russian mean temperature trend +0.50 °C/decade (2023 report, secondary source), against 0.47 °C/decade for 1976–2019 and 0.18 °C/decade globally (more than 2.5 times). | SOFT | https://naked-science.ru/article/climate/novyi-otchet-rosgidrometa
FACT 25: Arctic warming 1979–2021 was 3.8 times the global rate (range 3.7–4.1). The Barents Sea near Novaya Zemlya warmed up to 7 times. | HARD | https://www.carbonbrief.org/the-arctic-has-warmed-nearly-four-times-faster-than-the-global-average
FACT 26: Norilsk climate: about 280 days of stable frost per year and more than 130 blizzard days. Winds reach 50 m/s in "black purga". Mean January temperature is –26.5 °C. Polar night runs from about November to February. | SOFT | https://t-j.ru/norilsk/
FACT 27: Cold-related failure rates (IFTPS SB RAS-linked work): bulldozer and tractor part failures up to 6 times more frequent in winter, excavator parts up to 7 times. Rail breakages from cracks are 7–15 times more frequent in Jan–Feb than in Jul–Sep. The highest risk is at restart after stops. Dump trucks are not covered. | SOFT | https://kmu.itmo.ru/file/download/application/12734
FACT 28: Sea ice, September minimum: NSIDC gives 4.60 million km² on 10 Sep 2025 (10th lowest). The 1979–2024 trend is –12.4% per decade, about –77,000 km²/yr. There is no significant trend in September minima for 2007–2025. | HARD | https://cires.colorado.edu/news/arctic-sea-ice-has-reached-minimum-extent-2025
FACT 29: AARI-based studies show a statistically significant fall in July–September ice extent in the Kara, Laptev, East Siberian and Chukchi seas, with an abrupt drop after 2005. The NE Kara and E. Siberian seas lost more than 40% of ice area in 1980–2011. | HARD | https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D71C7C10CEC9ECF98C5C3C8F6F2B94EA/S0260305500260333a.pdf/sea-ice-coverage-variability-on-the-northern-sea-routes-1980-2011.pdf
FACT 30: NSR transit season length varies strongly year to year. The 15 seasons of 2008–2022 are too few for a trend, and the east and west sectors have different drivers. May Kara Sea extent predicts ice-free navigation 2–3 months ahead. | HARD | https://yadda.icm.edu.pl/baztech/element/bwmeta1.element.baztech-5032dee7-73fb-4bcc-97aa-52290f082b53/c/1_Marsz_Pastusiak_Styszynska_TransNav_18_3_2024.pdf
FACT 31: NSR 2025 transit season: first entry 30 June, last exit 17 Nov, about 4.5 months. 103 transits (CHNL count, a Western-based monitoring centre). The 2024 and 2025 seasons ended early because of ice formation. | SOFT | https://en.highnorthnews.com/business/northern-sea-route-2025-season-concludes-with-stable-transit-traffic-amid-challenging-ice-conditions/1096859
FACT 32: CMIP6 (16 models): September ice-free around 2076 under SSP2-4.5 and around 2055 under SSP5-8.5. Under SSP1-2.6 September extent stays above 2 million km². Open-water ships get an Aug–Oct window in 2045–55. PC6 ships' window shifts to Oct–Dec in 2021–30. No 2035 NSR-specific figure found. | HARD | https://doaj.org/article/31b3b9dfc3f747c7889abc0167600e87
FACT 33: Laptev fast ice reaches about 2 m in mid-winter (2.5 m in the coldest winters). Kara fast ice peaks April–May and fills the Ob and Yenisei estuaries until about July. The Vilkitsky Strait is a hazardous point. | HARD | https://polarresearch.net/index.php/polar/article/download/2093/5344
FACT 34: Arc7 ice capability (Yamal LNG): Christophe de Margerie is rated for 2.1 m of independent ice by one source, while another gives 1.4 m (2.0 m with escort). It sails west from Sabetta year-round and east for about 6 months (Jul–Dec). Vessel ratings differ by source. | SOFT | https://www.bairdmaritime.com/shipping/ice-breaking-lng-carrier-ready-to-serve-yamal-lng-project-2
FACT 35: River ice 2001–2024 (Ob, Yenisei, Lena, 6 rivers): freeze-up delayed by 9.0 days on average, breakup 7.8 days earlier, ice duration 14.1 days shorter. Over 65% of segments show this. | HARD | https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/2703/2026/
FACT 36: Estuary ice dates vary by year. Lena freeze-up came 4–7 days later than the norm in one season, the Kolyma 4–9 days later in 2015, and the Yenisei 9–14 days later in 2011. These are single-year bulletins, not trends. | SOFT | https://old.aari.ru/resources/f0021/2015_spring.pdf
FACT 37: Yakutia winter roads: the head of the republic warned in Feb 2025 that a warm winter may force early closure of automobile winter roads. Roads normally open in January, and the most remote villages are reached in early March. | SOFT | https://rg.ru/2025/02/06/reg-dfo/doroga-skoro-rastaet.html
FACT 38: Tibbitt–Contwoyto winter road (Canada, the best-documented benchmark): 1981–2010 mean season 65 days (range 47–78). Projected 58 days in the 2020s and 49 in the 2050s. The shortest season on record was 49 days (1991). A 2021 lake-ice model puts the tipping point at about 2 °C of warming. | HARD | https://yukonu.arcabc.ca/_flysystem/repo-bin/2022-10/yukonu_198.pdf
FACT 39: Tibbitt–Contwoyto: the operator disputed the "direness" of the 2021 study. Projected travel time Yellowknife to Bathurst Inlet rises from 3.8 to 6.5 days by 2050. | SOFT | https://cabinradio.ca/60164/news/environment/climate-change-threatens-viability-of-winter-road-to-mines/
FACT 40: Chara basin (Udokan): seismicity under current norms is rated 9 points or more. The site sits between the Kodar and Udokan ridges. A basin 125 km long and up to 35 km wide has about 5,000 m of neotectonic amplitude. | HARD | https://www.gt-crust.ru/jour/article/download/2210/971
FACT 41: Heap leach in cold climates: a survey of 28 cold-region projects found coldest-month means of –18.1 °C (extremes –26 to –31 °C) and cold seasons of 4–10 months. Ice layers block percolation and can cut leaching to about 8 months/yr. Heated solution and buried drip lines are the countermeasures. | SOFT | https://911metallurgist.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Cold-climate-heap-leaching.pdf
FACT 42: Fort Knox (Alaska) runs a double-lined, year-round heap leach. The operator withheld winter design details as proprietary. Kupol-specific winter data was not found. | SOFT | https://miningnewsnorth.com/story/2009/11/22/news/fort-knox-heap-leach-yields-first-gold/2118.html
FACT 43: Tailings and dams in permafrost: Colomac dam (NWT) seepage rose from 6 gpm (1996) to about 300 gpm (1999) over a talik in the valley bottom. The Mt Nansen thermosyphon plan assumes a 50-year life and fails if warming is unabated. A Nunavut frozen-core dam had a 7-year design life. | HARD | https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/cpc/CPC6-617.pdf
FACT 44: Canadian MEND reports (Nanisivik, Raglan, Lupin, Rankin Inlet) give cover designs governed by active-layer depth. Meliadine places filtered tailings in thin yearly lifts to promote permafrost aggradation in the tailings and foundation. | SOFT | https://canadianminingjournal.com/news/tailings-solutions-permafrost-studies-available-from-mend
FACT 45: Diamond pits in Yakutia (Udachny): freeze–thaw cycles thicken the sloughing layer on pit walls. Deep brines, mainly in fault zones, complicate underground mining. Re-injected brines dissolve ice in fractures and create an artificial aquifer in degrading permafrost. | SOFT | https://rudmet.ru/journal/1993/article/33527/
