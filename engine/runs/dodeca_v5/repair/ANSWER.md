=== IDEAS ===
IDEA 1
NAME: Staff-mounted provincial symbolic bronze head
MECHANISM: The dodecahedron was a prestige object with symbolic meaning, made in regional bronze workshops of Gaul, the Rhineland and Britain. It was a lost-wax shell about 1 mm thick cast around a clay core. Every face is pierced because the core had to be supported during casting and broken out afterwards. The holes were then finished to a six-axis plan. Opposite holes were sized alike (very clearly on Tongeren, weakly on the second published table). Ten holes were ringed. One larger, unringed and sometimes oval opposite pair formed a through-axis, by which the head could be fitted onto a rod or handle; the bone object about 15 × 3 cm lying beside the Gellep piece (grave of a wealthy woman, c. AD 350) suggests this. When it was not mounted, the corner knobs let it stand on any face as a finished ornament. Its meaning was cosmological or astral: the 4th-century Geneva lead dodecahedron, which carries the twelve zodiac names, shows that in this region a twelve-face solid could stand for the twelve signs. Well-off civilians of both sexes owned it, and some soldiers did too. They used it rarely or in ceremonies, which is why wear is slight. They valued it enough to repair it (Kortessem) and to put it in rich graves and hoards. It belonged to a north-western provincial tradition, carried by people moving to the Danube forts and across the river. That explains why it is missing from the Mediterranean and why no Greek or Latin author describes it.
VALUE: It is the only reading that accounts at once for the hole in every face, for holes that vary but match axis by axis, for the corpus-wide special unringed opposite pair, for the thin unworn shells, and for the repaired, grave and hoard contexts. The lot, display and rangefinder readings each fail on at least one of these. Still unexplained: what exactly the symbol meant, why the tradition stayed regional, why it ends in the 4th century, and where it was made (no workshop is known). Decisive test: under a stereo-microscope, compare the rims and inner walls of the unringed pair with the ringed holes on about 10–20 type-A pieces. If the unringed pair shows no more abrasion, filing-for-fit or organic residue than the ringed holes, and is not coaxial and wide enough to take a shaft, the mounting reading is refuted.

IDEA 2
NAME: Bronzesmith's hollow-casting display piece
MECHANISM: The dodecahedron was a showpiece from regional north-western workshops that demonstrated difficult thin-wall hollow casting. No guild "masterpiece" institution is needed for this, and there is no Roman evidence for one. The core was held and later removed through the face openings, set along the six axes, so opposite openings came out matched in size. Finishing (triple rings, one plain and sometimes oval pair) varied from workshop to workshop. The knobs were cast-on ornament and feet, not vents. Nobody used it for anything, so it shows no wear. People kept it as a valued item and placed it in graves and hoards. Its distribution follows the workshop tradition.
VALUE: It explains the pierced faces, the axis matching, the thin walls and the lack of wear without assuming any use. It cannot explain why one pair is consistently left unringed, made larger and sometimes oval across the corpus, or the rod beside the Gellep piece. The same rim test refutes it if the unringed pair does show fitting marks or abrasion that the ringed holes lack.
