=== IDEAS ===

IDEA 1
NAME: Staff-mounted provincial symbolic bronze head
MECHANISM: The dodecahedron was a prestige object with symbolic meaning, made in regional bronze workshops of Gaul, the Rhineland and Britain. It was a lost-wax shell about 1 mm thick cast around a clay core. Every face is pierced because the core had to be supported during casting and broken out afterwards. The holes were then finished to a six-axis plan. Opposite holes were sized alike, very clearly on Tongeren and weakly on the second published table. Ten holes were ringed. One larger, unringed and sometimes oval opposite pair formed a through-axis, by which the head could be fitted onto a rod or handle. The bone object (about 15 × 3 cm) found right beside the Gellep piece, in the grave of a wealthy woman from about AD 350, suggests this. When it was not mounted, the corner knobs let it stand on any face as a finished ornament. Its meaning was probably cosmological or astral: the 4th-century Geneva lead dodecahedron carries the twelve zodiac names, so in this region a twelve-face solid could stand for the twelve signs. Well-off civilians of both sexes owned it, and some soldiers did too. They used it rarely or in ceremonies, which is why wear is slight. They valued it enough to repair it (Kortessem) and to put it in rich graves and hoards. It belonged to a north-western provincial tradition, carried by people moving to the Danube forts and across the river. That explains why it is missing from the Mediterranean and why no Greek or Latin author describes it.
VALUE: It is the only reading that accounts at once for:
- a hole in every face;
- holes that vary in size but match axis by axis;
- the special unringed opposite pair found across the corpus;
- thin, unworn shells;
- the repaired, grave and hoard contexts.

The lot, display and rangefinder readings each fail on at least one of these. Still unexplained: what exactly the symbol meant, why the tradition stayed regional, why it ends in the 4th century, and where the objects were made, since no workshop is known. Decisive test: examine the unringed pair against the ringed holes under a microscope on 10–20 type-A pieces. If the unringed pair shows no more abrasion, filing for fit or organic residue than the ringed holes, and is not coaxial and wide enough to take a shaft, the mounting reading is refuted.

IDEA 2
NAME: Bronzesmith's hollow-casting display piece
MECHANISM: The dodecahedron was a showpiece from regional north-western workshops that demonstrated difficult thin-wall hollow casting. No guild "masterpiece" institution is needed for this, and there is no Roman evidence for one. The core was held and later removed through the face openings, set along the six axes, so opposite openings came out matched in size. Finishing (triple rings, one plain and sometimes oval pair) varied from workshop to workshop. The knobs were cast-on ornament and feet, not vents. Nobody used it for anything, so it shows no wear. People kept it as a valued item and placed it in graves and hoards, and its distribution follows the workshop tradition.
VALUE: It explains the pierced faces, the axis matching, the thin walls and the lack of wear without assuming any use. It cannot explain why one pair is consistently left unringed, made larger and sometimes oval across the corpus, or the rod beside the Gellep piece. The same rim test refutes it if the unringed pair does show fitting marks or wear that the ringed holes lack.

**Anchors checked**

Kept:
- **Fishguard:** 27.7–40.6 mm holes, about 128 mm across, 553 g (British Museum record).
- **Type A typology:** ten round holes, usually with three engraved circles, plus one opposite pair without circles, "the most common type by far" (Wikipedia, citing Guggenberger). FACT 3 calls it "type 2a"; the source says "type a". The content is confirmed.
- **Guggenberger & Leach:** *Antiquaries Journal* 105:31–54, online 17 Nov 2025. It rests on texts, including a passage in Iamblichus' *On the Pythagorean Life*. Their thread-divination follow-up is an unpublished academia.edu draft, so I did not rely on it.
- **Gellep:** grave of a wealthy woman who died about AD 350; a bone object about 15 × 3 cm lay right beside the piece; the authors propose it was fitted on a handle through the "production holes" (Cambridge core reader). That it was "too decayed to recover" is not re-verified.
- **Geneva die:** silvered lead, 4th century, zodiac names, found in 1982 in the Saint-Pierre cathedral excavations. Confirmed only by secondary sources (a CERN collector page and a search excerpt); I did not see ge.ch. It is a different type: lead, inscribed, and probably not hollow.
- **Arloff icosahedron:** in Bonn, long filed as a dodecahedron (Wikipedia). The 1953 find date comes only from a book blurb.
- **Deonica:** published 2021 (*Zbornik Narodnog muzeja Jagodina* XXV/1), but in the museum since 2006.
- **Hurbanovo and Chotín:** three fragments (two plus one) from Germanic sites about 6 km north of the Danube, opposite Brigetio (Dragonidesová 2025).
- **Kortessem:** shows fractures and repairs. Reading the break as ritual is the excavators' interpretation.
- **Druid bans:** Tiberius (Pliny, *Natural History* 30.13) and Claudius (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.5) both confirmed.
- **Five-knob resting:** follows from the geometry. With equal knobs at the 20 corners, every support plane is parallel to a face and touches 5 knobs.

Corrected:
- **Norton Disney alloy:** 75% copper, 7% tin, 18% lead (McDonnell's handheld XRF), but another source gives 26% lead. The lead figure is unresolved.
- **Druid argument against Idea 3:** the reviewer's chronology objection is overstated. Ausonius (*Professores* 4 and 10) records 4th-century Bordeaux professors who claimed Druid descent and served Belenus. The *Historia Augusta* druidesses (Diocletian story, *Numerian* 14) are probably fictional (Wawrzyniak 2021). Druidic ideas could survive; nothing links them to these objects, so they carry no weight here.
- **Sparavigna table (FACT 37):** "holds whichever oval axis is used" overstates it. My own recheck (`pairs.py`) gives p = 0.010 using the ovals' long axis and 0.039 using the short axis. With the known type-A oval pair removed, the ten round holes give **p = 0.073, not significant**. Combining both objects, p is about 4×10⁻⁴ to 7×10⁻⁵, not a flat 7×10⁻⁵.
- **Tongeren (FACT 36):** I reproduced p = 0.0005. It still holds without the largest pair (p = 0.001) and without the two largest pairs (p = 0.01). But the data come from one hobby page (wingarden.de, citing a 1998 book), where the numbers appear as ranges, and German Wikipedia speaks of "eight" holes. Reading the ranges as opposite pairs is likely but unconfirmed.
- **Count of readable sizes:** my greedy count gives 7–9 classes for Tongeren and 4–6 for Sparavigna at a 5–10% limit. The method changes the number by about one class; neither object reaches 12.
- **Kortessem source:** "Belgium has two dodecahedra" conflicts with the Bassenge piece in the Grand Curtius museum.

Dropped:
- Idea 4's three-knob stance (geometrically impossible).
- Idea 2's guild masterpiece (a medieval institution) and its 20 corner vents (no vent stubs reported).
- Idea 1's 12 readable faces.
- The Gallic Empire as a cause: Jublains (about AD 200) is earlier than 260–274, and 4th-century contexts are later.
- The knitting reading: no fibre has been found, and knitting is post-Roman according to secondary sources.

**Corpus versus single objects**

Established across the corpus:
- the shared template and a hole in every face;
- hole sizes that vary within each object, with no shared scheme;
- the type A ringed-ten plus one unringed opposite pair;
- size and weight spread continuously, and thin walls (derived from mass);
- no inscriptions and rare wear (reported, but there has been no systematic wear survey);
- mostly civil contexts, plus graves, hoards and rivers;
- the north-western and Danube distribution.

Single objects or two-object results:
- the Gellep rod;
- the Geneva lead die;
- the Kortessem repair;
- the Norton Disney alloy;
- the wax residue;
- the irregularly cut holes on the East Riding piece;
- the Carnuntum 0.3 mm measurement error;
- the axis matching, on only two tables, one of which is weak.

The best explanation leans on corpus-level type A, with Gellep and the pairing as supporting evidence only. It does not stand or fall on any one of them.

**The map and the dates**
- **Regional workshop or practice:** supported. About 70% of finds are from France and Germany, the template is uniform, and the outliers sit at Danube forts and legionary sites (Carnuntum, Brigetio) or are imports across the river. Untested, because no workshop is known and there is no alloy grouping yet. The Hitchens handheld-XRF project in Bonn could test it.
- **A casting technique that did not travel:** rejected. Bronze casting was practised across the whole empire.
- **Gallic Empire:** rejected as a cause. It is only a proxy for the region.
- **Druid cult:** not supported. Survivals are attested, but nothing ties them to the objects.
- **Cold-climate demand (gloves):** rejected.
- **Dates:** fixed by only a few contexts (Jublains about AD 200, Gellep about AD 350, the Saint-Parize hoard in the 4th century). The start and end of the tradition are unexplained.

**Decisive test.** Before the lab work, a free first step is to rerun the pairing test on Guggenberger's catalogue nos. 1–134. If corpus-wide pairing turns out random, the six-axis plan falls. The main test is to examine, under a stereo-microscope, the rims and inner walls of the unringed pair against the ringed holes on 10–20 type-A pieces in Bonn, Tongeren and the British Museum.
