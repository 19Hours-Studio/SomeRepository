=== REVIEW ===
IDEA 1: Self-heated barge boilerhouse
DEFECT | MAJOR | The design never says how the barge gets refilled with coal. In the north the heating season runs about 9–10 months and covers the whole 3–4 month navigation window, so the boiler hull can't sail off to the coal port without leaving the village cold. That leaves a ship-to-ship transfer from a delivery barge, which is the same double handling the idea claims to remove. | The claimed 0.8–3.4k rub/t saving is mostly avoided unloading. Season lengths are my estimate; I did not source them.
DEFECT | MAJOR | A backwater at least 2.5 m deep is too shallow once ice and the winter drop in water level are counted. Average end-of-winter ice on the Lena at Yakutsk is 1.2–1.4 m and the record is 2.2 m, and still backwaters freeze thicker than the main channel. With 1.4 m of ice, about 1.1 m of water is left under a loaded coal barge drawing about 2 m, before any level drop. | [Lena ice regime 1938–2014](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2017/data/10560/uid110987_report.pdf)
DEFECT | MINOR | The 36 kW for an uncovered moat is 1.4–4.7x too low. An open 0 °C water surface under −40 °C air loses about 290–800 W/m² at 2–8 m/s wind (sensible heat plus longwave, in python). For a 70×14 m hull the moat is about 168 m², which gives 49–134 kW. That is still at most 28% of the 480 kW flue loss, so the heat budget holds. | python: q = ρ·cp·Ch·U·ΔT + 120 W/m²
DEFECT | MINOR | Hazards not covered: coal stored in a closed hold next to the boiler can self-heat and give off CO or methane. A boilerhouse moored all year needs both a River Register class and a Rostekhnadzor hazardous-facility permit. | not costed
SURVIVES | The 1.04–1.3x arithmetic reproduces (0.8/23 → 1.036; 3.4/15 → 1.29). The flue heat is more than enough for a covered moat.
RATIO | 1.05 | Once refilling costs a barge-to-barge transfer, what is left is roughly the cost of the shore coal yard and its handling.
KNOWN | partly | Russia already uses floating heat sources, e.g. the floating nuclear plant Akademik Lomonosov supplies heat to Pevek. Floating boilerhouses are an existing class of vessel.

IDEA 2: Single-use ice barges on spring flood
DEFECT | FATAL | No seagoing ship can meet the hulls at the delta in June. Tiksi is open to navigation only from about mid-July to mid-October. A convoy that leaves just after the late-May or early-June ice run reaches the delta in mid-June and has to wait 4–6 weeks in warming water. At only 2 °C, with the same heat-transfer coefficient that gives their 7 cm/day, the walls lose 0.28 m/day, about 10 m over 35 days, against 1.5 m of wall. | [Tiksi navigation mid-Jul–mid-Oct](https://www.kupibilet.ru/gid/rossiia/tiksi); python: h ≈ 500 W/m²K
DEFECT | FATAL | The melt figure holds only at 0.5 °C, and the river does not stay that cold. Their 7 cm/day implies h ≈ 500 W/m²K. At 5 °C the same h melts 0.7 m/day, so a 1.5 m wall lasts about 2 days, against a 7–17 day trip. Water behind the ice front warms past 0.5 °C within days. I could not source June Lena water temperatures, but the hull survives only if the water stays at or below about 1 °C for the whole trip. Midnight sun adds about 3 cm/day of melt from the top. The 24-day soak-through figure doesn't matter, because the failure is surface melt. | python melt table
DEFECT | MAJOR | The plan contradicts itself. A hull frozen onto river ice can't launch after the ice run, because the ice it is built on is what runs. It either rides out the run among 1.2–1.4 m floes moving at up to 2.5 m/s and through the jams, or it has to be built on the bank and launched down a slipway, which the plan doesn't include. | [Lena ice speeds up to 2.5 m/s](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/17/4671/2013/hess-17-4671-2013.html)
DEFECT | MAJOR | The pykrete creep advantage is cited for temperatures below −16 °C, but the hull spends the whole voyage at 0 °C. Pykrete also needs about 14% wood pulp, which is about 410 t per hull, not loose chips. Using pykrete (density about 0.98) also cuts payload from 2,510 t to 2,320 t. | python
DEFECT | MAJOR | The baseline doesn't match the route. $77/t is a 2010 average for all Lena freight, mostly upstream and with no transshipment. The idea's costs leave out storage and transshipment at the delta, the tug for 7–17 days, loading 2,500 t onto ice, and the expected loss of hull and cargo, which for metal concentrate is also an environmental incident. The $29/t figure is unsourced. | —
DEFECT | MINOR | Two speed figures disagree. 1–2.5 m/s is 86–216 km/day, not 160–290 km/day, which would give 5–9 days instead of 7–17. A single 1,000 t test hull can't show the loss rate: with zero losses, n trips give a 95% upper bound of 3/n, so about 60 hull-voyages are needed to show the rate is below 5%. | python; rule of three
SURVIVES | The geometry reproduces: 2,988 m³ of ice and 2,510 t payload with pure ice. A 0.2 m/day build rate is plausible, and the hull takes about 23 days to build.
RATIO | 0.8 | Not viable as designed. Expected cargo and hull losses plus delta storage outweigh the hull savings.
KNOWN | partly | Single-use timber craft (karbas, pauzok) were historically floated down Siberian rivers and broken up at the destination; this is from memory, I did not source it. Pykrete hulls go back to Project Habakkuk in WWII.

IDEA 3: Fuel on empty coal-barge legs
DEFECT | MAJOR | The legs run out of deck area long before draft becomes the limit. Pillow tanks hold about 70% of their bounding box, so a 50 m³ (42 t) bladder about 1.2 m high covers about 60 m². Twenty-four of them need about 1,430 m², while the hold of a 1,000 t river barge is about 500 m², and pillow tanks can't be stacked. That gives about 8 bladders, about 340 t per leg, so 51 legs are needed, not 17. | [pillow-tank shape 70%](https://canflexinc.com/?p=7078); python
DEFECT | MAJOR | The bladder fleet's capital cost is left out. One hundred bladders at $8–15k each with a 2–3 season life in coal-gritted holds adds about 1,400–4,000 rub/t. That is enough to wipe out the 2,100 vs 2,700 rub/t edge at the low end of the range. | python; prices are assumed
DEFECT | MAJOR | Regulation: arctic diesel is a flammable liquid (flash point roughly 30 °C or above). Carrying it in soft containers inside an open dry-cargo hold needs River Register and dangerous-goods approval. That is not shown. After any leak the hold also has to be cleaned before it can take coal again. | GOST 305 flash point from memory, not sourced
SURVIVES | The arithmetic holds: 17 legs × 24 × 42 t = 17.1 kt, about 405 fills, so about 4 trips per bladder, and 1.29–5.7x reproduces.
RATIO | 1.2 | 2,100 rub/t plus 1,400–4,000 rub/t of bladders, plus three times as many legs, against 2,700–12,000 rub/t.
KNOWN | partly | Fuel already moves in tank-containers and drums on dry barges in Yakutia's Northern Delivery. Flexitanks are a commodity product.

IDEA 4: Pre-staged flood-crest barges
DEFECT | MAJOR | A backwater of at least 2.5 m doesn't fit a loaded barge. 1.2–1.4 m of ice (up to 2.2 m), plus a loaded draft of about 1.5–2 m, plus the winter drop in level, needs about 4–5 m. At 2.5 m the loaded hull grounds or freezes in, and the ice pressure and breakup put hull and cargo at risk. | [Lena ice regime](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2017/data/10560/uid110987_report.pdf)
DEFECT | MAJOR | Two costs are missing: tying up both the barge and the cargo for about 8 months, and the risk of the main-stem ice run passing the tributary mouth where the barge waits. Both cut into the 1.5–3.6x. | not costed in the text
DEFECT | MINOR | The 35–62% load loss comes from an unsourced model. A linear 12-day recession would give 42–83% lost time for 5–10 days late, so the figure depends on a depth-to-draft curve that isn't shown. | python
SURVIVES | 4,700/3,100 = 1.52 and 11,300/3,100 = 3.65 reproduce. Loading in autumn afloat rather than through the ice is sound.
RATIO | 1.2 | Assumes a deeper and more distant wintering basin, plus capital tie-up and ice-run risk.
KNOWN | partly | Wintering loaded vessels to deliver on the high water is, as far as I know, done in the Lena basin, but I could not source it.

IDEA 5: Winter-cold salt removal for lithium brines
DEFECT | MAJOR | The wrong salt is named. In calcium-chloride brines sodium and potassium are minor. Cooling crystallises calcium chloride hexahydrate (the CaCl2–H2O eutectic is about −50 °C at about 30–31 wt%), plus magnesium chloride hydrates. The patent cited (US9932241B2) is about sodium/potassium salt-lake water. Lithium is lost in the liquid trapped in the crystal cake, and that loss is never measured. | [CaCl2–H2O phase diagram](https://www.phasediagram.dk/phase-diagrams-for-binary-salt-solutions/); [US9932241B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9932241)
DEFECT | MAJOR | "Free cold" is a large plant. 20 kt/yr of lithium carbonate equivalent at 400 mg/L and 80% recovery is about 11.75 Mm³/yr of brine. Cooling it by 60 K in 150 winter days means rejecting about 200 MW through about 0.6 km² of ponds or spray. It also leaves about 5 Mt/yr of wet salt for disposal (assuming 30% crystallises). | python
DEFECT | MAJOR | The saving may not exist. The direct-lithium-extraction sorbent planned for Kovykta is selective against sodium and potassium, so taking them out first gains little. Brine at −40 °C also has to be warmed back up for extraction kinetics and viscosity. | Kovykta lithium is planned for 2029 ([investfuture](https://investfuture.ru/articles/gazprom-nachnet-dobychu-litiya-iz-promyshlennykh-vod-kovyktinskogo-mestorozhdeniya-v-2029-godu-FD8CCABDC9FE591E43258D6F00226FB2))
DEFECT | MINOR | The Kovykta-specific figures (600 mg/L lithium, 170–631 g/L salinity) are not sourced, and I could not verify them. A single lab chill needs a full lithium mass balance including the solids, run in replicate. | —
SURVIVES | 2.26 GJ/t latent heat is correct. Calcium-chloride brine at 35% or more won't freeze to ice at −40 °C.
RATIO | 1.0 | Uncosted. The plausible benefit is offset by reheating and salt disposal.
KNOWN | partly | Cold precipitation is patented (US9932241B2). Winter freezing is used on Chinese salt-lake brines ([CN113896212B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN113896212B/en)).

IDEA 6: Kite-towed sled trains, no roads
DEFECT | MAJOR | The pull figure is the peak, and the source is contested. SkySails' own figure for 320 m² is 16 t "under ideal wind"; 32 t is the inventor's press quote. Taymyr and Anabar winters average around −30 °C, which is the regime where they admit friction rises. With 16 t and μ = 0.2–0.3 the train is 53–80 t gross, not 320–640 t, and less at typical rather than ideal wind. | [ing.dk 16 t](https://ing.dk/artikel/kaempedrage-paa-320-kvadratmeter-skal-traekke-fragtskib); [The Local 32 t](https://www.thelocal.de/20110415/34411); the μ range is assumed, I did not source it
DEFECT | MAJOR | The scale-up from the Windsled is about 50–150x. The Windsled carries 2–3 t under a kite of up to 150 m², which is 13–20 kg/m². The claim is 1,000–2,000 kg/m². The kite's vertical lift and side force also have to be resisted by runners on snow, which have no keel. Recovering a 320 m² kite in polar night and blizzards is untested. | [WindSled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WindSled), [explorersweb](https://explorersweb.com/now-this-is-kiting/)
DEFECT | MAJOR | The example sites have little outbound freight: Anabar produces diamonds, and the Taymyr coal mines sit on the coast. A competent operator's baseline is a tractor sled train on groomed snow, which also needs no built road and where fuel is a minor share of cost. Kites remove only that share, and tractor backup, crews and sleds all stay. | —
DEFECT | MINOR | A pilot of 3–5 trains in one season can't pin down cost per t-km when it is driven by wind-hours that vary from year to year. | —
SURVIVES | 1,541 km in 17 days is 91 km/day, consistent with the Windsled record.
RATIO | 1.1 | Only the fuel share of a tractor-train cost is removed.
KNOWN | partly | The WindSled carries 2 t science payloads across Greenland and Antarctica. Tractor sled traverses already supply polar stations.
